# Chapter 5 - Repentance from dead works

<!-- p1 -->
Often in Christianity we view repentance as something fundamental only to the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The essence of repentance goes much deeper than simply turning from sin to salvation. Repentance from dead works means living with a heart turned away from what harms us or hinders our growth - and turned toward what is from God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of the Epistle to the Hebrews puts ‘repentance from dead works’ first in the list of foundational teachings after the foundational principles of the Messiah (Chapter 4).

### Dead works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever had a job knows what work is. Work — is a set of tasks that you perform to get a paycheck and make a living (employment). Employment — is an agreement to exchange your talents and efforts for something of value that helps you support yourself. You work because you get something from it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can bring a sense of purpose, often a sense of identity. You often hear the question ‘What do you do for a living?’ and the answer begins with an identity statement ‘I am’. Although work can be enjoyable and meaningful, it is also very often physically or mentally hard, difficult, demanding, and stressful. If your job is none of these, you might consider it too easy and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work 40 hours a week, and sleep 8 hours a day, you spend 36% of your waking time at work, and that does not include the time you spend getting ready for work, getting to it, keeping house, and doing all the other necessary tasks to keep your life on the right track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? The reasons vary, but by definition, because we get something valuable from it.

<!-- p13 -->
Would you keep working at a job if they stopped paying you? For most of us, the easy and painfully obvious answer — a loud, NO!

<!-- p14 -->
Would you keep working at a job if it were killing you? I suppose that depends on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
The context of ***nekros*** in combination with ***erga*** — is labor or work that is dead, has no life force, cannot reproduce! The author of the Epistle to the Hebrews warns us about labor that is dead.

<!-- p16 -->
In biology there are several criteria that are used to determine whether an organism is alive (this is a partial list for our purposes):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: living beings convert energy through metabolic processes in order to grow, develop, and sustain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: life includes growth and changes over time, with a defined life cycle and developmental stages.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: living beings are capable of reproduction, creating offspring or new entities.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (for example, light, heat, or sound) and can adapt to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, for example, regulating temperature or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living beings can adapt to their environment through genetic changes, which allows them to survive in various conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this standard of «life» to our deeds with a little creativity, then dead works will be those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, cannot yield a positive or desired result.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God’s presence, or to the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Do not maintain internal stability, become dysregulated, lack self-control.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (too inflexible) and no longer help us survive.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not what God wants for us. God wants to bring us life!

</span>

<!-- p33 -->
Although «dead» and «alive» are a binary dichotomy. Dying is a gradient. Our dead works may not yet be completely dead in all categories, but they will become so! Dead works are like poisons: some have a stronger effect.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by interfering with its normal functioning. Some poisons have a small effect, for example, causing a headache. Others affect how we think, see, or even whether we can have children (live fruitfully). Dead works are exactly the same. They may be just a «headache», or they may lead to death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are any deeds which, upon examination and reflection, do not bring the abundant life that Jesus promised. Marital infidelity is a very potent poison; it will most likely have a strong and negative impact rather quickly. Unwarranted stubbornness and resentment will slowly poison a person until they drive everyone away.

<!-- p36 -->
To be freed from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
«To repent of dead works» means to turn to God and leave behind everything that harms our walk with Him — beginning with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Extending the poison analogy for dead works, if you found out that your favorite snack was filled with toxic heavy metals that, when consumed, would lead to a fatal condition, what would you do? We would, without hesitation, put it away from us and never enjoy it again, realizing that consuming it means our premature and likely painful death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must turn away from the things of this life that poison us and lead us to both physical and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to look a little deeper into the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The term used in the Epistle to the Hebrews is *repentance*. This is contrasted with the term *to repent*, an example is found in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
To repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or deed; it means to change one’s mind or intention; to turn away from sin and turn to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is the state of the one who has repented; it means a change of mind; the state of one who has turned away from sin and turned to God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in the Epistle to the Hebrews ‘repentance from dead works’ He is speaking of the state of the one who has repented! We must live in a continual state of having ‘turned away’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times do we need to repent? Trick question! We need to continually change our thinking until we no longer commit dead works. Repentance is not a request for forgiveness nor an apology, although that may be part of the process. Repentance is the turning away from the former way of thinking.

### Systems of change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in changing our minds. If we do not recognize the need to change our minds, then why would we even open our eyes to look for change? Again, returning to the poison analogy, if you do not realize that the poison is killing you, why would you stop consuming it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that a person simply has to reach ‘rock bottom’, then he will look at his life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that we humans are more complex. There are many theories related to behavior change in the study of psychology, one of the most authoritative is the — **Transtheoretical Model of Change** Developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People can go through a cycle of six stages of change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: No intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: An intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps toward change have been taken.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: Behavior has been changed for a short time.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: Behavior has been changed and continues to be maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: No desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no set duration for how long any stage may take. Some people, with respect to certain issues in their lives, never significantly progress through the stages. In other cases, we can move very quickly from stage 1 to 6.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father, experiencing a sudden realization after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *strict* but *loving* parent. He believes that raising his voice is necessary. He is not considering making changes. In fact, he may even justify it- “That’s how my father raised me.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, he snaps and yells at his little daughter sharply, louder and meaner than ever before. She flinches. Her lip quivers. She quietly walks away. In that moment… something **changes**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. The image of her hurt face plays over and over in his mind. His stomach twists. He says to himself: *“This is not the person I want to be.”* He knows he needs to do something differently-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He walks into her room, drops to his knees, sincerely apologizes and says: *“Daddy was wrong. You didn’t deserve that.”* She hugs him. Motivated to solidify the change, he prays and talks with a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he begins reading parenting books to work on developing positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of his behavioral pattern. He is not perfect, but something really has changed. He no longer justifies the old behavior-and he has no desire to go back.

### Helping Others Change

<!-- p79 -->

We will also likely encounter those who desperately want to change but have no idea what to do (**preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
Jesus, preaching to the crowds, often encountered people who did not know they needed to change. We cannot forcibly change someone’s mind or repent for them. That is not our role, and it is not the example of Jesus.

Remember that bringing conviction and illumination is the work of the Spirit.

### Godly sorrow and God's goodness

<!-- p92 -->
Although reaching "rock bottom" is more a myth than a prerequisite for change, God can use sorrow to influence our thinking. God will use sorrow to bring us to repentance. He will convict us regarding our actions and the state of our lives and hearts to open our eyes and show us our complete and absolute need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but worldly sorrow produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>an experience of grief or suffering that leads to *metanoia*</u>, is not the same as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of grief or suffering, but the world offers no hope, only relief and a promise of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and grief.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the enduring of pain—physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual—especially when it is prolonged or intense.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is a multifaceted emotional response to a significant personal loss, especially in relationships or on an existential level.

<!-- p100 -->
Sadness, suffering, and grief — are closely related concepts. In our human life we will experience all three; through each of them we can learn and grow, but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives in order to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
Scripture says that every good gift and every perfect gift comes from above, from God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is — a good Father, Who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow will bring discomfort, but His discipline is just and full of love. Just as a good father will not deliberately break his child’s arm to ‘teach him a lesson,’ God will not send sickness or infirmity upon His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### An example of worldly sorrow and godly sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick and deliberately hit her, which made her cry, and a red mark was left on her skin.

<!-- p104 -->
Hearing the commotion, their father found out what was going on and, learning what his son had done, took the stick, broke it in half, and threw it away. He sent the son into the house to think about what he had done, and comforted the daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came in to talk with the son, he found him in tears. In answer to the question of why he was crying, the son replied: "because you broke my stick".

<!-- p106 -->
The father answered: "I am sad that you care more about your stick than about your sister whom you hurt" and sent him back to the room for further reflection.

<!-- p107 -->
After a few minutes the son came out and apologized. He said that he was still sad because Dad had broken his stick, and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example the boy experienced both worldly sorrow — because of the lost stick, — and, in the end, godly sorrow — because of the pain caused to his sister.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow is connected with God’s goodness. God is — a good Father, who will allow us to experience the consequences of our actions, but will still stand by when we go through these consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
God’s unfailing goodness — His willingness to redeem and restore us when we come to Him — is a positive factor on our path of repentance. His goodness is not that we should continue to sin, but that, in spite of our sin, He opens the way to our redemption and lets us experience His salvation.

<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

Despite God’s goodness, mercy, and grace, some people do not repent and do not change; they get stuck in a cycle of disappointment and pain. Conversely, for some, even a single example of sin and its consequences in another person’s life is enough for them to change their thinking.

<!-- p114 -->
A human being has a tremendous capacity for change; it is built into our very nature. People are highly adaptable: we can learn and grow, and find better ways of doing things. However, the ability to change — is not the same as the willingness or conviction to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some get stuck while others are so ready to change, but I can say that the entire process of change — is truly by God’s grace.

The process of repentance begins even before we receive Jesus as our personal Lord and Savior, but the lifestyle of repentance should continue throughout our Christian journey. We must renounce dead works and turn our minds from them to God’s truth.

<!-- p120 -->
Repentance can be a purely natural process, but repentance is also the work of the Spirit, who calls out to us, opens our eyes to the truth, changes our hearts, calling us to transformation. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:1</span> reminds us that some will find themselves ‘stuck’ in a transgression (sin) and need help to get out! That help comes in the form of mature men and women who help restore us in a spirit of gentleness.

<!-- p121 -->
The Holy Spirit is the greatest agent of change with whom humanity can interact, and this is affirmed by both Scripture and research. The study “Lyons et al. (2010) found that up to 82% of clients who experienced a spiritual awakening during addiction treatment and recovery were completely abstinent at 1-year follow-up compared to 55% of clients without a spiritual awakening.” \[2\] Although a person can initiate and mediate change, we can accomplish far more when we walk with the Spirit of God.

In our subsequent studies on the Holy Spirit and on sanctification, we will see more deeply how the Spirit calls and transforms the hearts of people. Let us look at one such example—the story of King David and his example of repentance.

### David’s Example of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no account in Scripture provides a more vivid example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin as recorded in 2 Samuel 11-12. It begins with King David remaining in Jerusalem instead of going to war and seeing Bathsheba bathing. Despite knowing that she is the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David sends for her, and they have an affair, resulting in Bathsheba becoming pregnant.

<!-- p125 -->
To conceal his actions, David summons Uriah from the battlefield, hoping he will spend time with Bathsheba and appear to be the child’s father. However, Uriah, out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, refuses to go home to Bathsheba. David then arranges for Uriah to be sent to the front lines, where he is ultimately killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David hastily marries Bathsheba to hide the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was displeased with David’s actions and sends the prophet Nathan to rebuke him. When the prophet rebuked him, David immediately admitted: “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify or excuse his actions. This admission was the first step of his repentance, acknowledging his wrongdoing directly before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51 David’s words, “*For I acknowledge my transgressions, and my sin is always before me,*” show his full ownership of his actions. He expresses grief not only over the sin itself but over having offended God, saying: “*Against You, You only, have I sinned.*” David displayed a “*broken and contrite heart,*” which is a necessary part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he deeply mourned the sin committed and the pain caused. Such depth of remorse was a sign that he desired not only forgiveness but a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God: “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He sought an inner transformation that would keep him from repeating the same mistakes. Rather than simply asking for forgiveness, David pleaded with God to cleanse him, saying: “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He understood that only God could purify his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire for spiritual renewal reflects a sincere resolve to continue walking in God’s ways. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) shows his hope to be aligned with God in every area of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God forgave David his sin, the Lord struck the child, and he became gravely ill. David fasted, prayed, and begged God to spare the child, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find both a personal prayer of repentance and a public acknowledgment of his offenses. David’s decision to record his plea for mercy, creating one of the most famous psalms in the Bible, shows his willingness to be open about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony to repentance and restoration from which others could learn, memorializing his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s path of repentance was not merely a feeling of regret; it was a profound change in his heart and life. Through confession, acceptance, and heart transformation as described in Psalm 51, David memorialized his sin as a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most significant biblical examples of genuine repentance.
