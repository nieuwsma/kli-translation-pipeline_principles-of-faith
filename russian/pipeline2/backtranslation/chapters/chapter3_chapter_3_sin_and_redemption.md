# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
Scripture tells us many stories about sin—its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin — means to fall short of God's standard, or to 'miss the mark,' an archery term describing an archer's failure to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old and the New Testaments use various terms for sin, each highlighting aspects such as its type, degree of severity, intention, victim, nature, and required punishment. There are several broad categorizations that Scripture uses to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against man, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Intentional sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- One-time sin, sin committed habitually

<!-- p6 -->
- Sin requiring severe punishment, such as death, sin requiring financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In our modern criminal justice systems we have similar distinctions: degrees of crimes, premeditated or crimes of passion, crimes requiring financial restitution, or imprisonment, or even the death penalty. An act can be both a crime and a sin, but crime is a human categorization, sin is God's.

<!-- p8 -->
Various Greek and Hebrew words for sin overlap in many ways. All sin is broadly described under the umbrella term 'miss the mark,' ***Hamartia*** in Greek and ***Chata’*** in Hebrew. This umbrella term is used for the gravest sins — adultery, murder — but also for less serious sins, such as showing favoritism to wealthy people in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

Even in the midst of judgment, God’s heart for redemption shines. Considering these Old Testament sacrifices, we begin to see how God was preparing His people to understand the cost of sin and the hope of restoration.

## Sin requires a sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God kills animals and makes garments for them from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first shedding of blood to cover the consequences of sin. There are a few more similar episodes in the book of Genesis, but in the book of Leviticus God officially establishes the order of sacrifices for sin.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general offering for propitiation and dedication to God, often symbolizing complete surrender. This offering addressed sin in a broad sense, covering both intentional and unintentional sins. The rite involved bringing a male animal-usually a bull, a sheep, a goat, or a bird-without any blemish. The one bringing the sacrifice laid hands on the head of the animal, identifying with the offering, after which the entire animal was burned on the altar. This act of burning the whole offering signified complete dedication to the removal of sin and the conforming of one’s life to God. The burnt offering emphasized the seriousness of sin and the need for total devotion, expressing reverence and repentance through the giving of something valuable.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, known as ***Minchah***, was an offering of thanksgiving and dedication rather than a direct sin offering. This offering accompanied other sacrifices, such as the burnt offering and the peace offering, and was intended to express gratitude, worship, and dedication to God. Made from fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, the grain offering did not involve blood. Part of the flour was burned on the altar as a gift to God, and the rest was given to the priests for food. Although it was not intended specifically for sin, the grain offering was an important part of communal worship, symbolizing the offerer’s dedication and gratitude for God’s provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, was focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This offering often followed sin and propitiatory sacrifices, celebrating restored relationship with God after sin had been dealt with. The offerer could bring a male or female without blemish, such as a cow, a sheep, or a goat. In the rite, the fatty portions-the best parts of the animal-were burned on the altar as God’s portion, and the remaining meat was shared by the priests and the offerer in a communal meal. The peace offering was unique because it symbolized fellowship and unity, reflecting a sense of wholeness and oneness with God. After atonement had been made for sin, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was specifically intended for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When an individual or the community realized that they had sinned unintentionally, they could bring a sin offering to seek cleansing and forgiveness. The type of animal brought depended on the status of the offender: a priest or the community would bring a bull, a leader would bring a male goat, and an individual would bring a goat or a lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the offering before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and the remaining part of the animal was taken outside the camp if it was a communal sin. This offering showed the seriousness even of unintentional sins and provided a means for the people to remain cleansed and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins involving a breach of trust, causing harm to another, or the desecration of holy things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins that needed to be repaid, such as property violations or offending sacred items. To perform this offering the sinner had to bring a ram without blemish. If the sin involved harming another person or property, the guilty party also had to make restitution by returning the property plus an additional 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering emphasized the importance of acknowledging one’s guilt and taking tangible steps to put wrongs right. It underscored the interpersonal and communal nature of sin, showing that true atonement requires not only seeking God’s forgiveness but also making things right with those who were harmed by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Collective sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific sacrifices for individual sin, there were also sacrifices and offerings for the collective sin of the people, performed daily, weekly, monthly, but the most important of these was the **Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite, described in Leviticus 16, intended to cleanse the entire community of Israelites from their collective sins of the previous year. This unique ceremony was held once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, and addressed the sins of the people rather than individual offenses. It served to purify the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people, symbolizing both propitiation and cleansing for the whole community.

<!-- p79 -->
Central to the Day of Atonement were **two goats**, used to represent both sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat, designated “for the Lord,” was offered as a sin offering, and its blood the high priest brought into the Holy of Holies. There the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat-the lid of the Ark of the Covenant-symbolizing atonement for the people’s sins and reconciling them to God. This act of sprinkling had profound significance, as it signified the cleansing of the sacred space and ensured the indwelling of God’s presence among His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, known as **the scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest would lay his hands on the scapegoat, confessing the collective sins of Israel, thereby placing them upon the animal. Then the goat was sent into the wilderness to die alone, which symbolized the complete removal of sin from the camp. This vivid act served as a powerful illustration of both atonement and deliverance from sin, showing that God not only forgave but also removed the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God’s forgiveness and cleansing, allowing the people to put off the consequences of sin and to postpone judgment each year. This **atonement did not ultimately forgive** the people’s sins, but postponed God’s judgment.

## We have all sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible focus on the issue of sin in different ways. When Moses recorded the law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he set down specific punishments for specific sins/crimes. I use the word “crime” here interchangeably, because the books of the law were the legal system of the time for the Israelites.

<!-- p89 -->
In the Prophets and in the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in terms of the fate of the whole nation. When the king and the people did not obey God, the Israelites were subjected to oppression by their neighbors, suffered defeats in battles, and, in some cases, were led into captivity.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs the author writes as a loving father to his son, warning him about the personal dangers of sin and a lifestyle contrary to God’s commandments. In the Psalms many of David’s works are devoted to the personal and emotional consequences of committed sin.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels Jesus warns about sin, calls people to personal repentance, and brings the final sacrifice for sin. In the Epistle to the Romans the apostle Paul compellingly shows how God intends to deal with sin and how we can be free from its power.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is this: we are all guilty of sin, and we all must pay the price for our crime.

It may be easy for us to try to justify our actions and say that we have not committed particularly serious sins such as murder or adultery, and that we are mostly moral and good people. This false attempt to justify ourselves ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and against whom we have sinned, and ignores the reality of a holy and perfect God. Scripture says: if we deny that we sin, we are liars, and the truth is not in us!

From the first sacrifice in Genesis to the cross of Christ, God’s plan has always been to redeem us and restore our relationship with Him. There is no sin too great and no distance too far for the grace of God to reach us.
