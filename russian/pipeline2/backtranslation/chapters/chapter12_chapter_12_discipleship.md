# Chapter 12 - Discipleship

<!-- p1 -->
Discipleship means following Jesus, learning from Him, and becoming more like Him in how we live and love. It entails a lifelong journey of obedience, transformation, and service, led by the Holy Spirit and rooted in God’s Word.

<!-- p2 -->
Discipleship — is the core command in the Great Commission. Jesus articulated this mission in:

<!-- p3 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p4 -->
> **<u>Matthew 28:18-20</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And Jesus came and spoke to them, saying, "All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth.
>
> Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,
>
> teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you; and behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age." Amen.
>

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

<!-- p6 -->
We, His followers, are to *«go and make disciples of all nations».* Discipleship — is about personal growth and about multiplying that growth by training others to follow Christ. Discipleship is the means by which we build God’s Kingdom; the teaching of Jesus is the message.

<!-- p7 -->
The call to be a disciple — is more than attending church or memorizing Bible verses-it is an invitation to live like Jesus and to partner with Him in advancing God’s Kingdom. Genuine discipleship helps believers become spiritually mature and effective for ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:13</span>). It shapes both the heart and the skills needed to serve others and proclaim the Gospel. As Paul instructed Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, discipleship follows a pattern of spiritual multiplication, where trained disciples go on to raise up new followers to lead others to Christ.

<!-- p8 -->
Discipleship — is both a personal calling and a global mission. On a personal level, it involves growth in faith, cultivating an active spiritual life, abiding in relationships with others &amp; God. At the same time, it fuels the Church’s mission—to reach every nation with the Gospel, ensuring that Christ’s message continues to spread and transform lives from generation to generation.

<!-- p9 -->
## Why Discipleship Matters

<!-- p10 -->
The impact of discipleship goes beyond personal transformation-it has a Kingdom effect. Paul reminded Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> to entrust what he had learned to faithful people who would be able to teach others also. This generational pattern underscores that discipleship is the engine that drives the growth and resilience of the Church. Discipleship matters because it was Jesus’ chosen method for spreading the Gospel and building His Church.

<!-- p11 -->
Rather than focusing on large crowds, Jesus intentionally invested in a small group of followers, teaching them, modeling faithfulness, and preparing them to carry on His mission. This personal, relational approach ensured the multiplication of His message, as each disciple was equipped to make new disciples.

<!-- p12 -->
At its core, discipleship is central to fulfilling the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). Jesus called us not merely to make converts, but to make disciple-followers, rooted in God’s Word, empowered by the Holy Spirit, and equipped for ministry. Through discipleship, believers grow in knowledge of Scripture, develop spiritual disciplines, and learn to walk in obedience to Christ. It prepares the Church to impact the world, advance the Gospel, and reflect God’s glory to all nations.

<!-- p13 -->
### Nature versus Nurture

<!-- p14 -->
In biology, psychology, and anthropology, we often consider the influences of nature and nurture. The question is often asked: Is a person — more the result of his innate, embedded genetic heritage? Or is he more the result of his environment and upbringing? The truth is that every person — is a combination of nature and nurture. Both elements, in close interaction, orchestrate the development of the human soul.

<!-- p15 -->
When you accepted Jesus, the miracle of salvation caused you to be born again of imperishable seed. You received the Divine nature! Your spiritual DNA was rewritten! This Divine nature moves our hearts to desire to be close to our Heavenly Father, to know Him, to experience Him.

<!-- p16 -->
Like a newborn whose astonishing, beautiful, innocent nature must be formed by loving parents in order to grow into a healthy and mature person, we too must be formed and nurtured for development.

<!-- p17 -->
Discipleship — is a process of spiritual development that builds upon our Divine nature and makes us spiritually mature. By investing in discipleship, we ensure the passing on of the faith, the raising up of leaders, and that the Church remains healthy and alive-both locally and globally.

<!-- p18 -->
### Didactic Model versus Mentoring Model

<!-- p19 -->
In modern Western education, the primary connection of the pupil — is with the material. Student learning is focused on content, materials, books, videos, assignments, etc. Educational transformation happens through the curriculum. This method of teaching is known as the *didactic model*.

<!-- p20 -->
The paradigm of the didactic model has not always been the norm. In the time of Jesus, the discipleship model was used. This model of learning emphasizes that knowledge is more "caught than taught," and places greater emphasis on the relationship between the student and the teacher than between the student and the curriculum.

<!-- p21 -->
The didactic model gradually became the default method over roughly a millennium: from the first medieval universities of Europe in the 11<sup>th</sup> - 13<sup>th</sup> centuries, the invention of the printing press in the 15<sup>th</sup> century, the Enlightenment of the 17<sup>th</sup> &amp; 18<sup>th</sup> centuries, and the impact of the Industrial Revolution on information and education in the 19<sup>th</sup> &amp; 20<sup>th</sup> centuries.

<!-- p22 -->
The advantage of the didactic model is that it scales more efficiently than the discipleship model. Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) can teach thousands of students at once, whereas even a very skilled master can simultaneously lead at most a few dozen disciples.

<!-- p23 -->
The outcome of the didactic model is that the student masters the content; however, he does not become like the teacher. The teacher is a facilitator of the curriculum. The teacher transmits knowledge.

<!-- p24 -->
In the discipleship model the master shares not only knowledge; he imparts wisdom. The outcome of the discipleship model is that the disciple becomes like the master to such an extent that he can reproduce his work and take on disciples of his own.

<!-- p25 -->
The didactic model has its use and place in the Kingdom of God—a good example is this book or any other Christian study material. Jesus, as a rabbi, skilled and trained in the rabbinic method, practiced the discipleship model as the primary one for His disciples.

<!-- p26 -->
Jesus describes the discipleship model in:

<!-- p27 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="40" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p28 -->
> **<u>Luke 6:40</u>** (NKJV)
>
> A disciple is not above his teacher; but everyone who is perfectly trained will be like his teacher.
>

<!-- p29 -->
</span>

<!-- p30 -->
In the didactic model the student becomes a carrier of knowledge; in the discipleship model the student becomes like the teacher. Let’s study the key words from this passage in Luke.

<!-- p31 -->
[See Table 6](../tables/table6.md)

<!-- p32 -->
To summarize, the aim and purpose of the discipleship model used in discipleship is twofold:

<!-- p33 -->
First, the disciple becomes fully trained, like the teacher. This does not mean the disciple imitates everything his teacher does. We are not required to create clones, but to raise to maturity men and women who can carry the message of Jesus to the ends of the earth.

<!-- p34 -->
Second, the disciple now himself becomes a teacher for others. The disciple must pass on what he has learned to someone else, otherwise the message of the Kingdom will stop with him. This does not mean he will necessarily have the same gifts as his teacher, or that he will inevitably enter full-time ministry, but it does mean he must reproduce for his own training to be considered complete.

<!-- p35 -->
Any organism that does not reproduce is sterile, and its line is cut off. An animal that cannot reproduce is unhealthy; a church that is unable to reproduce is likewise unhealthy.

## The Model and Process of Jesus' Discipleship

<!-- p37 -->
Jesus showed a mature and perfect example of discipleship in the Gospels with His chosen disciples. We should make broad use of these steps as the foundation for our own path of becoming and making disciples.

<!-- p38 -->
The discipleship model has two roles: mentor and disciple. Every mentor was once a disciple, and, hopefully, every disciple will in time become a mentor. I will use the terms *disciple* to refer to the student and *mentor* to refer to the master/teacher.

<!-- p39 -->
Any discipleship relationship goes through a sequence of eight phases. These phases, or steps, are not completely isolated from one another: often the relationship will flow between them. In each phase there is its own emphasis for both the disciple and the mentor. We will study the qualities of mentors in detail later in this chapter.

<!-- p40 -->
Eight stages of discipleship:

<!-- p41 -->
1.  Invitation/Call

<!-- p42 -->
2.  Teaching

<!-- p43 -->
3.  Demonstration

<!-- p44 -->
4.  Participation/Practice

<!-- p45 -->
5.  Correction

<!-- p46 -->
6.  Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p47 -->
7.  Multiplication

<!-- p48 -->
8.  Staying connected

<!-- p49 -->
### Invitation/Call

<!-- p50 -->
Discipleship begins with a personal invitation from Jesus to follow Him. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span> Jesus called His first disciples, saying, *“Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.”* This invitation was both personal and transformative—it required leaving behind the former way of life and entering into a new purpose centered on God’s mission. Jesus’ call was not to contemplation but to participation in His ministry and the work of the Kingdom.

<!-- p51 -->
Modern disciple-makers should invite others into authentic relationships that inspire spiritual growth and obedience. The invitation to discipleship is deeply personal. Jesus meets people where they are, addressing their unique needs and circumstances, but He also calls them to go deeper—to grow, serve, and become more like Him. This model reflects the relational nature of discipleship, where the focus is not only on knowledge but on a life-changing relationship with Christ.

<!-- p52 -->
### Teaching

<!-- p53 -->
Teaching lies at the very heart of discipleship. Jesus devoted much of His ministry to teaching the principles of the Kingdom of God, especially in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7). His teachings provided the foundation for righteous living, love for neighbor, and walking in obedience to God. Instruction in the Word of God remains central to discipleship today, as Scripture is the primary tool for spiritual growth, correction, and training in righteousness (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16–17</span>).

<!-- p54 -->
Disciple-makers should commit themselves to faithful teaching of the Bible, helping others understand it and apply it to their lives. Teaching involves more than the transfer of knowledge—it requires modeling a Christlike life so that disciples can see faith in action. Jesus embodied the truths He taught, showing that effective instruction is both verbal and visual. Likewise, disciple-makers should strive to live what they teach, providing practical examples of obedience and faithfulness.

<!-- p55 -->
### Demonstration

<!-- p56 -->
Jesus did not merely teach His disciples—He demonstrated how to live out the truths He proclaimed. Jesus showed what it means to love, serve, and carry out ministry in practice. Some examples from the Gospels include: Jesus washing the disciples’ feet as an act of humility and service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="13" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 13:12–15</span>), healing the sick, and feeding the hungry. Through His actions He demonstrated compassion, faith, and boldness, leaving a clear example for His followers to imitate.

<!-- p57 -->
Discipleship requires showing, not just telling. Modern disciple-makers should be open about their journey of faith, including struggles and victories, so that others can see how faith works in real life. Whether praying with someone in need, sharing testimonies, or serving the community, discipleship flourishes when it is rooted in practical demonstration. Actions often speak louder than words, making demonstration a powerful tool for teaching others the faith.

<!-- p58 -->
### Participation/Practice

<!-- p59 -->
Jesus did not keep His disciples on the sidelines—He actively involved them in ministry. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 9:1–6</span> and <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 10:5–15</span> Jesus sent the disciples to preach the Gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons. These hands-on opportunities allowed the disciples to learn through action, gaining confidence and skills under His guidance.

<!-- p60 -->
Contemporary discipleship should also include practical involvement. Disciple-makers should invite disciples to participate in ministry, whether leading prayer groups, sharing testimonies, or serving at evangelistic events. Providing safe spaces for practice allows disciples to grow in boldness with support and encouragement. Learning happens best through application, and participation is key to turning theory into action.

<!-- p61 -->
### Correction

<!-- p62 -->
Growth often requires correction, and Jesus lovingly corrected His disciples when they misunderstood His teachings or failed in their assignments. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-37" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Mark 9:33-37</span> He addressed their pride, teaching them servant leadership. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="22" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="31-32" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 22:31-32</span> He prayed for Peter, preparing him for restoration after failure and to strengthen others.

<!-- p63 -->
Correction in discipleship should be grace-filled and constructive-based on Scripture and aimed at restoration (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 4:2</span>). It is not about condemnation, but about helping disciples grow and avoid pitfalls. Disciple-makers should provide accountability, using God’s Word to guide and encourage growth, while also modeling humility in receiving correction themselves.

<!-- p64 -->
### Empowerment/Granting Authority

<!-- p65 -->
Jesus not only taught and corrected- He empowered His disciples to carry out His mission. He gave them authority to preach the Gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:18-20</span>). After His resurrection He promised the Holy Spirit as the source of power for their ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>).

<!-- p66 -->
Contemporary discipleship should include empowering others to operate in their spiritual gifts and serve effectively. This includes identifying gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:11-13</span>), encouraging their use, and releasing disciples into ministry. Empowerment also emphasizes dependence on the Holy Spirit for power, boldness, and wisdom, ensuring that disciples are spiritually equipped for the work to which God has called them.

<!-- p67 -->
### Multiplication

<!-- p68 -->
The goal of discipleship is not merely personal growth-it is multiplication. Jesus demonstrated this by training disciples to make more disciples (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). After His resurrection the apostles continued this multiplying mission, spreading the Gospel and planting churches throughout the known world. Paul’s instructions in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> affirm this principle, urging Timothy to teach others who will teach others.

<!-- p69 -->
Discipleship involves equipping disciples to make other disciples, creating a chain reaction of faith that expands God’s Kingdom. This process requires intentionality-teaching disciples how to mentor, teach, and coach others, sending them out with confidence and purpose (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 10:15</span>).

<!-- p70 -->
### Stay Connected

<!-- p71 -->
Discipleship does not end when someone is sent. Jesus showed ongoing connection with His disciples, continuing to encourage and instruct them even after His resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="21" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 21:15-19</span>). The early church maintained this pattern, gathering regularly for teaching, fellowship, and prayer (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>).

<!-- p72 -->
Contemporary discipleship should also maintain ongoing relationships, providing accountability, encouragement, and support as disciples continue to grow and lead. Preserving unity in the Body of Christ ensures that disciples are never isolated but remain strengthened and encouraged on their faith journey.

<!-- p73 -->
## Who Does Discipleship?

<!-- p74 -->
Everyone in the Body of Christ is responsible first to become a disciple, and then to contribute to the process of discipleship. There is a variety of gifts and functions, so our contribution may look different, but we are all responsible. The people ultimately most responsible for the state and health of discipleship in the local church are the leaders (**elders and deacons - <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 14:23</span>**), but everyone plays a role.

<!-- p75 -->
Discipleship is a living, growing process-like raising a child to maturity. Healthy discipleship brings strength and life to people. Like a parent who strengthens and nourishes a child and thereby makes them healthy and mature, disciple-makers help to nourish, strengthen, and cultivate the maturity of disciples. The ultimate goal of discipleship is not ‘transfer of knowledge’, but transformation.

<!-- p76 -->
We all must become perfect (mature) and make disciples. Though we have different gifts, there are common characteristics for all disciple-makers.

<!-- p77 -->
### Characteristics of a Disciple-Maker

<!-- p78 -->
A disciple-maker — is someone who not only follows Christ, but is dedicated to helping others grow in their faith. This role requires certain qualities that reflect both spiritual maturity and practical commitment. Disciple-makers must continually rely on God’s power, wisdom, and grace to overcome the challenges involved in leading others in their spiritual journeys.

<!-- p79 -->
#### Rooted in Christ

<!-- p80 -->
The foundation of a disciple maker’s life must be deeply rooted in Christ. Jesus said: *“I am the vine; you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="15" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 15:5</span>). Effective disciple makers know that the ability to grow and help others comes from daily closeness with Jesus. However, resistance to personal growth and spiritual change can hinder such dependence. Disciple makers must continually cultivate their personal relationship with Jesus through prayer, Scripture study, and obedience, demonstrating to those they mentor what it means to abide in Christ.

<!-- p81 -->
#### Devoted to the Mission

<!-- p82 -->
A disciple maker must remain devoted to the mission of making disciples, as Jesus commanded in the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). This mission requires intentional focus and prioritization, recognizing that disciple making is not an optional task but the central purpose of the church. However, challenges such as lack of time, busyness, and personal distractions can hinder maintaining focus. Disciple makers should intentionally set aside time for mentoring, teaching, and relationship-building, viewing disciple making as a way of life rather than an occasional activity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 19:10</span>).

<!-- p83 -->
#### Led by the Spirit

<!-- p84 -->
Disciple making cannot be accomplished by human strength alone; it requires the guidance and empowerment of the Holy Spirit. Jesus promised His disciples that they would receive power when the Holy Spirit came upon them (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>), enabling them to be witnesses and ministers of the Gospel. Disciple makers must be sensitive to the Spirit’s leading, relying on His wisdom to navigate complex situations, cultural barriers, and difficult conversations. Such reliance on the Holy Spirit enables disciple makers to act in love, boldness, and spiritual discernment, ensuring that their efforts are directed by the Spirit rather than by themselves.

<!-- p85 -->
#### Humble and Teachable

<!-- p86 -->
Humility is a distinguishing mark of an effective disciple maker. As Paul instructed, *“Let nothing be done through selfish ambition or conceit. But in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than himself”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 2:3</span>). Disciple makers recognize that they themselves are lifelong learners who must remain teachable and accountable. They welcome correction and feedback, modeling humility to those they mentor. Pride can become a serious obstacle to growth for both disciple makers and their disciples. Embracing accountability and maintaining a servant’s heart builds trust and creates an environment for growth and openness.

<!-- p87 -->
#### Patient and Persistent

<!-- p88 -->
Discipleship is rarely instantaneous—it is a long-term process that requires patience and persistence. Paul reminds believers to *“not grow weary while doing good, for in due season we shall reap if we do not lose heart”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:9</span>). Growth in faith often includes setbacks, questions, and failures, but disciple makers are called to walk alongside others, offering encouragement and support every step of the way. It is easy to become discouraged when progress seems slow, but disciple makers trust that God is at work even when results are not immediately visible.

<!-- p89 -->
Being a disciple maker requires a combination of spiritual dependence, commitment, and humility. It is a calling that demands patience and persistence while remaining Spirit-led and willing to learn. Disciple makers are not expected to be perfect, but they are expected to be faithful—to abide in Christ, invest in others, and trust God to bring growth and transformation in His time. These qualities reflect the love of Christ and help others become mature disciples who, in turn, are able to mentor others.

## Practical Tools for Discipleship

<!-- p91 -->
Effective discipleship requires thoughtful tools and practices that foster spiritual growth, accountability, and community. These tools help disciples build a solid foundation in the faith, grow in closeness to God, and be equipped to make disciples. Practical methods such as Bible study, prayer, small groups, and mentoring provide both the structure and the support needed for ongoing growth.

<!-- p92 -->
### Scripture Study

<!-- p93 -->
A deep immersion in Scripture is the foundation of discipleship. Jesus emphasized the importance of God’s Word in spiritual growth, stating that *“man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:4</span>). Regular Bible study and Scripture memorization enable disciples to internalize truth, resist lies, and apply God’s wisdom in everyday life.

<!-- p94 -->
Disciples should be encouraged to engage Scripture not merely as an academic exercise, but to know God and transform their hearts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="119" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="105" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 119:105</span>). Practical approaches such as reading plans, study guides, and group discussions make it possible to explore doctrine and discuss application in community. By developing regular study habits, disciples lay a solid foundation for spiritual maturity and fruitful ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p95 -->
### Prayer

<!-- p96 -->
Prayer is the lifeline of discipleship, connecting believers to God’s presence, power, and guidance. Jesus taught His disciples to pray in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 11:1-4</span>, emphasizing both dependence on God and spiritual alignment with His will. Through prayer, disciples learn to seek God’s wisdom, intercede for others, and receive strength for ministry.

<!-- p97 -->
Teaching disciples to pray—both individually and together—equips them to cultivate closeness with God and discern His leading (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 4:6-7</span>). Incorporating prayer journals, prayer groups, and the practice of fasting fosters spiritual discipline and strengthening, ensuring that disciples remain Spirit-led and focused on the mission.

<!-- p98 -->
### Small Groups and Community

<!-- p99 -->
Discipleship thrives in community. The early church modeled relational discipleship through fellowship, prayer, and breaking bread together (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>). Small groups and accountability partnerships provide a safe space where disciples can grow together, share struggles, and celebrate victories.

<!-- p100 -->
The local church is the primary hub of discipleship relationships, offering opportunities for group Bible studies, prayer meetings, and fellowship events. These gatherings allow disciples to practice love, bear one another’s burdens (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:2</span>), and encourage one another on the path to spiritual maturity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="24-25" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:24-25</span>). Small groups also provide a foundation for multiplying leaders, as mature disciples train and guide others to lead groups and mentor new believers.

<!-- p101 -->
### Mentoring

<!-- p102 -->
Mentoring offers a personal and intentional approach to discipleship. Jesus modeled this by investing deeply in the twelve disciples and walking with them through a process of teaching, correction, and encouragement. Similarly, one-on-one relationships allow mentors to tailor guidance to individual needs, helping disciples grow in faith, character, and leadership.

<!-- p103 -->
Mentors serve as accountability partners, offering support, prayer, and wisdom during seasons of growth and struggle. Practical tools such as spiritual assessments, goal-setting, and regular check-ins help disciples stay focused and motivated. Peer relationships within mentoring structures also foster mutual growth, as both mentor and mentee learn from shared experiences (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PRO" data-chapter="27" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Proverbs 27:17</span>).

## **Personal Questions for Reflection**

<!-- p105 -->
1.  What does it personally mean to you to be a disciple of Jesus? How do you actively follow Him in your everyday life?

<!-- p106 -->
2.  Reflect on Jesus’ model of discipleship. Which aspect of His approach - invitation, teaching, demonstration, or empowerment - stands out to you the most, and why?

<!-- p107 -->
3.  How can you multiply your faith by mentoring others? What challenges or fears might hold you back, and how can you overcome them? (Notice, I said multiply your faith, not multiply your disciples!)

<!-- p108 -->
4.  What mentor qualities (e.g., patience, humility, relational orientation) do you see in yourself? Which traits do you feel prompted to develop further?

<!-- p109 -->
5.  Think about the people in your life. Whom might God be calling you to mentor? How can you take the first step in building these relationships?

## Questions for Group Discussion (20-30 minutes)

<!-- p111 -->
1.  **Icebreaker question:** Who has had the greatest impact on your spiritual growth, and what did he or she do that influenced you?

<!-- p112 -->
2.  **Exploring Jesus’ Model:** Read <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span>. How does Jesus’ invitation “Follow Me” express the very essence of discipleship? What does this look like today?

<!-- p113 -->
3.  **The Call to Make Disciples:** Discuss <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>. Why is making disciples a central part of the church’s mission? How can we, as individuals, participate in this mission?

<!-- p114 -->
4.  **Overcoming Challenges:** What common obstacles exist to making disciples (e.g., time, fear, lack of self-confidence)? How can we support one another in overcoming these obstacles?

<!-- p115 -->
5.  **Practical Application:** What is one specific step you can take this week to invest in someone’s spiritual growth? How can we, as a group, encourage one another in this process?
