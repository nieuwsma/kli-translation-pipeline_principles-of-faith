# Chapter 2 - God-Eternal Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is extremely important to lay a firm foundation in the knowledge of who God is. We can come to an understanding by studying:

<!-- p2 -->
- His desires, intentions, and actions,

<!-- p3 -->
- His nature, character, and attributes,

<!-- p4 -->
- What is said about Him, and what He says about Himself.

<!-- p5 -->
Scripture reveals the aspects of God in a unique way, and each facet deepens our understanding of the unchanging, eternal God.

<!-- p6 -->
Reducing God to a single definition is difficult, if not impossible, since He is too great and multifaceted to be simplified. Although we can and will make great efforts to explain, describe, and depict God, ultimately, like art and music, He must be experienced to be understood.

<!-- p7 -->
When a person listens to music, he describes it by expressing how it moves him-how it resonates within, going beyond mere notes and rhythms. A melody can carry warmth or intensity, a rhythm can pulse like a heartbeat, and a crescendo can create a sense of rapture or longing. Music can soothe like a gentle wave, or electrify like a storm, lifting the listener's mood, clarifying his thoughts, or transporting him to an entirely different place. This experience is much more than a sequence of sounds; it is the soul of the music that remains, revealed in how it shapes the listener's emotions and his personal journey.

<!-- p8 -->
In the same way, a viewer of a painting looks beyond the colors or brushstrokes, exploring what these elements embody. A landscape can evoke a sense of quiet seclusion, whereas an abstract canvas can stir curiosity or self-examination. Certain hues can bring warmth, while the composition can create a sense of peace, chaos, or beauty. A painting becomes a living experience, provoking a shift in perspective or a profound emotional response that words alone cannot convey. And music and art thus turn into something more than their physical forms-they become encounters that touch something essential, changing the listener or viewer in deep and highly personal ways.

## Understanding God through the Old Testament

<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: God, Who Dwells with His Creation

<!-- p11 -->
A key theme in the narrative about God is His desire to dwell with His creation. This is clear in the stories throughout Genesis. Over its fifty chapters and the genealogies from Adam (the first man) to Joseph (the last major character in the book of Genesis, the last significant leader of God’s people before Moses) a period of approximately ~2,400 years passes. Through these stories we see the constancy of God’s goodness, despite human depravity.

<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God establishes relationships with chosen individuals.

<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - The first humans, created by God and placed in the Garden of Eden. God walks and speaks with them, gives them instruction, and establishes a relationship of mutual love and obedience. However, this relationship is affected by their disobedience when they eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 1-3).

<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - Although Cain ultimately rebels by killing his brother Abel, God speaks to him directly, warning of the danger of sin and offering guidance before the murder. Even after Cain is punished, God puts a mark on him to protect him from harm (Genesis 4).

<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - God establishes a special relationship with Noah, a righteous man in a time of universal corruption. God commands Noah to build an ark to save his family and the animals from the Flood. Afterwards God makes a covenant with Noah, promising never again to destroy the earth with a flood, and sets the rainbow as the sign of this covenant (Genesis 6-9).

<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - One of the most significant relationships in Genesis: God calls Abram to leave his homeland and promises to make him the father of a great nation. God makes a covenant with Abram, promising land, descendants, and blessing for all nations through him. Abram’s faith and obedience become central themes, and his name is changed to Abraham as part of the covenant (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22).

<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - Sarah’s Egyptian servant, Hagar encounters God in the wilderness after being mistreated. God comforts her, promises a future for her son Ishmael, and commands her to return to Abraham and Sarah. Hagar names God “El-Roi” (“The God Who Sees Me”), acknowledging His care for her (Genesis 16).

<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - The son of Abraham and Sarah, Isaac inherits the covenant promises given to his father. God directly reaffirms these promises to Isaac, including the promise of offspring and blessings. God also leads Isaac during a famine, protecting him and confirming the covenant relationship (Genesis 26).

<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - Although her relationship with God is indirect, God answers Rebekah’s prayer about her difficult pregnancy, revealing that her twins, Jacob and Esau, will become two nations with different destinies (Genesis 25).

<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God establishes a personal relationship with Jacob, appearing to him in a dream and confirming the covenant promises about land, descendants, and blessings. Jacob encounters God multiple times, including the famous wrestling match in which God changes his name to Israel, symbolizing his transformation and the emergence of Israel as a nation (Genesis 28, 32, 35).

<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Although Joseph’s relationship with God is not based on direct appearances or covenants like Abraham’s or Jacob’s, God is deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, God’s grace, and providence, God guides Joseph’s path from being sold into slavery to becoming a powerful ruler in Egypt. Joseph’s faith in God’s sovereignty enables him to forgive his brothers, understanding that God used his sufferings for good (Genesis 37, 39-50).

<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this narrative, God, desiring to dwell with His creation, is forced to be content with dwelling with individual people and, ultimately, through the covenant with Abraham forms a people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="17" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 17:7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: God, Who Dwells with His People

<!-- p24 -->
God’s desire to dwell with His people is also clearly seen in the second book of the Bible, Exodus. In the book of Exodus, the people of Israel, who had lived for a time in relative peace and harmony in Egypt, are now oppressed by a pharaoh who did not know about Joseph and feared the Hebrews (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 1:6-8</span>). God’s people lived in Egypt for approximately ~400 years (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="40-41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 12:40-41</span>), with the latter part of this period spent in slavery (there is no universal consensus regarding how long Israel was in Egyptian bondage).

<!-- p25 -->
In the mighty presence of a bush that was burning but not consumed, God reveals Himself to Moses, a Hebrew by birth, raised in Egypt, a murderer in exile, who was tending his father-in-law’s flock in the wilderness.

<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: 'I AM has sent me to you.' "
>

<!-- p28 -->
</span>

<!-- p29 -->
The phrase **“I AM THAT I AM”** (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is one of the most profound self-revelations in Scripture. When Moses asks for God’s name, God answers with this phrase, which can be translated as "I AM WHO I AM," "I WILL BE WHO I WILL BE," or simply "I AM." This statement is at first a bit puzzling, but it has deep meaning. Can you imagine introducing yourself to someone new as ‘I am’? When God reveals Himself as ‘I AM WHO I AM,’ He testifies to His nature, His eternal presence, His sovereignty. ‘I am’ is infinite in meaning and purpose.

<!-- p30 -->
God uses Moses to reveal Himself to Pharaoh:

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Exodus 7:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And you shall say to him: 'The Lord God of the Hebrews has sent me to you, saying: "Let My people go, that they may serve Me in the wilderness"; but indeed until now you have not listened!'
>

<!-- p33 -->
</span>

<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh that God is ‘I AM,’ and that God demands the Israelites be released so that they may worship Him in the wilderness.

<!-- p35 -->
Plagues and calamities follow, but in the end Pharaoh relents and allows the Israelites to go.

<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="31" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p37 -->
> **<u>Exodus 12:31</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Then he called for Moses and Aaron by night and said: "Rise, go out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel. And go, serve the Lord, as you have said.
>

<!-- p38 -->
</span>

<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness, God miraculously delivers His people from the vengeful Egyptian army (Exodus 14), provides protection from the sun and a pillar of fire to warm them during the cool desert nights (Exodus 13), food from heaven (Exodus 16), and water to drink (Exodus 17).

<!-- p40 -->
God brings His people to Mount Sinai, where He commands Moses to consecrate the people, gives them the Law (including the Ten Commandments), and establishes His covenant with them (the Old Covenant) (Exodus 19, 20, 24). This covenant (the Mosaic Covenant) is founded on the Abrahamic Covenant and lasts from Moses to Jesus.

<!-- p41 -->
Despite making a covenant with His people, the relationship remains that of a loving and jealous God who seeks them and abides with a very stubborn and ungrateful people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="32" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 32:9</span>). Ignoring God’s miraculous intervention, they maintain a rebellious posture throughout a significant part of the narrative. God even brings them into the Promised Land and says they may inherit it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="33" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 33:3</span>), but out of fear and disobedience they neglect the supernatural deliverance God gave them and instead are forced to wander the wilderness for 40 years, until nearly all of that generation had died (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-34" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).

<!-- p42 -->
### The Rest of the Old Testament

<!-- p43 -->
Over ~1,400 years between the death of Moses and the birth of Christ, the Israelites experienced a recurring cycle of devotion that brought God’s favor and blessings, followed by apostasy, God’s judgment, their subsequent repentance, and, ultimately, God’s deliverance. This pattern is especially evident in the books of Judges, Kings, and Chronicles, which describe cycles in which the people turn away from God, often worshiping other gods, leading to periods of oppression or exile. In response to their cry, God repeatedly raised up judges, prophets, or leaders to deliver them.

<!-- p44 -->
Throughout these years, despite the Israelites’ frequent failures to keep the covenant with Him, God remains faithful, displaying both justice and mercy. He saves and forgives His people again and again, demonstrating unwavering commitment and love.

<!-- p45 -->
As we see in Scripture, God reveals Himself through actions, relationships, and even names that speak of His character. Each name represents a particular aspect of His holistic character. These names were given as part of the narrative of how God revealed Himself to His people. For personal study, an Appendix includes a reference table: Table 10 - Names of God in the Old Testament. Read these names and study the Scripture references. Each reference is a story that shows precisely how God saved, delivered, and rescued His people.

<!-- p46 -->
Also included in the Appendix is a further set of attributes that describe His nature, descriptions of His actions and intentions: Table 11 - Attributes of God. These attributes, though somewhat more abstract than the names of God, are no less real!

<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand God the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (the Trinity)-acting together as a single whole. In further study we will look in more detail at the names and attributes of Jesus and the Holy Spirit.

<!-- p48 -->
The character and actions of God are fully expressed in the unity of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. Scripture teaches that God is one in essence but exists as three distinct Persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Scripture affirms God’s oneness, saying, “there is no God besides Me” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="45" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 45:5</span>). Yet the Godhead is understood as a composite unity—three distinct Persons who act with a single mind and purpose, united in the truest sense. Each Person of the Trinity has a unique role: the Father creates, the Son redeems, and the Holy Spirit sanctifies. The term “Trinity” does not appear in the Bible; it was developed as a theological expression to describe this concept revealed through Scripture.

<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can be illustrated with examples from nature that help clarify this idea of unity in diversity. Like water, which can be liquid, ice, or vapor, yet remains H₂O, each Person of the Trinity is fully God yet distinct as a Person. Or consider a triangle with three sides and three angles forming one complete figure; if you remove one side, it ceases to be a triangle. Similarly, the nature of God is incomplete without all three Persons, who act together in perfect unity.

<!-- p50 -->
- God the Father: He is the Creator and Sustainer of all, often understood as the initiator within the Godhead. His love for humanity is a central theme of the biblical story, vividly shown in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:16</span>. In our study we will consider God the Father as a loving Father and a righteous Judge who made a way for our redemption.

<!-- p51 -->
- God the Son: Co-existent and co-eternal with the Father, Jesus took on human nature, was conceived by the Holy Spirit, and born of the virgin Mary. He lived a sinless life, died to redeem humanity, and rose again, conquering sin and death. He now sits at the right hand of the Father, interceding for us (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In our study we will consider Jesus as the perfect propitiatory sacrifice, the risen Lord, and the Head of the church.

<!-- p52 -->
- God the Holy Spirit: The Holy Spirit, co-existing and equal with the Father and the Son, is sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world of sin and to prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. He empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In our study we will consider the Holy Spirit as the Comforter given to us by God to equip, empower, sanctify, and abide with us.

<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct Persons, the triune nature of God is revealed as a perfect unity of purpose, will, and love, each Person playing a vital role in the divine mission of creation, redemption, and sanctification. The Trinity invites believers to know God fully — as loving Father, redeeming Savior, and transforming Spirit.

<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion

<!-- p55 -->
We can be uniquely recognized by our names: husband, father, son, brother, friend. Each of these is a statement of who we are in relation to someone. We can also be known by our qualities: faithful, patient, kind, intelligent, cheerful. Similarly, we can know God by His qualities and names- He is our Healer, Provider, Shepherd, and Redeemer. However, since labels and words are only a small part of us, for others truly to know us, we need to be experienced. All the more is this true with regard to God. To truly and fully know Him, we need to experience an encounter with Him.

<!-- p56 -->
The names of God reveal His character, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of what these names mean. For example, “God—my Healer” takes on a richer, deeper meaning when we grasp the depth of sickness, despair, or helplessness from which God delivers. Knowing Him as our Provider is transformed when we have experienced real need and found Him faithful.

<!-- p57 -->
The Scriptures provide context for these encounters, showing us God’s faithfulness to His people across time and circumstances, affirming that His names and attributes are not merely titles but realities that He brings to life, manifesting in the lives of people. Through this interplay of personal experience and biblical narrative we come to know God—not as a distant description, but as a living, active relationship in every season of our lives.
