# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
The Scriptures tell us many stories about sin-its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin is to fall short of God’s standard, or 'miss the mark,' a term from archery that describes an archer’s failure to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
The Old Testament and the New Testament use various words for sin, each highlighting aspects such as its type, gravity, intent, victim, nature, and the punishment required. There are several broad categories that Scripture uses to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against man, sin against self

<!-- p4 -->
- Deliberate sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- One-time sin, habitual sin

<!-- p6 -->
- Sin requiring severe punishment such as death, sin requiring financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In our modern criminal justice systems, we have similar distinctions: degrees of crime, premeditated or crimes of passion, crimes that require financial restitution, or imprisonment, or even capital punishment. An act can be a crime and also a sin, but crime is a human classification, whereas sin is God’s classification.

<!-- p8 -->
The various Greek and Hebrew words for sin show a high degree of correspondence. All sin broadly falls under the umbrella of the word 'missing the mark', ***Hamartia*** in Greek, and ***Chata'*** in Hebrew. This umbrella term is used for very grave sins—adultery, murder—but also lighter sins, such as showing favoritism to the rich in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV">James 2:9</span>).

<!-- p9 -->
These are some of the words that Scripture uses for sin. They help us understand how weighty-and how deeply we need the grace of God.

## Sin Requires Sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the account of Adam and Eve, God killed animals and made garments from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first shedding of blood to cover the consequences of sin. There are a few other similar accounts in the book of Genesis, but God formally established the way of sacrifice for sin in the book of Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general offering for atonement and devotion to God, often representing total surrender. This offering addressed sin broadly, covering both known and unknown sins. The rite involved presenting a male animal—typically a bull, sheep, goat, or bird—without blemish. The offerer laid his hands on the animal's head, signifying identification with the sacrifice, and then the entire animal was burned on the altar. This act of burning the whole offering signified a complete commitment to remove sin and to align one's life with God. The burnt offering emphasized the weight of sin and the need for full dedication, expressing reverence and repentance through the giving of something valuable.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, known as ***Minchah***, was an offering of thanksgiving and dedication rather than a direct offering for sin. This offering accompanied other sacrifices, such as the burnt and peace offerings, and was intended to express gratitude, worship, and devotion to God. Made of fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, the grain offering did not involve blood. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar as a gift to God, while the remainder was given to the priests as food. Though it did not target specific sins, the grain offering was an important part of communal worship, signifying the offerer's devotion and gratitude for God's provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and signified peace with God. This offering often followed sin and atonement sacrifices, celebrating a restored relationship with God after sin had been addressed. The offerer could bring a male or female animal without blemish, such as a bull, sheep, or goat. In the procedures, the fat portions—the choicest parts of the animal—were burned on the altar as God's portion, while the remaining meat was shared by the priests and the offerer in a communal meal. The peace offering was unique because it symbolized fellowship and unity, reflecting a state of wholeness and oneness with God. After sin was atoned for, this offering allowed the community to enjoy God's acceptance and celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was designated specifically for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When an individual or the community realized they had sinned unintentionally, they could present a sin offering to seek cleansing and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the person's status: a priest or the assembly brought a bull, a leader brought a male goat, and an ordinary person brought a female goat or a lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the sacrifice before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and dispose of the remains of the animal outside the camp if it was a communal sin. This offering highlighted the gravity of even unintentional sins and provided a way for people to remain purified and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins involving breach of trust, harming another, or profaning sacred things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins that demanded amends, such as property offenses or violations against sacred items. To perform this offering, the offender brought an unblemished ram to be sacrificed. If the sin involved harm to another person or property, the offender was also required to make restitution by returning the property along with an additional twenty percent as a penalty. The guilt offering emphasized the importance of acknowledging one's guilt and taking concrete steps to make things right. It highlighted the social and relational nature of sin, stressing that true atonement required not only seeking God's forgiveness but also setting things right with those affected by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Corporate Sin

<!-- p77 -->
Beyond these offerings specific to individual sin, there were also sacrifices and offerings for the nation's corporate sin that were offered daily, weekly, and monthly, but the most important of these was the **Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was the annual set of rites described in Leviticus 16, designed to cleanse the entire community of Israel from their corporate sins of the previous year. This unique observance took place once a year, on the 10th day of the seventh month, and addressed the sins of the nation rather than individual offenses. It served to purify the tent of meeting, the priesthood, and the people, signaling atonement and cleansing for the whole community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the heart of the Day of Atonement were two goats that served to represent both the sacrifice and the removal of sin. The High Priest cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat, designated "for the Lord," was sacrificed as a sin offering, and its blood was taken by the High Priest into the Most Holy Place. There, the blood was sprinkled upon the mercy seat—the cover of the Ark of the Covenant—signifying atonement for the people's sins and reconciling them to God. This act of sprinkling was highly significant, as it represented the purification of the holy place and ensured that God's presence would continue to remain with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, known as the scapegoat (Azazel), symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest would lay his hands on that goat, confessing the collective sins of Israel, thus placing them upon that animal. Then the goat was sent into the wilderness to die alone, signifying the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act was a powerful illustration of atonement and being set free from sin, showing that God not only forgave but also removed the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound manifestation of God’s forgiveness and cleansing, which allowed the nation to delay the consequences of sin, and to postpone judgment each year. This atonement did not permanently forgive the nation’s sins, but rather delayed God’s judgment.

## We Have All Sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible address the issue of sin in different ways. When Moses recorded the law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament) he recorded specific penalties for particular sins/crimes. I use the word crime here interchangeably, because the law books were the legal system of that time for the Israelites.

<!-- p89 -->
In the prophetic and historical books, sin and its consequences are often described from the perspective of the fate of the entire nation. When the king and the people rebelled against God, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, were defeated in battle, and in some instances were taken into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs the author writes like a loving father to his son, warning him about the personal dangers of sin, and a lifestyle that goes against God’s commands. In Psalms, many of David’s writings deal with the personal and emotional consequences of having sinned.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns about sin, calls people to repent personally, and offers the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the Apostle Paul makes a sweeping case about how God planned to deal with sin, and how we can be set free from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
The truth is that we are all guilty of sin, and we all must pay the cost for our breaking of the law.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV">
<!-- p94 -->
&gt; **<u>Romans 3:23</u>** (NKJV) *for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God,*

<!-- p95 -->
</span>
<!-- p96 -->
Each of us has sinned, separated ourselves from God, and will stand before the final judgment and its consequences. It may be easy for us to try to justify our actions and say that we have not committed grave sins like murder or adultery and that we are largely moral and good people. This false attempt at self-justification ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and sinned against, and it ignores the reality of a holy and perfect God. Scripture says that if we deny that we sin we are liars, and the truth is not in us!

<!-- p97 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="1JN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="8-10" data-version="NKJV">
<!-- p98 -->
&gt; **<u>1 John 1:8-10</u>** (NKJV) **8** *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us.* ***9** If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.* ***10** If we say that we have not sinned, we make Him a liar, and His word is not in us.*

<!-- p99 -->
</span>
<!-- p100 -->
Since we have all sinned, we all need redemption; otherwise we will face judgment and eternal separation from God. But the story doesn’t end there. Thanks be to God—He made a way for us to be saved, healed, and restored to Him!

<!-- p101 -->
&gt; <u>**John 3:16**</u> (NKJV) For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life.*

<!-- p102 -->
From the first sacrifice in Genesis to the cross of Christ, God’s plan has always been to redeem us and restore our relationship with Him. No sin is too great, and no distance too far, for the grace of God to reach us.
