# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
Often in Christianity we think of repentance as something fundamental only at the beginning of our faith in Jesus. A repentant heart goes far beyond turning from sin to salvation. Repentance from dead works means living with our hearts turned away from things that harm us or hinder our growth-and directed toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews places ‘repentance from dead works’ as the first item in the list of foundational teachings after the elementary principles of the Messiah (Chapter 4).

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Everyone who has ever been employed knows what work is. Work is a collection of responsibilities you carry out in order to receive payment of wages so that you can earn a living (employment). Employment is an agreement to exchange your talents and efforts for something of value that will help you sustain yourself. You work because you get something from it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can bring a sense of purpose, and often a sense of identity. It is very common to be asked ‘What do you do for a living?’ and the answer begins with an identity statement ‘I am’. Although work can be enjoyable and purposeful, it is also very likely to be physically or mentally hard, burdensome, challenging, and stressful. If your job is not any of these things, you may find it to be too menial and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work 40 hours a week, and sleep 8 hours per night, you spend 36% of your waking hours working, and this does not include the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting, maintaining a household, and all the other necessary tasks you must do to keep your life in order.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? It varies, but fundamentally because we get something of value from it.

<!-- p13 -->
Would you continue working at that job if they stopped paying you? For most of us the simple and very clear answer is, NO!

<!-- p14 -->
Would you continue working at that job if it was killing you? I suppose it depends on how fast.

<!-- p15 -->
The context of ***nekros*** attached to ***erga*** is work or activity that is dead, lifeless, unable to bear fruit! The author of Hebrews gives us a warning about dead work.

<!-- p16 -->
In biology, there are several criteria used to determine whether an organism is alive (this is a partial list for our purposes):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living organisms convert energy through metabolic processes in order to grow, persist, and sustain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and Development**: Life involves growth and development over time, with a specific life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living organisms have the ability to reproduce, bringing forth offspring or new organisms.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to Stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adjust to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as temperature regulation or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living organisms can adapt to their environments through genetic changes, thus allowing survival in various conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this standard of ‘life’ to our works with a bit of creative license, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow growth or personal development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, they cannot produce positive or desirable outcomes.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, the light of God’s presence, or the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Do not maintain internal stability, they become uncontrolled, they lack self-control.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not change (are excessively rigid) and no longer help us to live.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s desire for us. God wants to bring us life!

<!-- p30 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV">
<!-- p31 -->
&gt; **<u>John 10:10</u>** (NKJV) *The thief does not come except to steal, and to kill, and to destroy. I have come that they may have life, and that they may have it in abundance.*

<!-- p32 -->
</span>
<!-- p33 -->
Although the states of death and life are only two. There are degrees of death. Our dead works may not yet be completely dead in every aspect, yet, but they will be! Dead works are like poison; some have more severe effects.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning normally. Some poisons have minor effects, such as headaches. Others affect how we think, see, or even whether we can have children (living by bearing fruit). Dead works are just like that. They may be just a headache, or they may lead to death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are any works which, upon review and assessment, do not bring the abundant life that Jesus promised. Having an extramarital relationship is a very potent poison, it is highly likely to have full and negative effects soon. Being unreasonably stubborn and resentful will slowly poison a person until he has driven everyone away from himself.

<!-- p36 -->
To be delivered from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
‘Repenting from dead works’ is turning to God and leaving behind anything that harms our walk with Him-starting with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Building on the poison analogy for dead works, if you discovered that your favorite snack was full of toxic heavy metals that would cause you a fatal condition if eaten, what would you do? We would put it away from us without hesitation, and we would never enjoy it again. Recognizing that eating it means our early death, and perhaps a painful one.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must turn and abandon the things of this life that poison us and lead us to our physical and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To understand this fully we need to look more closely at the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The word used in Hebrews is *repentance*. This is distinguished from the word *repent,* an example is found in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV">
<!-- p44 -->
&gt; **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV) - “*From that time Jesus began to preach, and to say, Repent: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.*”

<!-- p45 -->
</span>
<!-- p46 -->
Repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb, an action or event, meaning to change one’s mind or purpose; to turn from sin and toward God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun, a state of having repented, meaning to have a change of mind; the state of having turned from sin and toward God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews ‘repentance from dead works’ He is speaking of the state of having repented! We should live in a continual state of having ‘turned’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How often do we need to repent? A tricky question! We need to keep changing our thinking until we stop doing dead works. Repenting is **not asking forgiveness, or apologizing**, though that may be involved. Repenting is turning away from the former way of thinking.

We change our thinking by hearing the truth that transforms our death-producing beliefs. His truth may be spoken to us by a friend, read in a book, revealed personally by the Holy Spirit, or in many other ways.

<!-- p59 -->
### Patterns of Change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in changing our minds. If we don’t see the need to change our minds, then why open our eyes to look for change? Again, returning to the poison analogy, if you cannot recognize that the poison is killing you, why would you stop continuing to use it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that a person must reach ‘rock bottom,’ then they will look at their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that we human beings are more complex. There are many theories related to behavior change in the field of psychology, one of the most respected is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** Developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **[1].**

<!-- p62 -->
People can go through a cycle of six stages of change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: No intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: There is an intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken in order to change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has been changed for a short period.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has been changed and continues to be maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: There is no desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no set time for how long any stage may take. For some people, with regard to certain issues in their lives, they do not meaningfully progress through these stages. At other times, we may move from stage 1 to 6 very quickly.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Situation:**, a father who has a sudden pang of conscience after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *strict* but *loving* parent. He believes raising his voice is important. He is not thinking about changing. In fact, he may even justify it- “That’s how my father raised me.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, he loses patience and yells at his young daughter more harshly, louder, and angrier than before. He is shocked. His lip trembles. He leaves quietly. In that moment… something changes.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. The image of her hurt face replays in his mind over and over. His stomach twists. He tells himself, *“That is not the person I want to be.”* He knows he needs to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He walks into her room, kneels, sincerely asks for forgiveness, and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You didn’t deserve that.”* He hugs her. Motivated to reinforce that change, he prays and talks to a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he begins reading parenting books to cultivate positive personal emotional regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He’s not perfect, but something has truly changed. He no longer justifies the old behavior-and has no desire to go back.

<!-- p78 -->
### Helping Others Change

<!-- p79 -->
A large part of our discussion has focused on the act and necessity of repentance from the perspective of the person who needs to change. However, we will encounter people who very much need to change, but are stuck in precontemplation/**contemplation** - blind to their need to change. We may also encounter those who greatly desire to change, but do not know what to do (**preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
Jesus, as He was preaching to the crowds, often encountered people who did not know they needed to change. We cannot change a person’s mind by force nor repent on their behalf. That is not our place, nor is it the example of Jesus.

<!-- p81 -->
Jesus addresses this slow and sometimes failed process of change by speaking in parables. Why did Jesus choose to speak in parables? He reveals the reason in Matthew:

<!-- p82 -->
&gt; <u>**Matthew 13:10,11,13,15**</u> (NKJV)
&gt;
&gt;***10** The disciples came to Him and said, "Why do You speak to them in parables?"*
&gt;
&gt;***11** He answered and said to them…*
&gt;
&gt;***13** … Therefore I speak to them in parables, because seeing they do not see, and hearing they do not hear, nor do they understand.*
&gt;
&gt;***15** … FOR THE HEARTS OF THIS PEOPLE HAVE GROWN DULL. THEIR EARS ARE HARD OF HEARING, AND THEIR EYES THEY HAVE CLOSED, LEST THEY SHOULD SEE WITH THEIR EYES AND HEAR WITH THEIR EARS, LEST THEY SHOULD UNDERSTAND WITH THEIR HEARTS AND TURN, SO THAT I SHOULD HEAL THEM.'*

<!-- p83 -->
Jesus says that He uses parables to gradually open the minds and eyes of people who neither see nor understand the truth. Truth is like a blinding light, so intense all at once that it forces us to shut our eyes; it hurts. By exposing ourselves to a portion of that light, it brings illumination that makes us truly see the darkness we are in. When we receive this light, we recognize that the word of God is like a lamp to our path and a light to our feet (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="Psalm" data-chapter="119" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="110" data-version="NKJV">Psalm 119:110</span>) and that we can use words of truth to bring light into any aspect of our lives.

<!-- p84 -->
If you find yourself dealing with someone whose mind is closed, I advise you to pray and seek God’s counsel on how to proceed. From experience and from the example of Jesus, an encounter full of grace together with truth, sometimes gradual, can begin to open the eyes of the blind. Remember it is the work of the Spirit to bring conviction and illumination.

<!-- p85 -->
As they begin to go through the transformative process of repentance, it brings good fruit in their lives. If they have been sickened by poison, having their eyes opened, abandoning that poison, and taking the antidote will begin to make them healthy.

<!-- p86 -->
When we truly embrace this change we can pray like King David:

<!-- p87 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="Psalm" data-chapter="139" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="23-24" data-version="NKJV">
<!-- p88 -->
&gt; **<u>Psalm 139:23-24</u>** (NKJV) **23** *Search me, O God, and know my heart; Try me, and know my anxieties;* ***24** And see if there is any wicked way in me, And lead me in the way everlasting.*

<!-- p89 -->
</span>
<!-- p90 -->
David’s prayer of repentance is an invitation for the light of truth to shine into our lives and reveal the areas of darkness that make us sick and produce death.

### Godly Sorrow and the Goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although hitting rock bottom is more of a story than a sure requirement for change, God can use sorrow to affect our thinking. God will use sorrow to bring us to repentance. He will show the wrongness of our actions and the state of our lives and hearts in order to open our eyes to see our total and overall need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV">
<!-- p94 -->
&gt; **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV) *For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.”*

<!-- p95 -->
</span>
<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>the experience of grief or distress that brings about *metanoia*</u>, is not the same as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of grief or distress, but the world offers no hope, only relief and the promise of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and mourning.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the endurance of pain—physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual—especially when it is prolonged or severe.

<!-- p99 -->
Mourning is a multifaceted emotional response to a significant personal loss, especially of a relationship or of presence.

<!-- p100 -->
Grief, suffering, and sorrow are closely related concepts. In the human condition we will go through all three, we can learn and grow through all three, but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
Scripture says every good and perfect gift comes from God above (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give His children good gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow will bring discomfort, but His discipline is righteous and loving. Just as a good father would not deliberately break his child's arm to ‘teach him a lesson’, God will not bring sickness or disease upon His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### An Example of Worldly Sorrow versus Godly Sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick, and deliberately hit her, causing her to cry and leaving her with a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
When their father heard the commotion he investigated, and when he learned what his son had done he took the stick, broke it in two and threw it away. He sent his son inside to think about what he had done and calmed his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came inside to deal with his son he found him crying. When he asked him why he was crying his son said, “because you broke my stick”.

<!-- p106 -->
The father's reply was, “I am sorry that you care more about your stick than you care about your sister whom you hurt” then he sent him back to his room for further reflection.

<!-- p107 -->
After a few minutes his son came out again and asked for forgiveness. He said he was still sad because his father had broken his stick, and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example the boy experienced worldly sorrow, for the stick he had lost, and then finally godly sorrow, for his sister who had been hurt.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and the goodness of God are linked. God is a good Father who will allow us to go through the consequences of our actions but will still stand by our side as we walk through those consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
The unending goodness of God, of being ready to redeem us and restore us when we come to Him, is a positive catalyst on our path of repentance. His goodness is not that we should continue in sin, but that despite our sins He makes a way for us to be redeemed and to receive His salvation.

<!-- p112 -->
<u>**<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV">Romans 2:4</span>**</u> (NKJV) *Or do you despise the riches of His goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?*

<!-- p113 -->
Despite the goodness, mercy, and grace of God, some people do not repent nor change, they get stuck in a cycle of discouragement and pain. On the complete opposite end, for some people just the example of sin and its consequences in another person's life is enough to make them change their minds.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a great capacity to change; it is wired into our very nature. Humans have great adaptability, we can learn and grow, and find better ways of doing things. However, the capacity to change is not the same as the willingness or the motivation to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some people get stuck and others are very ready to change, but I can say that the entire process of change is due to the grace of God.

<!-- p116 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="8-10" data-version="NKJV">
<!-- p117 -->
&gt; **<u>Ephesians 2:8-10</u>** (NKJV) ***8** For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God,* ***9** not of works, lest anyone should boast.* ***10** For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them.*

<!-- p118 -->
</span>
<!-- p119 -->
The process of repentance begins before we receive Jesus as our personal Lord and Savior, but the lifestyle of repentance should continue throughout our entire Christian walk. We should cast aside dead works and change our minds from those toward the truth of God.

<!-- p120 -->
Repentance can be an entirely natural process, but repentance is also the work of the Spirit who calls us, opening our eyes to the truth, changing our hearts, drawing us to be transformed. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1" data-version="NKJV">Galatians 6:1</span> reminds us that there are those who will be caught in a trespass (sin) and need help to get unstuck from it! That help comes in the form of mature men and women who help restore us in a spirit of gentleness.

<!-- p121 -->
The Holy Spirit is the primary agent of change with whom human beings can cooperate, and this is affirmed by Scripture and also by research. A study by “Lyons et al. (2010) found that up to 82% of clients who experienced a spiritual awakening during substance abuse treatment and recovery were completely abstinent at a 1-year follow-up compared with 55% of non-spiritually awakened clients.” [2] While a human being can initiate and lead change, we can accomplish much more when we walk with the Spirit of God.

<!-- p122 -->
In our subsequent lessons about the Holy Spirit and about sanctification, we will see in more detail how the Spirit calls and transforms people’s hearts. Let us look at one such example, the story of King David and his model of repentance.

<!-- p123 -->
### David’s Model of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no narrative in Scripture presents a richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin found in 2 Samuel 11-12. It begins with King David remaining in Jerusalem instead of going to war, where he sees Bathsheba bathing. Despite knowing that she was the wife of Uriah, one of his faithful soldiers, David sent for her, and they entered into an adulterous relationship, resulting in Bathsheba becoming pregnant.

<!-- p125 -->
To conceal his actions, David brought Uriah back from the battlefield, hoping he would stay with Bathsheba so it would appear that he was the father of the child. However, Uriah, out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, refused to go to his home to be with Bathsheba. David then arranged for Uriah to be sent to the front lines where he was ultimately killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David quickly married Bathsheba to hide the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was not pleased with David’s actions and sent the prophet Nathan to confront him. When confronted by the prophet, David immediately confessed, “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify or make excuses for his actions. This confession was the first step in his repentance, acknowledging his wrongdoing directly to God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I know my transgressions, and my sin is always before me,*” show his complete ownership of his actions. He shows sorrow not only for the sin itself but also for how it offended God, saying, “*Against you, you only, have I sinned.*” David displayed “*a broken and contrite heart,*” a vital part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="Psalm" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he mourned deeply over the sin he committed and the pain it caused. This depth of regret was a sign that he desired not only forgiveness but also a transformed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God, “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="Psalm" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV">Psalm 51:10</span>). He wanted an inner change that would prevent him from repeating the same mistakes. Rather than asking only for forgiveness, David cried out for God to cleanse him, saying, “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="Psalm" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV">Psalm 51:7</span>). He recognized that only God could purify his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be renewed spiritually shows a true commitment to walk in God’s ways going forward. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="Psalm" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV">Psalm 51:12</span>) reflects his hope of being reconciled to God in every part of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God put away David’s sin, the Lord struck the child and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and begged God to spare the child, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find a personal prayer of repentance and an open confession of his wrongdoing. David’s decision to write his plea for mercy, forming one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible, shows his willingness to be open about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony of repentance and restoration for others to learn from, recording his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not just about feeling remorse; it was a deep transformation in his heart and life. Through the confession, acceptance, and heart change recorded in Psalm 51, David memorialized his sin as a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story among the most important examples of true repentance in the Bible.
