# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
Often in the Christian faith we consider repentance to be merely foundational for the start of our faith in Jesus. The essence of repentance is far deeper than turning from sin to obtain salvation. Repentance from dead works means that our heart is turned away from the things that harm us or hinder our growth-and turned toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews places ‘repentance from dead works’ as the first topic in the list of basic teachings after the elementary principles of Christ (Chapter 4).

### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever been employed knows what work is. Work is the set of tasks you do to receive a wage so that you can earn a living (employment). Employment is an agreement to exchange your skills and efforts for something of value that will help sustain you. You work because you get something from it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can give a sense of purpose, and often a sense of identity. It is very common to ask, ‘What do you do for a living?’ and the answer begins with a statement of identity, ‘I am.’ While work can be enjoyable and purposeful, it is also very likely to be physically or mentally hard, arduous, challenging, and stressful. If your job is none of these, you might find it very ordinary and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work a 40-hour job in a week, and sleep 8 hours per night, then you spend 36% of your waking time working, and that does not include the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting, taking care of the house, and all the other tasks necessary to keep your life on track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? The reasons vary, but by definition it is because we receive something valuable from it.

<!-- p13 -->
If they stopped paying you, would you continue to work at the same job? For most of us the easy and very obvious answer is—an emphatic, no!

<!-- p14 -->
If a job were killing you, would you keep working at it? I suppose it depends on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
The context of joining ***nekros*** with ***erga*** is labor or work that is dead, with no life-force, that cannot reproduce! The author of Hebrews is warning us about such dead labor.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, several criteria are used to determine whether an organism is alive or not (for our purposes this is a partial list):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism (metabolism)**: Living things convert energy through metabolic processes so that they grow, develop, and sustain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: Life involves increase and progress over time, with a distinct life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living things have the ability to reproduce, producing offspring or new units.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adjust itself according to changes occurring in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as the regulation of temperature or the balance of pH.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living organisms can adapt to their environment through genetic changes, allowing them to survive in various conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this standard of ‘life’ to our deeds with a bit of creative freedom, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  do not reproduce, cannot produce positive or desirable outcomes.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God's presence, or to the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  do not maintain internal stability, become dysregulated, are not self-controlled.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  do not adapt (are too rigid) and no longer help us survive.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God's will for us. God wants to give us life!

Where dead versus alive is a binary. Dying is a gradual process. Our dead works may not be completely dead in every category yet, and still they will become so! Dead works are like poisons; some have stronger effects.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning normally. Some poisons have minor effects, like headaches. Others affect the way we think and see, or even whether we can reproduce (live a fruitful life). Dead works are exactly the same. They may be nothing more than a headache, or they may cause death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are any works that, upon review and reflection, are not bringing about the abundant life promised by Jesus. Having an extramarital affair is a very potent poison; its full and negative impact is likely to set in very quickly. Being needlessly stubborn and spiteful will slowly poison a person until they have driven everyone away from them.

<!-- p36 -->
To be free from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
To ‘repent from dead works’ means to turn to God and leave behind anything that is harming our walk with Him-beginning with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Continuing the poison analogy for dead works, if you learned that your favorite snack was filled with toxic heavy metals that would give you a lethal condition if eaten, what would you do? We would set it aside without any hesitation, and never enjoy it again. Knowing that eating it would cause our untimely, and possibly painful, death.

<!-- p40 -->
We should repent! We must turn away from those things in this life that are poisoning us and leading us toward both our natural and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to look a little deeper into the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The word used in Hebrews is *repentance*. It is contrasted with the word *repent*; an example is found

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
Repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or event; it means to change one’s mind or purpose; to turn from sin and return to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is the state of having repented; it means a change of mind; the condition of having turned from sin and returned to God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews “*repentance from dead works*,” He is saying to remain in the state of having repented! We are to live in a continual state of having “turned away” from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times should we repent? Trick question! Until we stop doing dead works, we must keep changing our mind. Repenting is not asking for forgiveness, or apologizing, though that may be included. Repentance means turning away from the former way of thinking.

### Systems of Change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in changing our thoughts. If we do not even sense the need to change our thinking, why would we ever open our eyes in search of change? Again, returning to the poison example, if you do not realize that the poison is killing you, why would you stop taking it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that a person simply has to reach ‘rock-bottom’, then they will look at their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that we humans are more complex. In the study of psychology there are many theories related to behavior change, one highly respected theory is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People may pass through a cycle of six stages for change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: No intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: Intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken toward change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has been changed for some time.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has been changed and is being maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: No desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no set time for how long any stage will take. With respect to some issues in their lives, some people never meaningfully move forward through the stages. In other situations, we may progress from Stage 1 to 6 very quickly.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father who experiences a sudden self-awareness after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He considers himself a *firm* but *loving* parent. He believes that raising his voice is necessary. He is not considering changing. In fact, he may even justify it- “My dad raised me the same way.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, his patience snaps and he yells at his little daughter more loudly and more angrily, more harshly than ever before. She flinches. Her lips tremble. She quietly walks away. In that moment… something **changes**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. The image of her hurt face keeps playing in his mind again and again. His stomach twists. He says to himself, *“I don't want to become that kind of person.”* He knows he has to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes to her room, gets on his knees, apologizes sincerely, and says, *“Dad was wrong. You didn't deserve that.”* She hugs him. Motivated to cement the change, he prays and talks to a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he starts reading books on parenting to work on developing positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He is not perfect, but something has truly changed. He no longer justifies the old behavior-and he has no desire to return to it.

### Helping Others Change

<!-- p79 -->

However, we will meet people who badly need to change but are stuck in **Precontemplation**/**Contemplation** - blind to their need to change. We will likely also meet people who are desperate to change but are unsure what to do (**Preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
While preaching to the crowds, Jesus often encountered people who did not even realize that they needed to change. We cannot forcibly change someone’s mind, nor can we repent on their behalf. That is not our task, nor is it Jesus’ example.

### Godly Sorrow and the Goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although hitting rock bottom is more a myth than any definite precondition for change, God can still use sorrow to impress upon our minds. God will use sorrow to bring us to repentance. He will convict us about our actions and the state of our lives and hearts so that our eyes are opened and we see our total and complete need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or the experience of such grief or distress that leads to a *metanoia*, is not the same as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of grief or distress, but the world offers no hope, only the promise of relief and death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also distinct from suffering and grief.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the endurance of pain—physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual—especially when it is chronic or intense.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is a multifaceted emotional response to significant personal loss, especially loss related to relationships or existence.

<!-- p100 -->
Sorrow, suffering, and grief are closely related ideas. In human life we will experience all three; we can learn and grow through them, but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
Scripture says that every good and perfect gift is from above, from God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow will produce discomfort, but His discipline is just and loving. Just as a good father would not deliberately break his child's hand to ‘teach him a lesson,’ likewise God does not put sickness or disease on His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### Example of Worldly Sorrow vs. Godly Sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick, and he deliberately hit her with it, making her cry and leaving a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
Their father heard the noise and came to check, and when he found out what his son had done, he took the stick, broke it in half, and threw it away. He sent his son inside to reflect on what he had done and comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came inside to deal with his son, he found him crying. He asked him why he was crying, and the son said, “Because you broke my stick”.

<!-- p106 -->
The father replied, “I’m saddened that you care more about your stick than about the sister you hurt” and sent him back to his room to think further.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later his son came out and apologized. He said that he was still sad because Dad had broken his stick, and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example the boy experienced both kinds of sorrow: worldly sorrow over his lost stick, and ultimately godly sorrow over his injured sister.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and the goodness of God are interconnected. God is a good Father who will allow us to experience the consequences of our actions, yet will still stand with us as we go through those consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
God’s steadfast goodness—that if we come to Him He is ready to deliver and restore us—is a positive factor on our path of repentance. His goodness is not that we remain in sin, but that in spite of our sin He makes a way for our deliverance and for us to experience His salvation.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a great capacity for change; it is inherent in our very nature. Humans are highly adaptable; we can learn and grow, and find better ways of doing things. However, the capacity to change is not the same as the desire or motivation to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some people get stuck and others are so ready to change, but I can say that the entire process of change is truly by the grace of God.

### The Example of David’s Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps there is no richer example of repentance in Scripture than the story of King David’s sin described in 2 Samuel 11-12. It begins when King David remains in Jerusalem instead of going to war, where he sees Bathsheba bathing. Even knowing that she is the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David summons her, and they have relations, which leads to Bathsheba becoming pregnant.

<!-- p125 -->
To hide his act, David calls Uriah back from the battlefield, hoping that he will spend time with Bathsheba and appear to be the child’s father. But out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, Uriah refuses to go to his home, to Bathsheba. Then David arranges for Uriah to be sent to the front, where he is ultimately killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David hastily marries Bathsheba to conceal the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was displeased with David’s actions and sent the prophet Nathan to rebuke him. When the prophet confronted him, David immediately admitted, “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David neither tried to justify his actions nor made excuses. This confession was the first step in his repentance, in which he acknowledged his offense directly before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51 David’s words, “*For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me,*” reflect full responsibility for his actions. He laments not only the sin but also that he has offended God, saying, “*Against you, you only, have I sinned.*” David showed a “*broken and contrite heart*”—an essential part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This grief was not superficial; he deeply lamented the sin he had committed and the hurt it caused. This profound remorse was a sign that he sought not merely forgiveness but a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David prayed to God, “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He asked for an inner transformation that would keep him from repeating the same mistakes. Rather than merely asking for forgiveness, David begged God to purify him, saying, “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He recognized that only God could cleanse his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be spiritually renewed further reflects his genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) shows that he wanted to align with God in every part of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God put away David’s sin, the Lord struck the child and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God for the child’s life, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find a personal prayer of repentance and a public acknowledgment of his offenses. David’s decision to write his plea for mercy—which became one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible—shows his desire to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony of repentance and restoration for others, memorializing his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not merely about being sad; it was a deep transformation in his heart and life. Through confession, acceptance, and the heart-change inscribed in Psalm 51, David made his sin a lesson on God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibilities of restoration, making his story one of the most significant examples of true repentance in the Bible.
