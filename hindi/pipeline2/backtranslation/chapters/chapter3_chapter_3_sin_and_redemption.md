# Chapter 3 - Sin and Salvation

<!-- p1 -->
The Scriptures tell us many stories about sin-its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin is to fall short of God's standard, or to 'miss the mark,' an archery term that describes an archer failing to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old and New Testaments use a variety of words for sin, highlighting aspects such as its types, severity, disposition, victim, nature, and expected punishment. The Scriptures use several broad classifications to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against other people, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Deliberate sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- Momentary sin, habitual sin

<!-- p6 -->
- Sin for which a severe penalty such as death is expected, sin for which financial restitution is expected

<!-- p7 -->
Our modern criminal justice systems make similar distinctions: degrees of crimes, crimes that are premeditated or committed in passion, crimes that require financial restitution, or imprisonment, or even the death penalty. An act can be both a crime and a sin; but crime is a human classification, sin is God's.

<!-- p8 -->
There is a great deal of overlap among the Greek and Hebrew words used for sin. All sin is broadly placed under the umbrella of 'missing the mark,' in Greek ***Hamartia*** and in Hebrew ***Chata’***. This umbrella term is used for the most serious sins, such as adultery and murder, and also for less serious sins, such as showing favoritism to the rich in our church assemblies (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## A sacrifice is required for sin

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God kills animals and makes garments for them from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first instance of blood being shed to cover the consequence of sin. There are a few other similar instances in the book of Genesis, but God formally establishes the method of sacrifice for sin in the book of Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a common offering for atonement and dedication to God, often symbolizing total surrender. This offering addressed sin in a broad sense, covering both known and unknown sins. The procedure involved the sacrifice of a male animal without defect - typically a bull, sheep, goat, or bird. The offerer would lay his hand on the animal's head, symbolizing identification with the sacrifice, and then the entire animal would be burned on the altar. Burning the whole offering symbolized complete devotion to remove sin and to align one's life with God. The burnt offering underscored the seriousness of sin and the necessity of complete surrender, expressing reverence and repentance by giving something costly.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, called ***Minchah***, was not a direct offering for sin but a sacrifice of thanksgiving and dedication. This offering was given alongside other sacrifices, such as the burnt offering and the peace offering, and served as a means of expressing gratitude, worship, and devotion to God. Made by mixing fine flour with oil and frankincense, this offering was bloodless. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar as an offering to God, while the remainder was given to the priests for food. Though it was not specifically for sin, the grain offering was an important part of communal worship, symbolizing the offerer's devotion and gratitude for God's provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This offering often followed the sin and atonement sacrifices; once sin had been dealt with, it celebrated a restored relationship with God. The offerer could bring a male or female animal without defect, such as a cow, sheep, or goat. The procedure involved burning the fatty portions of the animal - the best parts - on the altar as God's portion, while the remaining meat was shared in a communal meal by the priests and the offerer. The peace offering was distinctive because it symbolized participation and fellowship, reflecting a sense of wholeness and unity with God. After atonement for sin, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God's acceptance and celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was specifically appointed for sins committed unintentionally or in ignorance. When an individual or the community realized that they had sinned unwittingly, they could offer a sin offering to receive purification and forgiveness. The type of animal to be offered depended on the status of the person involved: a priest or the congregation would offer a bull, a leader would offer a male goat, and an ordinary person would offer a female goat or a lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the sacrifice before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and if it was a communal sin, the remaining parts of the animal would be taken outside the camp and destroyed. This offering highlighted the seriousness even of unintentional sins and provided a means for people to remain clean and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins that involved a breach of trust, harming another, or the desecration of holy things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins that needed compensation, such as property-related offenses or violations against sacred things. For this offering, the sinner would bring an unblemished ram to be sacrificed. If the sin had resulted in damage to a person or property, the offender was required to return the property along with an additional 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering highlighted the importance of acknowledging one's guilt and taking concrete steps to make things right. It emphasized the relational and communal nature of sin, stressing that true atonement requires not only seeking God's forgiveness but also making things right with those affected by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Collective sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific offerings for individual sin, sacrifices and offerings were made daily, weekly, and monthly for the nation's collective sin, but the most important among them is **the Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16, designed to cleanse the entire Israelite community from their collective sins of the previous year. This distinctive ceremony occurred only once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, and addressed the sins of the nation rather than individual offenses. It served to purify the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people, and symbolized both atonement and purification for the whole community.

<!-- p79 -->
The central elements of the Day of Atonement were the **two goats** which represented both sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest would cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat, designated “for the Lord,” was sacrificed as a sin offering, and its blood was taken by the high priest into the Most Holy Place. There the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat - the lid of the ark of the covenant - symbolizing atonement for the people's sins and reconciling them to God. This act of sprinkling was of utmost importance, because it represented the purification of the holy place and ensured that God's presence remained with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, which was called the **scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest would lay his hands on that goat, confess the collective sins of Israel, and thus place them upon that animal. Then the goat was sent into the wilderness to die alone, which symbolized the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act was a powerful depiction of both atonement and deliverance from sin, showing that God not only forgave but also removed the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God's forgiveness and cleansing, allowing the nation each year to defer the consequence of sin and postpone judgment. This atonement did not permanently forgive the nation's sins, but rather delayed God's judgment.

## We have all sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible address the issue of sin in different ways. When Moses wrote the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he prescribed specific penalties for particular sins/offenses. I use the word offense interchangeably here, because the books of the Law were also the Israelites’ legal code at that time.

<!-- p89 -->
In the Prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in terms of the destiny of the entire nation. When the kings and the people disobeyed God, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, defeated in wars, and in some cases even taken into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs, the author writes as a loving father to his son, warning him about the personal dangers of sin and a lifestyle against God's commandments. In the Psalms, many of David's compositions deal with the personal and emotional consequences of sinning.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns about sin, calls people to personal repentance, and offers the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the apostle Paul presents a compelling argument for how God has planned to deal with sin, and how we can be freed from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is that we are all guilty of sin, and we must pay the price for our iniquities.

This false attempt to justify ourselves ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and against whom we have sinned, and it also ignores the reality of a holy and perfect God. Scripture says that if we deny that we sin, we are liars, and the truth is not in us!
