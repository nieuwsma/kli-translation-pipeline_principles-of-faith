# Chapter 4 - Jesus- the Messiah

<!-- p1 -->
Continuing our study of sin, we concluded that sin exacts a serious price, on those against whom sin is committed and who must be restored, and also on those who commit sin and must make restitution. The price of sin must be paid. In the Old Testament, God established a series of sacrifices that were performed by priests, individuals, and communities to address sin, to resolve it, and to render judgment concerning it।

<!-- p2 -->
Even after all the sacrifices made for sin, they were still not sufficient-this was not because God failed, but because people kept choosing sin। Although animal sacrifice could provide limited atonement, no matter how many animal sacrifices were offered, they could not change the sinful nature of humans।

<!-- p3 -->
God had a wonderful plan, <u>Messiah</u> (in Hebrew ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) also known as <u>Christ</u> (in Greek ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**), which means “Anointed One"; a person anointed for a special role (e.g., king, priest, or savior)।

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it is helpful to see what the people of His time expected from the Messiah। These expectations shaped how they saw Jesus and, in many cases, why they struggled to recognize Him as the fulfillment of God’s plan।

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical Context

<!-- p6 -->
The Jewish people had long been waiting for the coming Messiah। In the period just before Jesus, Jewish beliefs about the Messiah were shaped by Old Testament prophecies, whose interpretations were varied and not universally agreed upon। Most Jews expected that the Messiah would be a political and military leader who would deliver Israel from foreign oppression (primarily Roman rule) and restore the Davidic kingdom। Here is an overview of common messianic expectations and how they did (or did not) align with the Bible’s prophecies:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish Expectations about the Messiah before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A Political and Military Deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would be a king like David, who would lead Israel in a victorious revolt against foreign powers and restore Israel’s independence।

<!-- p10 -->
- **Referenced Scripture:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 9:6-7</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JER" data-chapter="23" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Jeremiah 23:5-6</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ZEC" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Zechariah 9:9-10</span>।

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical Context:** Under Roman rule, the Jews were oppressed and heavily taxed, which stirred hope for a Messiah who would overthrow Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom।

<!-- p12 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus is a king, but His kingdom is not of this world (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="36" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 18:36</span>)। He established a spiritual kingdom at His first coming, but He will return as a victorious king to judge in righteousness and reign, as described in the book of Revelation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 19:11-16</span>)।

<!-- p13 -->
### **2. Restorer of the Davidic Kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would rebuild the Davidic dynasty and bring a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace।

<!-- p15 -->

<!-- p17 -->
- **Truth:** As a descendant of David, Jesus fulfills this promise by establishing an eternal kingdom। Although this kingdom is spiritual in nature for now, it will culminate in a physical reign at His second coming (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="32-33" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:4-6</span>)।

### **3. A Prophet like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief:** Some Jews believed that the Messiah would be a prophet like Moses, who would speak directly with God and speak on His behalf।

<!-- p20 -->
- **Quoted Scripture:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="DEU" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Deuteronomy 18:15-19</span>.

<!-- p21 -->
- **Expectation:** Although many hoped for a prophetic figure, they often considered this role not central to the Messiah but an additional aspect.

<!-- p22 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus fulfilled this prophecy as the ultimate Prophet, speaking God's word and directly revealing the Father (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:18</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:10</span>). His prophetic role goes beyond Moses, for He mediates a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### **4. A leader who will bring justice**

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah will judge the wicked, uphold the righteous, and bring justice for the oppressed.

<!-- p25 -->
- **Quoted Scripture:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 11:3-5</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="72" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 72:1-4</span>।

<!-- p26 -->
- **Interpretation:** Justice was expected to be primarily political and social, not spiritual.

<!-- p27 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus brings ultimate justice. At His first coming, He provided spiritual salvation and justice through the cross (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Colossians 2:13-15</span>). At His second coming, He will judge the wicked and establish perfect justice forever (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>).

<!-- p28 -->
### Various Messianic Beliefs

<!-- p29 -->
Just as the Christian world today is divided into different denominations, sects, or belief systems, so too were the Jews of Jesus' era divided. In Jesus' time, four major Jewish sects shaped Israel's religious and political landscape, each with distinct beliefs and expectations about the Messiah. Understanding some background on these differing groups can provide meaningful context for the study of New Testament Scriptures.

<!-- p30 -->
**Pharisees** were the most influential among the common people, known for strict adherence to the Law (Torah) and oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, and emphasized personal piety and obedience to God's commandments. Their messianic expectation focused on a figure who would uphold the Law, bring justice, and restore Israel both politically and religiously.

<!-- p31 -->
**Sadducees**, by contrast, were a wealthy and politically powerful group who controlled the Temple and the priesthood. They rejected oral traditions and recognized only the Torah as authoritative Scripture. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, and instead focused on collaborating with Rome to maintain their power and ensure political stability. As a result, they placed very little emphasis on the Messiah, since their primary concern was preserving the status quo.

<!-- p32 -->
**Essenes** were a separatist group who withdrew from mainstream Jewish society and lived in isolated settlements such as Qumran. They were deeply focused on ritual purity, apocalyptic expectations, and a disciplined way of life. The Essenes believed in two messianic figures: a priestly Messiah who would restore the holiness of the Temple and a royal Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness. This two-Messiah concept reflected their hope for spiritual and political renewal. The Essenes are not mentioned explicitly in the Scriptures.

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, **Zealots** were armed nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent revolt. They rejected Roman authority and were committed to the restoration of Israel's sovereignty. Their messianic expectation was of a warrior-king Messiah, like David, who would lead them in battle against their oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p34 -->
Where these groups focused on worldly expectations, Jesus' mission reveals a far greater plan-that addresses not only political oppression but also humanity's spiritual bondage to sin.

## The Truth about the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### Jesus' Anointing

<!-- p37 -->
The Pharisees' and Zealots' messianic vision reflects a pattern seen repeatedly in human history- seeking salvation by earthly means while rejecting God's greater plan. As we see throughout the Scriptures, people often turn away from God- even though He is always faithful. Even when God remains faithful and receives us, He is often forgotten and rejected, giving rise to those cycles of disobedience and collapse that have plagued earthly kingdoms.

<!-- p38 -->
Although many expected a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a far greater purpose for His anointing- one that meets spiritual and eternal needs. If Jesus had come as a triumphant military leader according to the hopes and expectations of the Jews, it would have been only a matter of time before the kingdom fell due to human disobedience.

<!-- p39 -->
Instead, Jesus established a kingdom that cannot be shaken. His mission was rooted not in military power or political dominance, but in divine purpose. As the Anointed One, Jesus explained what He was anointed to do and how He would accomplish it:

In the time of Jesus, the presence of God on earth was mainly limited to the innermost part of the temple, the Most Holy Place। Here, behind the thick veil that separated the inner part from the Holy Place, the presence of God remained with the Ark of the Covenant। Man was separated from God, and God was apart from His creation।

God sent Jesus so that he would repair, rebuild, and restore humankind, and reconcile them to Himself। (2 Corinthians 5:17-21)

<!-- p60 -->
### The essential work of Jesus

<!-- p61 -->
To reconcile humanity fully to God, the Messiah’s mission required not only preaching and healing, but also the supreme work of atonement. Jesus had to become the perfect and complete sacrifice through His death on the cross.

<!-- p62 -->
The book of Hebrews, in its 13 chapters, explains in detail how Jesus became the perfect sacrifice, and was both the offering for sin and the High Priest who offered it (Hebrews 3 & 4). Jesus’ priesthood is not of the Levitical line (after the tribe of Levi), but in the order of Melchizedek, of whom it is said that he has neither beginning nor end, that is, he is not bound by human patterns and customs (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 7:3</span>). The limitation of the Levitical priesthood is that it relies on the blood of bulls and goats (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:12-13</span>), whose sacrifices are insufficient for the permanent and everlasting forgiveness of sins. From our study on sin and sacrifice we know that the consequences of people’s sins could be postponed for a year (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:3</span>) on the Day of Atonement, but this sacrifice had to be renewed again and again.

<!-- p63 -->
As Jesus died on the cross (the accounts are in Matthew 27, Mark 15, John 19, and Luke 23), He shed His blood and paid the price for the sins of the entire human race—past, present, and future. Isaiah 53 portrays Jesus as the suffering servant who took upon Himself the full weight of punishment and affliction. His once-for-all death became the final, supreme sacrifice. Jesus is the Lamb of God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="29" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:29</span>), without spot or wrinkle (sin); because He was perfect, complete, and holy, the shedding of His spotless blood makes atonement possible. **There is now neither need nor acceptability for further sacrifices. The former were only temporary—but Jesus’ was perfect and final.**

<!-- p64 -->
By His death and resurrection, we have a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6</span>), one that is established by the blood of God’s own Son; Jesus is the mediator of this covenant, the go-between. Through Christ’s sacrifice, we receive salvation, and He is the author of *‘eternal salvation to those who obey Him*’ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 5:9</span>).

<!-- p65 -->
In Greek, the word for salvation is **Sōtēria** (*so-tay-REE-ah - **G4991)**.* Its primary meaning is **salvation, deliverance, or preservation**. It encompasses the act of being saved, both in a spiritual sense (eternal salvation through Christ) and in physical contexts (rescue from danger or harm). The word conveys the notion of safety, wholeness, and restoration.

<!-- p66 -->
**Salvation or deliverance:**

<!-- p67 -->
- Denotes God’s deliverance of humanity from sin and its consequences through Jesus Christ.

<!-- p68 -->
- Used in contexts of eternal salvation (justification, sanctification, and glorification).

<!-- p69 -->
**Preservation:**

<!-- p70 -->
- Conveys the sense of being kept safe, rescued, or protected from danger.

<!-- p71 -->
**Wholeness or healing:**

<!-- p72 -->
- Includes restoration to a state of completeness or soundness.

<!-- p73 -->
Jesus’ sacrifice paid the price for our forgiveness and made a way for us to have a relationship with God, and established for us a path of remaining with Him that depends only on our obedience, not on our perfection or our ability to stay away from sin.

<!-- p74 -->
When we receive Jesus by faith (to be discussed further), we receive His salvation. This salvation connects us to God and writes our name in the ***Lamb’s Book of Life*** (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>). The Gospel of John says:

<!-- p75 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p76 -->
> **<u>John 3:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life.
>

<!-- p77 -->
</span>

<!-- p78 -->
To perish means to spoil—like a piece of fruit that goes bad before it can be enjoyed. It is a life that misses the purpose for which it was made. Jesus came to give us continuous, that is, eternal life, and to save us from perishing. Eternal life is not a life that begins after our death; it is the life that begins the very moment we receive Jesus as our salvation, when we are born again.
