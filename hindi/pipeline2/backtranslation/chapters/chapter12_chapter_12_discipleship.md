# Chapter 12 - Discipleship

<!-- p1 -->
Discipleship means following Jesus, learning from Him, and becoming more like Him in the way we live and love. It is a lifelong journey of obedience, transformation, and service, guided by the Holy Spirit and rooted in the Word of God.

<!-- p2 -->
In the Great Commission, discipleship is the primary command. Jesus defined this mission as follows:

<!-- p3 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p4 -->
> **<u>Matthew 28:18-20</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And Jesus came and spoke to them, "All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth.
>
> Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,
>
> teaching them to observe all things that I have commanded you; and lo, I am with you always, even to the end of the age." Amen.
>

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

<!-- p6 -->
We, His followers, are to *“Go and make disciples of all nations.”* Discipleship is about personal growth and about multiplying that growth by teaching others to follow Christ. Discipleship is the means by which we build the Kingdom of God; the teachings of Jesus are the message.

<!-- p7 -->
The call to be a disciple is more than merely going to church or memorizing Bible verses; it is an invitation to live like Jesus and to partner with Him in advancing the Kingdom of God. True discipleship helps believers become spiritually mature and effective for service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:13</span>). It shapes the essential mindsets and skills needed to serve others and share the gospel. As Paul instructed Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, discipleship follows a pattern of spiritual multiplication, where trained disciples continually raise up new followers so that they in turn lead others to Christ.

<!-- p8 -->
Discipleship is both a personal calling and a global mission. On a personal level, it involves growing in faith, developing a vibrant spiritual life, and being in relationship with others &amp; with God. At the same time, it fuels the church’s mission to reach every nation with the gospel, ensuring that the message of Christ continues to spread from generation to generation and transform lives.

<!-- p9 -->
## Why Discipleship Matters

<!-- p10 -->
The impact of discipleship goes beyond personal transformation; it has kingdom impact. Paul reminded Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> to entrust what he had learned to faithful people who would be able to teach others also. This generational pattern shows that discipleship is the engine that drives the church’s growth and stability. Discipleship is important because it is the very method Jesus chose to spread the gospel and build His church.

<!-- p11 -->
Rather than focusing on large crowds, Jesus intentionally invested in a small group of followers, taught them, modeled faithfulness, and prepared them to carry His mission forward. This personal, relational approach ensured that His message would multiply, because each disciple was equipped to make more disciples.

<!-- p12 -->
Essentially, discipleship is central to fulfilling the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). Jesus called us not merely to make converts but to make disciples—followers who are grounded in God’s Word, empowered by the Holy Spirit, and equipped for service. Through discipleship, believers grow in their knowledge of Scripture, develop spiritual disciplines, and learn to walk in obedience to Christ. This prepares the church to impact the world, advance the gospel, and reflect the glory of God before all nations.

<!-- p13 -->
### Nature versus Nurture

<!-- p14 -->
In the study of biology, psychology, and anthropology, we often consider the impact of nature versus nurture. The frequently asked question is: Is a person more the result of their inherent, built-in genetic inheritance? Or are they more the result of their environment and upbringing? The truth is that every person is a blend of nature and nurture. The powerful interaction of both elements governs the development of the human spirit.

<!-- p15 -->
When you accepted Jesus, the miracle of salvation caused you to be born again of incorruptible seed. You received a divine nature! Your spiritual DNA was rewritten! This divine nature produces a stirring in our hearts to draw near to our Heavenly Father, to know Him, to experience Him.

<!-- p16 -->
Just as a newborn child—whose wonderful, beautiful, unblemished nature is shaped by loving parents so that they can develop into a healthy and mature person—so we too must be shaped and nourished in order to grow.

<!-- p17 -->
Discipleship is the process of spiritual growth that builds upon our divine nature and makes us spiritually mature. By investing in discipleship, we ensure that the faith advances, leaders are prepared, and the church remains healthy and vibrant—both locally and globally.

<!-- p18 -->
### Didactic Model versus Apprenticeship Model

<!-- p19 -->
In modern Western education, the student’s primary relationship is with the content. A student’s learning centers on subject matter, materials, books, videos, assignments, etc. The transformation of education happens through the curriculum. This method of instruction is called the *didactic model*.

<!-- p20 -->
The paradigm of the didactic model has not always been the standard. The model and method used in Jesus' time was the *apprenticeship model*. This learning model emphasizes that knowledge is 'more absorbed than taught' and focuses more on the relationship between student and teacher than on the student and the curriculum.

<!-- p21 -->
Over roughly a millennium the didactic model gradually became the default method: the first medieval universities of Europe (11<sup>th</sup> - 13<sup>th</sup> centuries), the invention of the printing press in the 15<sup>th</sup> century, the Enlightenment of the 17<sup>th</sup> &amp; 18<sup>th</sup> centuries, and the impact of the Industrial Revolution on information and education in the 19<sup>th</sup> &amp; 20<sup>th</sup> centuries.

<!-- p22 -->
The advantage of the didactic model is that it can scale more efficiently than the apprenticeship model. Massive open online courses (MOOCs) can have thousands of students at the same time, whereas even a highly skilled master can probably handle only a few dozen apprentices at a time.

<!-- p23 -->
The didactic model results in the student gaining mastery of the subject matter, but the student does not become like the teacher. The teacher is a facilitator for the curriculum. The teacher imparts knowledge.

<!-- p24 -->
In the apprenticeship model the master shares more than knowledge; he imparts wisdom. The result of the apprenticeship model is that the disciple becomes so like the master that he can reproduce the master's work and take on apprentices of his own.

<!-- p25 -->
The didactic model also has its use and place in the kingdom of God; a good example is this book or any other Christian educational material. Jesus, as a rabbi, skilled and trained in the rabbinic method, practiced the apprenticeship model as the primary model for his disciples.

<!-- p26 -->
Jesus describes the apprenticeship model here:

<!-- p27 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="40" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p28 -->
> **<u>Luke 6:40</u>** (NKJV)
>
> A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone who is perfectly trained will be like his teacher.
>

<!-- p29 -->
</span>

<!-- p30 -->
In the didactic model the student becomes a carrier of knowledge; in the apprenticeship model, the student becomes like the teacher. Let us study the key words of that passage in Luke.

<!-- p31 -->
[See Table 6](../tables/table6.md)

<!-- p32 -->
In summary, the purpose and aim of the apprenticeship model used in discipleship are twofold:

<!-- p33 -->
**First, the disciple is fully trained**, like the teacher. This does not mean that the disciple imitates everything his teacher does. We are not being commanded to produce clones, but to raise up mature men and women who can take the message of Jesus to the ends of the earth.

<!-- p34 -->
**Second, the disciple now becomes a teacher for others**. The disciple must pass on what he has learned to someone else, otherwise the message of the kingdom will stop with him. This does not mean that his gifts will be exactly the same as his teacher's, or that he will enter full-time ministry, but it does mean that for his training to be complete he must reproduce.

<!-- p35 -->
Any creature that does not reproduce is barren, and its lineage is cut off. An animal that cannot reproduce is not healthy; likewise a church that cannot reproduce is also unhealthy.

## Jesus' model and process for discipleship

<!-- p37 -->
In the Gospels Jesus presented a mature and complete example of discipleship with his chosen disciples. We should broadly adopt these steps as the foundation for our own journey of becoming and making disciples.

<!-- p38 -->
The apprenticeship model has two roles: master and student. Every master was once a student, and it is hoped that every student will one day become a master. I will use the term *disciple* for the student and the term *disciple-maker* for the master/teacher.

<!-- p39 -->
Every discipleship relationship goes through an eight-stage progression. These stages, or steps, are not completely isolated from one another; often the relationship flows between these stages. In each stage there is a particular emphasis for the disciple, and for the disciple-maker as well. We will study the qualities of disciple-makers in depth later in this chapter.

<!-- p40 -->
The eight stages of discipleship are:

<!-- p41 -->
1.  Invitation/Call

<!-- p42 -->
2.  Teaching

<!-- p43 -->
3.  Modeling

<!-- p44 -->
4.  Participation/Attempt

<!-- p45 -->
5.  Correction

<!-- p46 -->
6.  Empowerment/Granting authority

<!-- p47 -->
7.  Multiplication

<!-- p48 -->
8.  Staying connected

<!-- p49 -->
### Invitation/Call

<!-- p50 -->
Discipleship begins with a personal invitation from Jesus to follow Him. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span>, Jesus called His first disciples and said, *“Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.”* This invitation was both relational and transformative—it demanded leaving the old way of life and entering a new purpose focused on God’s mission. Jesus’ call was not to remain spectators, but to participate in His service and the work of the Kingdom.

<!-- p51 -->
Modern disciple-makers should invite others into such authentic relationships that inspire spiritual growth and obedience. The invitation of discipleship is highly personal. Jesus meets individuals where they are, addressing their unique needs and circumstances, yet He also calls them deeper—to grow, to serve, and to become more like Him. This approach reflects the relational nature of discipleship, where the focus is not only on knowledge, but on a life-transforming relationship with Christ.

<!-- p52 -->
### Teaching

<!-- p53 -->
Teaching is central to discipleship. Jesus spent a large part of His ministry teaching the principles of the kingdom of God, especially in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7). His teachings provided the foundation for living a righteous life, loving others, and walking in God’s commandments. Even today, the teaching of God’s Word remains central to discipleship, because Scripture is the primary means for spiritual growth, correction, and training in righteousness (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p54 -->
Disciple-makers should be committed to faithfully teaching the Bible, helping others to understand it and apply it to their lives. Teaching is not limited to imparting knowledge—it demands modeling a Christlike life, so that disciples can see faith in action. Jesus lived the truths He taught, showing that effective teaching is both verbal and visible. Likewise, disciple-makers should strive to live out what they teach, providing practical examples of obedience and faithfulness.

<!-- p55 -->
### Modeling

<!-- p56 -->
Jesus not only taught His disciples—He also showed how to live out the truths He proclaimed. Jesus demonstrated in practical ways what it means to love, to serve, and to minister. Some examples from the Gospels are: washing the disciples’ feet as an act of humility and service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="13" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 13:12-15</span>), healing the sick, and feeding the hungry. Through His actions He modeled compassion, faith, and courage, leaving a clear example for His followers to imitate.

<!-- p57 -->
Discipleship requires not only saying, but also showing. Modern disciple-makers should be transparent about their faith journey, including struggles and victories, so that others can see how faith works in real life. Whether praying with someone in need, sharing testimonies, or serving the community—discipleship thrives when it is rooted in practical demonstration. Many times actions speak more effectively than words, making demonstration a powerful means of training others in the faith.

<!-- p58 -->
### Participation/Attempt

<!-- p59 -->
Jesus did not keep His disciples sitting on the sidelines—He actively involved them in ministry experiences. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 9:1-6</span> and <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 10:5-15</span>, Jesus sent the disciples to proclaim the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons. These practical opportunities enabled the disciples to learn by doing, and helped them gain confidence and skill in leadership.

<!-- p60 -->
Modern discipleship should also include practical participation. Disciple-makers should invite disciples to take part in ministry, whether by leading prayer groups, sharing testimonies, or serving in outreach programs. Providing safe spaces to practice helps disciples grow in courage while receiving support and encouragement. Learning is best when it is applied, and participation is the key to turning doctrine into practice.

<!-- p61 -->
### Correction

<!-- p62 -->
Growth often requires correction, and when the disciples misunderstood His teachings or failed in their actions, Jesus lovingly corrected them. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-37" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Mark 9:33-37</span>, He addressed their pride by teaching about servant-leadership. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="22" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="31-32" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 22:31-32</span>, He prayed for Peter, preparing him to recover from failure and to strengthen others.

<!-- p63 -->
Correction in discipleship should be grace-filled and constructive - rooted in Scripture and focused on restoration (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 4:2</span>). It is not about assigning blame, but about helping disciples grow and avoid stumbling. Disciple-makers should provide accountability, use God's Word to guide and encourage growth, while also modeling humility in receiving correction themselves.

<!-- p64 -->
### Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p65 -->
Jesus not only taught and corrected - He empowered His disciples to fulfill His mission. He gave them authority to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:18-20</span>). After His resurrection, He promised the Holy Spirit as the source of power for their ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>).

<!-- p66 -->
Modern discipleship should include empowering others to operate in their spiritual gifts and serve effectively. This includes identifying gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:11-13</span>), encouraging their use, and sending disciples into ministry. Empowerment also emphasizes dependence on the Holy Spirit for power, courage, and wisdom, ensuring that disciples are spiritually equipped for the work to which God has called them.

<!-- p67 -->
### Multiplication

<!-- p68 -->
The goal of discipleship is not merely personal growth - it is multiplication. Jesus modeled this by training disciples to make more disciples (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). After His resurrection, the apostles continued this mission of multiplication, spreading the gospel and establishing churches throughout the known world. Paul's instruction in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> reinforces this principle, urging Timothy to teach people who will in turn teach others.

<!-- p69 -->
Discipleship involves equipping disciples to make disciples, generating a chain-reaction of faith that expands God's kingdom. This process requires intentionality - teaching disciples how to guide, teach, and train others, while sending them with confidence and purpose (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 10:15</span>).

<!-- p70 -->
### Stay Connected

<!-- p71 -->
Discipleship does not end when someone is sent. Jesus modeled ongoing engagement with His disciples, continuing to encourage and instruct them even after His resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="21" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 21:15-19</span>). The early church maintained this pattern, gathering regularly for teaching, fellowship, and prayer (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>).

<!-- p72 -->
Modern discipleship should also foster ongoing relationships so that as disciples grow and lead they receive accountability, encouragement, and support. Maintaining unity in the body of Christ ensures that disciples are never isolated but remain strengthened and encouraged in their faith journey.

<!-- p73 -->
## Who does discipleship?

<!-- p74 -->
Every person in the body of Christ is responsible first to become a disciple themselves and then to contribute to the process of making disciples. There are varieties of gifts and functions, so our contributions may look different, but we are all responsible. In the local church, those most ultimately responsible for the state and health of discipleship are the leaders (**elders and deacons - <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 14:23</span>**), yet everyone plays their part.

<!-- p75 -->
Discipleship is a living, growing process - like nurturing a child to maturity. Healthy discipleship imparts strength and life to people. Just as parents strengthen and nourish a child, and in doing so make them healthy and mature, so disciple-makers help to nourish, strengthen, and mature disciples. The ultimate goal of discipleship is not the 'transfer of knowledge' but transformation.

<!-- p76 -->
We all are to be perfected (mature) and to make disciples. Our gifts will vary, yet there are some common characteristics for all disciple-makers.

<!-- p77 -->
### Characteristics of a Disciple-Maker

<!-- p78 -->
A disciple-maker is one who not only follows Christ but also remains committed to helping others grow in their faith. This role requires specific qualities that reflect both spiritual maturity and practical dedication. Disciple-makers must continually rely on God's power, wisdom, and grace to overcome the challenges that arise as they lead others in their spiritual journey.

<!-- p79 -->
#### Rooted in Christ

<!-- p80 -->
The foundation of a disciple-maker’s life must be deeply rooted in Christ. Jesus said, *“I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in Me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from Me you can do nothing”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="15" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 15:5</span>). Effective disciple-makers know that the ability to grow and to help others comes from staying close to Jesus every day. However, resistance to personal growth and spiritual transformation can hinder this dependence. Disciple-makers should continually cultivate their personal relationship with Jesus through prayer, the study of Scripture, and obedience, and model for those they are discipling what it means to abide in Christ.

<!-- p81 -->
#### Committed to the Mission

<!-- p82 -->
A disciple-maker must remain committed to the mission of making disciples given by Jesus in the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). This mission requires intentional focus and prioritization, recognizing that making disciples is not an optional task but the church’s core purpose. Yet challenges such as lack of time, busyness, and personal distractions can make it hard to stay focused. Disciple-makers should intentionally set aside time for mentoring, teaching, and relationship-building, viewing disciple-making not as an occasional activity but as a lifestyle (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 19:10</span>).

<!-- p83 -->
#### Spirit-led

<!-- p84 -->
Disciple-making is not possible by human strength alone; it requires the Holy Spirit’s guidance and empowerment. Jesus promised His disciples that when the Holy Spirit came upon them, they would receive power (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>), enabling them to be witnesses and servants of the gospel. Disciple-makers must be sensitive to the Spirit’s leading, relying on His wisdom to navigate complex situations, cultural barriers, and difficult conversations. This dependence on the Holy Spirit enables disciple-makers to act in love, courage, and spiritual discernment, so that their efforts are not self-directed but Spirit-directed.

<!-- p85 -->
#### Humble and Teachable

<!-- p86 -->
Humility is the mark of an effective disciple-maker. As Paul instructed, *“Do nothing out of selfish ambition or vain conceit. Rather, in humility value others above yourselves”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 2:3</span>). Disciple-makers recognize that they, too, are lifelong learners who must remain teachable and accountable. They welcome correction and feedback and model humility for those they mentor. Pride can become a major obstacle to growth for both disciple-makers and their disciples. Embracing accountability and maintaining a servant’s heart fosters trust and creates an environment for growth and transparency.

<!-- p87 -->
#### Patient and Persistent

<!-- p88 -->
Discipleship is hardly ever immediate—it is a long-term process that requires patience and perseverance. Paul reminds believers, *“Let us not grow weary while doing good, for in due season we shall reap if we do not lose heart”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:9</span>). Growth in faith is often accompanied by stumbles, questions, and setbacks; yet disciple-makers are called to walk alongside others, providing encouragement and support at every step. When progress seems slow it is easy to become discouraged, but disciple-makers trust that God is at work even when results are not immediately visible.

<!-- p89 -->
Being a disciple-maker requires a blend of spiritual dependence, commitment, and humility. It is a calling that demands patience and persistence, while remaining Spirit-led and teachable. Disciple-makers are not expected to be perfect, but they must be faithful—to abide in Christ, invest in others, and trust God to bring growth and transformation in His timing. These qualities reflect the love of Christ and help others become mature disciples who can, in turn, disciple others.

<!-- p90 -->
## Practical Tools for Discipleship

<!-- p91 -->
Effective discipleship requires intentional tools and practices that promote spiritual growth, accountability, and community. These means help disciples develop a strong foundation in the faith, grow in closeness with God, and become equipped to disciple others. Practical methods such as Bible study, prayer, small groups, and mentoring provide the structure and support necessary for ongoing growth.

<!-- p92 -->
### Bible Study

<!-- p93 -->
Engaging deeply with Scripture is the foundation of discipleship. Jesus emphasized the importance of God’s Word in spiritual growth, saying, *“Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:4</span>). Through regular Bible study and memorization, disciples internalize truth, confront falsehood, and apply God’s wisdom to daily life.

<!-- p94 -->
Disciples should be encouraged to immerse themselves in Scripture, not merely as an academic exercise, but to know God and have their hearts transformed (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="119" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="105" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 119:105</span>). Practical approaches such as reading plans, study guides, and group discussions provide opportunities to explore doctrine and discuss application in community. By developing regular study habits, disciples lay a solid foundation for spiritual maturity and fruitful service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p95 -->
### Prayer

<!-- p96 -->
Prayer is the lifeline of discipleship, connecting believers to God’s presence, power, and guidance. Jesus taught His disciples to pray in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 11:1-4</span>, emphasizing both dependence on God and spiritual alignment with His will. Through prayer, disciples learn to seek God’s wisdom, intercede for others, and receive strength for service.

<!-- p97 -->
Teaching disciples to pray—both personally and corporately—equips them to cultivate closeness with God and to discern His leading (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 4:6-7</span>). Incorporating practices such as prayer journals, prayer groups, and fasting fosters spiritual discipline and empowerment, helping disciples remain Spirit-led and mission-centered.

<!-- p98 -->
### Small Groups and Community

<!-- p99 -->
Discipleship thrives in community. The early church modeled relational discipleship through fellowship, prayer, and breaking bread together (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>). Small groups and accountability partnerships provide a safe space for disciples to grow together, share struggles, and celebrate victories.

<!-- p100 -->
The local church serves as the focal point for discipleship relationships, providing opportunities for group Bible studies, prayer meetings, and fellowship events. These gatherings allow disciples to practice love, bear one another’s burdens (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:2</span>), and encourage one another toward spiritual maturity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="24-25" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:24-25</span>). Small groups also provide a framework for the growth of leaders, as mature disciples train others and send them to lead groups and mentor new believers.

<!-- p101 -->
### Mentoring

<!-- p102 -->
Mentoring provides a personal and intentional approach to discipleship. Jesus modeled this by investing deeply in the twelve disciples, walking with them through teaching, correction, and encouragement. Similarly, one-on-one relationships allow disciple-makers to provide guidance tailored to individual needs, helping disciples grow in faith, character, and leadership.

<!-- p103 -->
Mentors serve as accountability partners, offering support, prayer, and wisdom during times of growth and struggle. Practical tools such as spiritual assessments, goal-setting, and regular check-ins ensure that disciples remain focused and motivated. Peer relationships within mentoring structures also promote mutual growth, as both mentor and disciple learn from shared experiences (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PRO" data-chapter="27" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Proverbs 27:17</span>).

## **Personal Reflection Questions**

<!-- p105 -->
1.  What does it personally mean for you to be a disciple of Jesus? How are you actively following him in your daily life?

<!-- p106 -->
2.  Reflect on Jesus’ model of discipleship. Which aspect of his approach—inviting, teaching, showing, or empowering—impacts you the most, and why?

<!-- p107 -->
3.  How can you multiply your faith by making disciples of others? What challenges or fears might hold you back, and how can you overcome them? (Note, I said multiply your faith, not your disciples!)

<!-- p108 -->
4.  Which qualities of a disciple-maker (e.g., patience, humility, relational focus) do you see in yourself? Which qualities do you feel motivated to develop further?

<!-- p109 -->
5.  Think about the people in your life. Whom might God be calling you to disciple? How can you take the first step toward building that relationship?
