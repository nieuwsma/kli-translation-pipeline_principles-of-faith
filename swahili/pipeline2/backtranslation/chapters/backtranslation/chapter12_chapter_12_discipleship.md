# Chapter 12 - Discipleship

<!-- p1 -->
Discipleship means following Jesus, learning from Him, and becoming more like Him in how we live and love. It involves a lifelong journey of obedience, transformation, and service, led by the Holy Spirit and rooted in the Word of God.

<!-- p2 -->
Discipleship is the primary command in the Great Commission. Jesus defined this mission in:

<!-- p3 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="28" data-verses="18-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">

<!-- p4 -->
> **<u>Matthew 28:18-20</u>** (NKJV) ***18** And Jesus came and spoke to them, saying, "All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth.* ***19** Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,* ***20** teaching them to observe all things that I have commanded you; and lo, I am with you always, even to the end of the age." Amen.*

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

<!-- p6 -->
We, His followers, are to *“go and make disciples of all nations.”* Discipleship is about personal growth and also multiplying that growth by teaching others to follow Christ. Discipleship is the vehicle by which we build the Kingdom of God; the teachings of Jesus are the message.

<!-- p7 -->
The call to be a disciple is more than attending church or learning Bible verses-it is an invitation to live like Jesus and to partner with Him in advancing God’s Kingdom. True discipleship helps believers grow spiritually and become effective for ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Ephesians" data-chapter="4" data-verses="13" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Ephesians 4:13</span>). It forms the heart and skills needed to serve others and share the Gospel. As Paul instructed Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2 Timothy" data-chapter="2" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, discipleship follows a pattern of spiritual multiplication, in which trained disciples continue to raise up new followers to lead others to Christ.

<!-- p8 -->
Discipleship is both a personal calling and a global mission. On a personal level, it involves growing in faith, cultivating a vibrant spiritual life, and being in relationship with others and with God. At the same time, it fuels the Church’s mission to reach every nation with the Gospel, ensuring that Christ’s message continues to spread and transform lives generation after generation.

<!-- p9 -->
## Why Discipleship Is Important

<!-- p10 -->
The impact of discipleship goes beyond personal transformation—it has Kingdom impact. Paul reminded Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2 Timothy" data-chapter="2" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 2:2</span> to entrust what he had learned to faithful people who would also teach others. This generational system shows how discipleship becomes the engine that drives the growth and resilience of the Church. Discipleship is important because it was Jesus’ chosen method for spreading the Gospel and building His Church.

<!-- p11 -->
Rather than focusing on large crowds, Jesus intentionally invested in a small group of followers, teaching them, modeling faithfulness, and preparing them to carry on His mission. This personal, relational approach ensured that His message would multiply, as each disciple was empowered to make more disciples.

<!-- p12 -->
At its core, discipleship is foundational to fulfilling the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="28" data-verses="19-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). Jesus did not call us merely to make converts but to make disciples—followers rooted in the Word of God, empowered by the Holy Spirit, and prepared for service. Through discipleship, believers grow in the knowledge of Scripture, develop spiritual disciplines, and learn to walk in obedience to Christ. It equips the Church to impact the world, advance the Gospel, and reflect the glory of God to all nations.

<!-- p13 -->
### Nature versus Nurture

<!-- p14 -->
In the fields of biology, psychology, and anthropology we often examine the effects of nature versus nurture. The frequently asked question: is a person more the product of their genetic inheritance within them? Or are they more the product of their environment and upbringing? The truth is that every person is a combination of nature and nurture. Both elements, in dynamic interaction, closely guide the development of the human person.

<!-- p15 -->
When you received Jesus, the miracle of salvation caused you to be born again of imperishable seed. You received a divine nature! Your spiritual DNA was rewritten! This divine nature produces an impulse in our hearts to be close to our heavenly Father, to know Him, and to experience Him.

<!-- p16 -->
Just like an infant, whose marvelous, good, and innocent nature should be shaped by loving parents so that he or she grows and matures in a healthy way, we too must be formed and nurtured in order to grow.

<!-- p17 -->
Discipleship is the process of spiritual growth that is built on our divine nature and brings us to spiritual maturity. By investing in discipleship, we ensure that the faith is passed on, leaders are raised up, and the Church remains healthy and vibrant—locally and globally.

<!-- p18 -->
### Didactic Model versus Apprenticeship Model

<!-- p19 -->
In modern Western education, the primary relationship a student has is with the content. A student’s learning focuses on content, materials, books, videos, assignments, etc. Educational change happens through the curriculum. This approach to teaching is known as the *didactic model*.

<!-- p20 -->
The concept of the didactic model has not always been the norm. The model and method used in the time of Jesus was the apprenticeship model. This learning model emphasizes that knowledge is received more than taught and focuses more on the relationship between the student and the teacher than between the student and the curriculum.

<!-- p21 -->
The didactic model gradually became the default approach for roughly a millennium, beginning with the first medieval universities in Europe in the 11<sup>th</sup> - 13<sup>th</sup> centuries, the invention of the printing press in the 15<sup>th</sup> century, the Enlightenment of the 17<sup>th</sup> & 18<sup>th</sup> centuries, and the effects of the Industrial Revolution on information and education in the 19<sup>th</sup> & 20<sup>th</sup> centuries.

<!-- p22 -->
The advantage of the didactic model is that it can scale more effectively than the apprenticeship model. Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) can have thousands of students at one time, whereas even a very skilled master can perhaps have only a few dozen apprentices at one time.

<!-- p23 -->
The outcome of the didactic model is that the student has mastered the content; however, the student does not become like the teacher. The teacher is a facilitator of the curriculum. The teacher imparts knowledge.

<!-- p24 -->
In the apprenticeship model the master shares more than knowledge; he instills wisdom. The outcome of the apprenticeship model is that the student has become like the master to the extent that the student can reproduce the master’s work and take on apprentices of their own.

<!-- p25 -->
The didactic model has its uses and place in the Kingdom of God; a good example is this book or any other Christian educational resource. Jesus, as a rabbi, skilled and trained in rabbinic methods, used the apprenticeship model as the primary model for His disciples.

<!-- p26 -->
Jesus describes the apprenticeship model in:

<!-- p27 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="Luke" data-chapter="6" data-verses="40" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">

<!-- p28 -->
> **<u>Luke 6:40</u>** (NKJV) *A <u>disciple</u> is not above his <u>teacher</u>, but everyone who is <u>perfectly trained</u> will be like his teacher.* (Emphasis added)

<!-- p29 -->
</span>

<!-- p30 -->
In the didactic model the student becomes a carrier of knowledge; in the apprenticeship model, the student becomes like the teacher. Let us examine the key words from that passage in Luke.

<!-- p31 -->
[See Table 6](../tables/table6.md)

<!-- p32 -->
In summary, the purpose and aim of the apprenticeship model used in discipleship are twofold:

<!-- p33 -->
**First, the student is perfectly trained**, like the teacher. This does not mean that the student imitates everything that their teacher does. We are not commanded to create clones, but to build men and women to maturity who can carry the message of Jesus to the ends of the earth.

<!-- p34 -->
**Second, the student now becomes a teacher to others**. The student must share with others what they have learned, otherwise the message of the kingdom ends with them. This does not mean that they must have the same gifts as their teacher, or that they will enter full-time ministry, but it means that they must reproduce so that their training is complete.

<!-- p35 -->
Any creature that does not reproduce is barren, and its lineage is cut off. An animal that cannot reproduce is not healthy; likewise a church that cannot reproduce is not healthy.

## Jesus’ Model and Process of Discipleship

<!-- p37 -->
Jesus demonstrated a mature and complete model of discipleship in the Gospels with the disciples He chose. We should broadly use these steps as the basis for our own journey of being and making disciples.

<!-- p38 -->
The apprenticeship model has two roles: master and student. Every master has once been a student, and the hope is that every student will become a master. I will use the term *student* to refer to the learner and *disciple-maker* to refer to the master/teacher.

<!-- p39 -->
Every discipleship relationship goes through a sequence of eight phases. The phases, or stages, are not entirely separate from one another; often the relationship flows between the phases. In each phase there is an emphasis for the student, and for the disciple-maker. We will study the qualities of disciple-makers in depth later in this chapter.

<!-- p40 -->
The eight steps of discipleship are:

<!-- p41 -->
1.  Call/Calling

<!-- p42 -->
2.  Teaching

<!-- p43 -->
3.  Showing

<!-- p44 -->
4.  Participation/Trying

<!-- p45 -->
5.  Correction

<!-- p46 -->
6.  Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p47 -->
7.  Multiplication

<!-- p48 -->
8.  Remain Connected

<!-- p49 -->
### Call/Calling

<!-- p50 -->
Discipleship begins with a personal invitation from Jesus to follow Him. In <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="4" data-verses="19" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 4:19</span>, Jesus called His first disciples, saying, *“Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.”* This invitation was relational and also life-changing-it required leaving old ways of life and entering into a new purpose centered on God’s mission. Jesus’ call was not to merely observe but to participate in His ministry and the work of the Kingdom.

<!-- p51 -->
Modern disciple-makers should invite others into genuine relationships that foster spiritual growth and obedience. The invitation to discipleship is deeply personal. Jesus meets people where they are, addressing their unique needs and circumstances, but He also calls them to go further-to grow, to serve, and to become more like Him. This pattern reflects the relational nature of discipleship, where the emphasis is not only on knowledge but on a life-transforming relationship with Christ.

<!-- p52 -->
### Teaching

<!-- p53 -->
Teaching is the core of discipleship. Jesus spent much of His ministry teaching the principles of the Kingdom of God, especially in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7). His teachings provided a foundation for righteous living, loving others, and walking in obedience to God. Training in the Word of God remains foundational in discipleship today, since Scripture is the primary instrument for spiritual growth, correction, and training in righteousness (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2 Timothy" data-chapter="3" data-verses="16-17" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p54 -->
Disciple-makers should be committed to faithfully teaching the Bible, helping others understand and apply it in their lives. Teaching involves more than delivering knowledge-it requires modeling a Christlike life so that disciples can see faith in action. Jesus put into practice the truths He taught, showing that effective teaching is both by words and by example. Likewise, disciple-makers should strive to live what they teach, providing practical examples of obedience and faithfulness.

<!-- p55 -->
### Showing

<!-- p56 -->
Jesus did not only teach His disciples- He showed how to live the truths He preached. Jesus demonstrated what it means to love, to serve, and to minister in practical ways. Several examples from the Gospels include Jesus washing the disciples’ feet as an act of humility and service (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="John" data-chapter="13" data-verses="12-15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 13:12-15</span>), healing the sick, and feeding the hungry. Through His actions, He set a pattern of compassion, faith, and courage, leaving a clear example for His followers to imitate.

<!-- p57 -->
Discipleship requires showing, not just telling. Modern disciple-makers should be transparent about their faith journey, including struggles and victories, so that others can see how faith works in real life. Whether it is praying with someone in need, sharing a testimony, or serving the community, discipleship thrives when it is grounded in practical demonstration. Actions often speak louder than words, making showing a powerful tool for teaching others in the faith.

<!-- p58 -->
### Participation/Trying

<!-- p59 -->
Jesus did not keep His disciples on the sidelines- He actively involved them in ministry experiences. In <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Luke" data-chapter="9" data-verses="1-6" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Luke 9:1-6</span> and <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="10" data-verses="5-15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 10:5-15</span>, Jesus sent the disciples to preach the Gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons. These hands-on opportunities allowed the disciples to learn by doing, gaining confidence and skill under His guidance.

<!-- p60 -->
Modern discipleship should also include practical participation. Disciple-makers must invite disciples to participate in ministry, whether by leading prayer groups, sharing testimonies, or serving in community outreach events. Providing safe places to practice enables disciples to grow in confidence while being supported and encouraged. Learning is most effective when applied, and participation is the key to turning theory into practice.

<!-- p61 -->
### Correction

<!-- p62 -->
Growth often requires correction, and Jesus lovingly corrected His disciples when they failed to understand His teachings or when they failed in their responsibilities. In <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Mark" data-chapter="9" data-verses="33-37" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Mark 9:33-37</span>, He addressed their pride by teaching them about servant leadership. In <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Luke" data-chapter="22" data-verses="31-32" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Luke 22:31-32</span>, He prayed for Peter, preparing him to return after failure and to strengthen others.

<!-- p63 -->
Correction in discipleship should be full of grace and constructive- rooted in Scripture and aimed at restoration (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2 Timothy" data-chapter="4" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 4:2</span>). It is not about judgment but about helping disciples grow and avoid pitfalls. Disciple-makers should provide accountability, using the Word of God to guide and encourage growth while showing humility in receiving correction themselves.

<!-- p64 -->
### Empowerment/Delegation of Authority

<!-- p65 -->
Jesus not only taught nor corrected- He empowered His disciples to carry out His mission. He gave them authority to preach the Gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="28" data-verses="18-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 28:18-20</span>). After His resurrection, He promised the Holy Spirit as the source of power for their ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Acts" data-chapter="1" data-verses="8" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 1:8</span>).

<!-- p66 -->
Modern discipleship should include empowering others to operate in their spiritual gifts and minister effectively. This involves recognizing gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Ephesians" data-chapter="4" data-verses="11-13" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Ephesians 4:11-13</span>), encouraging their use, and releasing disciples to minister. Empowerment also emphasizes relying on the Holy Spirit for power, boldness, and wisdom, ensuring disciples are spiritually prepared for the work that God has called them to do.

<!-- p67 -->
### Multiplication

<!-- p68 -->
The goal of discipleship is not just personal growth- it is multiplication. Jesus demonstrated this by teaching the disciples to make others into disciples (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="28" data-verses="19-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). After His resurrection, the apostles continued this mission of multiplication, spreading the Gospel and planting churches throughout the known world. Paul’s instructions in <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2 Timothy" data-chapter="2" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 2:2</span> affirm this principle, urging Timothy to teach others who will teach others.

<!-- p69 -->
Discipleship involves equipping disciples to make other disciples, thereby creating a chain of faith that expands the Kingdom of God. This process requires intentionality- teaching disciples how to nurture, teach, and sharpen others, while sending them out with confidence and purpose (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Romans" data-chapter="10" data-verses="15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Romans 10:15</span>).

<!-- p70 -->
### Stay Connected

<!-- p71 -->
Discipleship does not end when a person is sent out. Jesus modeled ongoing connection with His disciples, continuing to encourage and teach them even after His resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="John" data-chapter="21" data-verses="15-19" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 21:15-19</span>). The early church continued this pattern, gathering regularly for teaching, fellowship, and prayer (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Acts" data-chapter="2" data-verses="42-47" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 2:42-47</span>).

<!-- p72 -->
Modern discipleship should also cultivate ongoing relationships, providing accountability, encouragement, and support as disciples continue to grow and lead. Maintaining unity in the Body of Christ ensures that disciples are never isolated but continue to be strengthened and encouraged in their journey of faith.

<!-- p73 -->
## Who Does Discipleship?

<!-- p74 -->
Everyone in the body of Christ is responsible first to be a disciple, then to contribute to the process of making disciples. There is diversity of gifts and roles, so our contributions may look different, but we are all responsible. The people who are ultimately most responsible for the state and health of discipleship in the local church are the leaders (**elders and deacons - <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Acts" data-chapter="14" data-verses="23" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 14:23</span>**), but everyone has a part to play.

<!-- p75 -->
Discipleship is a living and growing process- like raising a child to maturity. Healthy discipleship brings strength and life to people. Like a parent who empowers and nourishes a child, and by doing so makes them healthy and mature, disciple-makers help to nurture, empower, and mature disciples. The ultimate goal of discipleship is not the ‘transfer of knowledge’, but transformation.

<!-- p76 -->
We must all be perfect (mature) and make disciples. Although we will have various gifts, there are common qualities for all disciple-makers.

<!-- p77 -->
### Qualities of a Disciple-Maker

<!-- p78 -->
A disciple-maker is someone who not only follows Christ but is also committed to helping others grow in their faith. This role requires specific qualities that reflect spiritual maturity as well as practical commitment. Disciple-makers must continue to rely on the strength, wisdom, and grace of God to overcome the challenges that come with leading others on their spiritual journeys.

<!-- p79 -->
#### Rooted in Christ

<!-- p80 -->
The foundation of a disciple maker’s life must be deeply rooted in Christ. Jesus said, “I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from me you can do nothing” (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="John" data-chapter="15" data-verses="5" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 15:5</span>). Effective disciple makers know that the ability to grow and to help others flows from staying close to Jesus each day. However, resistance to personal growth and spiritual change can hinder this dependence. Disciple makers must continue to cultivate their personal relationship with Jesus through prayer, Scripture study, and obedience, modeling for those they mentor what it means to abide in Christ.

<!-- p81 -->
#### Committed to the Mission

<!-- p82 -->
A disciple maker must remain committed to the mission of making disciples as Jesus commanded in the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="28" data-verses="19-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). This mission requires intentional focus and prioritization, recognizing that making disciples is not an optional task but the primary purpose of the church. However, challenges such as lack of time, busyness, and personal distractions can make it difficult to stay focused. Disciple makers must intentionally plan time to nurture, teach, and build relationships, viewing disciple making as a lifestyle rather than an occasional activity (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Luke" data-chapter="19" data-verses="10" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Luke 19:10</span>).

<!-- p83 -->
#### Led by the Spirit

<!-- p84 -->
Disciple making cannot be accomplished by human strength alone; it requires the guidance and empowerment of the Holy Spirit. Jesus promised his disciples that they would receive power when the Holy Spirit came upon them (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Acts" data-chapter="1" data-verses="8" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 1:8</span>, enabling them to be witnesses and servants of the Gospel. Disciple makers must be sensitive to the Spirit’s leading, relying on his wisdom to guide them through difficult situations, cultural barriers, and hard conversations. This dependence on the Holy Spirit enables disciple makers to act with love, courage, and spiritual discernment, ensuring their efforts are Spirit-led rather than self-driven.

<!-- p85 -->
#### Humble and Teachable

<!-- p86 -->
Humility is a hallmark of an effective disciple maker. As Paul instructed, “Do nothing out of rivalry or conceit. Rather, in humility value others above yourselves” (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Philippians" data-chapter="2" data-verses="3" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Philippians 2:3</span>). Disciple makers recognize that they too are lifelong learners and should remain teachable and accountable. They welcome correction and feedback, setting an example of humility for those they mentor. Pride can be a major obstacle to growth, both for disciple makers and for their disciples. Embracing accountability and maintaining a servant’s heart builds trust and creates an environment of growth and openness.

<!-- p87 -->
#### Patient and Resilient

<!-- p88 -->
Discipleship is rarely instantaneous—it is a long-term process that requires patience and endurance. Paul reminds believers “let us not grow weary in doing good, for in due time we will reap if we do not give up” (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Galatians" data-chapter="6" data-verses="9" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Galatians 6:9</span>). Growth in faith often involves setbacks, questions, and failures, but disciple makers are called to walk alongside others, offering comfort and support at every step. It is easy to become discouraged when progress seems slow, but disciple makers trust that God is at work, even when results are not immediately visible.

<!-- p89 -->
Being a disciple maker requires a blend of spiritual dependence, commitment, and humility. It is a calling that demands patience and endurance while remaining Spirit-led and teachable. Disciple makers are not expected to be perfect, but they should be faithful—abiding in Christ, investing in others, and trusting God to bring growth and transformation in his timing. These qualities reflect the love of Christ and help others become mature disciples who, in turn, can make other disciples.

<!-- p90 -->
## Practical Tools for Discipleship

<!-- p91 -->
Effective discipleship requires intentional tools and practices that foster spiritual growth, accountability, and fellowship. These tools help disciples build a firm foundation in the faith, grow in closeness to God, and be equipped to make other disciples. Practical approaches such as Bible study, prayer, small groups, and spiritual mentorship provide the structure and support needed for sustainable growth.

<!-- p92 -->
### Bible Study

<!-- p93 -->
Deep engagement with Scripture is foundational to discipleship. Jesus emphasized the importance of God’s Word in spiritual growth, saying that “man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God” (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="4" data-verses="4" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 4:4</span>). Regular Bible study and hiding Scripture in the heart enable disciples to internalize truth, confront lies, and apply God’s wisdom to everyday life.

<!-- p94 -->
Disciples should be encouraged to immerse themselves in Scripture, not merely as an academic exercise but in order to know God and have their hearts transformed (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Psalm" data-chapter="119" data-verses="105" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Psalm 119:105</span>). Practical methods, such as reading plans, study guides, and group discussions, provide opportunities to explore doctrines and discuss their application in fellowship. By building consistent reading habits, disciples lay a solid foundation for spiritual maturity and fruitful ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2 Timothy" data-chapter="3" data-verses="16-17" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p95 -->
### Prayer

<!-- p96 -->
Prayer is the lifeline of discipleship, connecting believers to God’s presence, power, and guidance. Jesus taught his disciples how to pray in <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Luke" data-chapter="11" data-verses="1-4" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Luke 11:1-4</span>, emphasizing dependence on God and spiritual alignment with his will. Through prayer, disciples learn to seek God’s wisdom, intercede for others, and receive strength for ministry.

<!-- p97 -->
Teaching disciples how to pray—privately and corporately—empowers them to cultivate closeness with God and recognize his leading (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Philippians" data-chapter="4" data-verses="6-7" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Philippians 4:6-7</span>). Incorporating prayer journals, prayer groups, and fasting practices fosters spiritual discipline and empowerment, ensuring that disciples remain Spirit-led and mission-oriented.

<!-- p98 -->
### Small Groups and Fellowship

<!-- p99 -->
Discipleship thrives in fellowship. The early church modeled relational discipleship through fellowship, prayer, and breaking bread together (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Acts" data-chapter="2" data-verses="42-47" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 2:42-47</span>). Small groups and accountability partnerships provide a safe environment for disciples to grow together, share struggles, and celebrate victories.

<!-- p100 -->
The local church functions as the primary hub of discipleship relationships, providing opportunities for group Bible studies, prayer meetings, and fellowship events. These gatherings enable disciples to practice love, bear one another’s burdens (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Galatians" data-chapter="6" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Galatians 6:2</span>), and encourage one another toward spiritual maturity (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Hebrews" data-chapter="10" data-verses="24-25" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Hebrews 10:24-25</span>). Small groups also provide a framework for multiplying leaders, as mature disciples train and send others to lead groups and nurture new believers.

<!-- p101 -->
### Mentoring

<!-- p102 -->
Mentoring offers a personal and intentional approach to discipleship. Jesus modeled this by investing deeply in the twelve disciples, walking with them through teaching, correction, and encouragement. Likewise, one-on-one relationships allow disciple-makers to tailor guidance to individual needs and help disciples grow in faith, character, and leadership.

<!-- p103 -->
Mentors serve as accountability partners, offering support, prayer, and wisdom during seasons of growth and struggle. Practical tools such as spiritual assessments, goal setting, and regular check-ins ensure that disciples remain focused and motivated. Peer relationships within mentoring structures also foster mutual growth, as both mentor and mentee learn from shared experiences (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Proverbs" data-chapter="27" data-verses="17" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Proverbs 27:17</span>).

## Group Discussion Questions (20-30 Minutes)

<!-- p111 -->
1.  **Icebreaker Question:** Who has had the greatest influence on your spiritual growth, and what did they do that impacted you?

<!-- p112 -->
2.  **Exploring Jesus’ Example:** Read <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="4" data-verses="19" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 4:19</span>. How can Jesus’ call to 'follow me' encapsulate the heart of discipleship? What does this look like today?

<!-- p113 -->
3.  **The Call to Make Disciples:** Discuss <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="28" data-verses="19-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 28:19-20</span>. Why is making disciples central to the mission of the church? How can we, as individuals, participate in this mission?

<!-- p114 -->
4.  **Overcoming Challenges:** What common obstacles are there in making disciples (e.g., time, fear, lack of confidence)? How can we help one another overcome these obstacles?

<!-- p115 -->
5.  **Practical Application:** What is one specific step you can take this week to invest in someone’s spiritual growth? How can we, as a group, encourage one another in this process?
