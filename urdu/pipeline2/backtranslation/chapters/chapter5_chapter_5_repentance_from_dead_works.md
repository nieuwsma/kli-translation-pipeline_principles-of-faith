# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
Often in Christianity we consider repentance to be merely a basic thing related to the beginning of our faith. The true spirit of repentance is far deeper than simply turning from sin to obtain salvation. Repentance from dead works means that our heart is turned away from the things that harm us or hinder our growth—and turned toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews placed ‘repentance from dead works’ as the first thing in the list of foundational doctrines after the elementary principles of Christ (Chapter 4).

### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Everyone who has ever held a job knows what work is. Work is the responsibilities you perform in order to receive wages so that you can earn a living (employment). Employment is a contract in which you give your skills and efforts in exchange for some valuable compensation that helps you make a living. You work because you get something out of it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work matters; it can give a sense of purpose, and often a sense of identity as well. It’s a very common question, ‘What do you do for a living?’ and the answer often begins with an identity statement, ‘I am.’ Although work can be enjoyable and meaningful, it is also very common for it to be physically or mentally strenuous, difficult, challenging, and stressful. If your job is none of these, you may find it very menial and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work a 40-hour job each week, and sleep 8 hours every night, you spend 36% of your waking hours at work, and that doesn’t include the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting, taking care of the house, and all the other necessary tasks you have to do to keep your life on track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? The reasons can vary, but by definition it’s because we gain some value from it.

<!-- p13 -->
Would you keep working at a job if they stopped paying you? For most of us, the easy—and painfully obvious—answer is a resounding, no!

<!-- p14 -->
Would you keep working at a job that was killing you? I suppose that depends on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
In the context of combining ***nekros*** with ***erga***, it means labor or work that is dead, that has no life, that cannot reproduce! The author of Hebrews is warning us against such dead work.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, some criteria are used to determine whether an organism is alive or not (this is a partial list for our purposes):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living things convert energy through metabolic processes in order to grow, develop, and sustain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and evolution**: Life includes growth and advancement over time, with a defined biological cycle and evolutionary changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living things possess the ability to reproduce, that is, to produce offspring or new units.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adapt itself according to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain their internal stability, such as regulation of temperature or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, organisms can adapt to their environment through genetic changes, making survival possible in different conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If, with a little creative freedom, we apply this criterion of ‘life’ to our actions, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, cannot produce any positive or desired outcome.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, the light of God’s presence, or His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Do not maintain internal stability, become disordered, lack self-control.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (are overly static) and no longer help our survival.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s desire for us. God wants to give us life!

<!-- p33 -->
Although living versus dead is a binary matter. Dying is a gradational state. Our dead works may not yet be completely dead in every category, but they will be! Dead works are like poisons; some have more severe effects.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison causes harm by preventing the body from functioning as it normally does. Some poisons have minor effects, like a headache. Some affect how we think, see, or even whether we can have offspring (live a fruitful life). Dead works are exactly like that. They may be only a headache, or they may cause death.

<!-- p35 -->
All actions which, after reflection, prove that they are not bringing the abundant life promised by Jesus are dead works. Having an illicit relationship outside of marriage is a very powerful poison; it is likely to very quickly have thorough and negative effects. Being needlessly stubborn and vindictive gradually poisons a person until he drives everyone away from himself.

<!-- p36 -->
To be freed from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
‘Repentance from dead works’ means that we turn to God and leave behind everything that is harming our way with Him-and this starts with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Continuing the analogy of poison for dead works, if you knew that your favorite food was filled with toxic heavy metals which, upon eating, would put you in a life-threatening condition, what would you do? We would, without hesitation, cast it away from ourselves, and never enjoy it again. Knowing that eating it is tantamount to our premature and likely painful death.

<!-- p40 -->
We should repent! We should turn away from those things in this life that are poisoning us and leading us toward both our physical and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to look a bit more deeply into the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
In Hebrews, the term *repentance* is used. It is contrasted with the term *to repent,* an example of which is found

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
To repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or event; it means to change one’s mind or will; to turn away from sin and turn to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is the state of having repented; it means a change of mind; the state of having turned away from sin and having turned to God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God in Hebrews says ‘*repentance from dead works*’ He is telling us to remain in the state of having repented! We are to live in a continual state of ‘having turned away’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times do we need to repent? Trick question! We need to keep changing our minds until we stop doing dead works. To repent is **not to ask for forgiveness, or to apologize**, although that may be included. To repent is to turn away from a former mindset.

### Systems of change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in changing your mind۔ If we do not even feel the need to change our mind، then why would we open our eyes to seek change؟ Then، returning to the poison analogy، if you cannot even perceive that the poison is killing you، then why would you stop using it؟

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that a person just has to reach ‘rock bottom’، then he will look at his life and decide to change۔ Experience and psychology tell us that we humans are more complex۔ In the study of psychology there are many theories related to behavior change، one highly credible theory is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** which was developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People can pass through a cycle of six stages for change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: There is no intention to change۔

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: There is an intention to change in the near future۔

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken toward change۔

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has been changed for a period of time۔

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has changed and is being consistently maintained۔

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: There is no desire to return to the previous behavior۔

<!-- p69 -->
There is no fixed limit for the duration of each stage۔ Some people, with respect to certain problems in their lives, never meaningfully move beyond these stages۔ In other cases، we can move very quickly from stage 1 to 6۔

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**، After yelling at his child, a father suddenly feels a sense of remorse۔

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He considers himself a *strict* but *loving* father۔ He believes that raising his voice is necessary۔ He is not considering change۔ Rather, he might even justify it - “My father raised me the same way۔”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day، after a long day at work، his restraint snaps and he yells at his young daughter more harshly than ever before، louder and with more anger۔ She is frightened۔ Her lips tremble۔ She quietly walks away from there۔ In that moment… something **changes**۔

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen stunned۔ The image of her sad face keeps playing in his mind again and again۔ He feels nauseated۔ He says to himself، *“I don’t want to become this kind of person.”* He knows he has to do something different -*now*۔

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes to her room، kneels، apologizes from the heart، and says، *“Papa was wrong۔ You did not deserve this treatment۔”* She hugs him۔ With a desire to solidify the change he prays and talks to a friend for support۔

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he starts reading parenting books to cultivate positive emotional self-control۔

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass۔ Yelling is no longer part of the routine۔ He is not perfect، but something really has changed۔ He no longer justifies the old behavior - and has no desire to return to it۔

### Helping others change

<!-- p79 -->
Much of our discussion has revolved around the process of repentance and its necessity from the perspective of the person who needs change۔ However، we will also encounter people who are in dire need of change, but are stuck in **Precontemplation**/**Contemplation** - blind to their need to change۔ We will also likely encounter people who are desperate for change, but do not know what to do (**Preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
When Jesus preached to the crowds, He often encountered people who did not know they needed change. We cannot force someone's mind to change, nor can we repent on their behalf. That is not our job, nor is it Jesus' example.

<!-- p81 -->

From experience and from Jesus' example, an encounter with truth filled with grace—sometimes gradual—can begin to open the eyes of the blind. Remember it is the Spirit's work to convict and to illuminate.

### Godly Sorrow and the Goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although hitting rock bottom is less a sure condition for change and more a myth, God can use sorrow to influence our minds. God will use sorrow to bring us to repentance. He will convict us for our actions and for the state of our lives and hearts, so that He may open our eyes and we may see our complete and total need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>a grief or distress experience that leads to a *metanoia*</u>, is not the same thing as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of grief or distress, but the world offers no hope—only the promise of relief and death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and mourning.

<!-- p98 -->
To suffer is to endure pain—physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual—especially when it is prolonged or severe.

<!-- p99 -->
Mourning is a multidimensional emotional response to a significant personal deprivation or loss, especially of a relational or existential nature.

<!-- p100 -->
Grief, pain, and sorrow are closely related ideas۔ In the human condition we will experience all three، we can learn and grow through all three، but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us۔

<!-- p101 -->
Holy Scripture says that every good and perfect gift is from above from God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>)۔ He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>)۔ Godly grief will create unease، but His discipline is fair and loving۔ Just as a good father will not deliberately break his child’s arm to ‘teach a lesson’، in the same way God will not impose sickness or illness on His children to teach us۔

<!-- p102 -->
### Example of Worldly Grief versus Godly Grief

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister۔ He was playing with a stick، and he deliberately hit her with it، which made her cry and left a red mark۔

<!-- p104 -->
Their father heard the commotion and investigated and when he found out what his son had done he took the stick, broke it in two, and threw it away۔ He sent his son inside to reflect on what he had done and comforted his daughter۔

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came inside to deal with his son he found him crying۔ He asked why he was crying، so the son said, “Because you broke my stick”۔

<!-- p106 -->
The father replied، “I am sorry that you are more concerned about your stick than about the sister you hurt” and sent him back to his room for further reflection۔

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later his son came back out and apologized۔ He said that he was still sad because Dad had broken his stick، and also because he had hit his sister۔

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example the boy experienced both kinds of grief: worldly grief over his lost stick، and finally godly grief for his injured sister۔

<!-- p110 -->
Godly grief and God’s goodness are interconnected۔ God is a good Father who lets us experience the consequences of our actions، yet when we go through those consequences He stands with us۔

<!-- p111 -->
God’s selfless goodness، that when we come to Him He is willing to redeem and restore us، is a positive factor on our path to repentance۔ His goodness is not that we remain engaged in sin، but that despite our sin He makes a way for us to be delivered and to experience His salvation۔

<!-- p114 -->
Man has a tremendous capacity for change؛ it is woven into our nature۔ Human beings are very adaptable؛ we can learn and grow، and find better ways of doing things۔ However، the capacity to change is not the same as the willingness to change or to be persuaded۔

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some people get stuck and why some are so ready to change، but I can say that the whole process of change is actually by the grace of God۔

### David’s Example of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps Scripture offers no richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin told in 2 Samuel 11-12. It begins with King David remaining in Jerusalem instead of going out to war, where he sees Bathsheba bathing. Even knowing that she was the wife of one of his loyal soldiers, Uriah, David sent for her, and they entered into an adulterous relationship, resulting in Bathsheba becoming pregnant.

<!-- p125 -->
To conceal his actions, David summoned Uriah back from the battlefield, hoping he would spend time with Bathsheba so that the child would appear to be his. But out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, Uriah refused to go home to Bathsheba. Then David arranged for Uriah to be sent to the front lines, where he was ultimately killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David hurriedly married Bathsheba to hide the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was displeased with David’s actions and sent the prophet Nathan to rebuke him. When the prophet confronted him, David immediately confessed, “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify or excuse his actions. This confession was the first step in his repentance, in which he acknowledged his guilt directly before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I acknowledge my transgressions, and my sin is always before me*,” show his complete responsibility for his actions. He expresses sorrow not only over the sin itself but also over having grieved God, saying, “*Against You, You only, have I sinned*.” David displayed a “*broken and contrite heart*,” which is a fundamental component of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he was deeply grieved over the sin he had committed and the harm it caused. The depth of this remorse signaled that he sought not merely forgiveness but a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God, “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He wanted an inner transformation that would keep him from repeating the same mistakes. Rather than merely asking for forgiveness, David begged God to cleanse him, saying, “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He knew that only God could purify his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be renewed spiritually reflects a genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways going forward. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) shows that he wanted to be aligned with God in every aspect of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God put away David’s sin, the Lord struck the child, and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God to spare the child’s life, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find a personal prayer of repentance and a public confession of his transgressions. David’s decision to put his plea for mercy into writing, which produced one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible, shows that he was willing to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony of repentance and restoration for others to learn from, memorializing his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance did not stop at feeling sorry; it was a profound transformation of his heart and life. Through confession, acceptance, and the heart-change recorded in Psalm 51, David turned his sin into a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most significant examples of genuine repentance in the Bible.
