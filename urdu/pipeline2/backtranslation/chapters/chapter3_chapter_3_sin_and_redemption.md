# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
The Holy Scriptures tell us many stories about sin-its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin is to fall short of God’s standard, or to ‘miss the mark,’ an archery term that describes an archer failing to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old Testament and the New Testament use different terms for sin, each highlighting aspects such as its type, seriousness, intent, offended party, nature, and prescribed punishment. To describe sin, the Scriptures use a few major categories:

<!-- p3 -->
- sin against God, sin against other people, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- deliberate sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- momentary sin, habitual sin

<!-- p6 -->
- sins that require severe punishment such as death, sins that require financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In our modern criminal justice systems, we have similar distinctions: classifications of crimes; crimes committed with premeditation or in the heat of passion; offenses that require financial restitution, or imprisonment, or even the death penalty. An act can be both a crime and a sin at the same time, but crime is a human classification, sin is God’s.

<!-- p8 -->
The various Greek and Hebrew words for sin have a great deal of overlap. All sin is broadly described under the umbrella term ‘missing the mark,’ ***Hamartia*** in Greek, and ***Chata’*** in Hebrew. This umbrella term is used for very serious sins—adultery, murder—as well as for relatively less serious sins, such as showing favoritism to the wealthy in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-verses="9" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">James 2:9</span>).

<!-- p9 -->

## Sin Demands Sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God slaughtered animals and made garments for them from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-verses="20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first instance of blood being shed to cover the consequences of sin. A few similar episodes appear in Genesis, but God formally instituted the sacrificial method for sin in the book of Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general sacrifice for atonement and consecration before God, often symbolizing total surrender. This offering addressed sin in a general sense, covering both known and unknown sins. In the rite, a male animal—typically a bull, sheep, goat, or bird—without blemish was sacrificed. The offerer laid a hand on the animal’s head, signifying identification with the sacrifice, and then the entire animal was burned on the altar. Burning the whole offering represented the removal of sin and complete consecration to align one’s life with God. The burnt offering highlighted the gravity of sin and the need for total commitment, expressing reverence and repentance through the presentation of something costly.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, called ***Minchah***, was an offering of thanksgiving and dedication rather than a direct offering for sin. It was presented alongside other offerings, such as the burnt and peace offerings, and served to express gratitude, worship, and dedication before God. Prepared by mixing fine flour with oil and frankincense, this offering contained no blood. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar as a gift to God, while the remainder was given to the priests as food. Although not specifically for sin, it was still an important part of corporate worship, symbolizing the offerer’s gratitude and devotion in reliance on God’s provision and generosity.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. It was usually offered after sin and atonement offerings, to celebrate a restored relationship with God once the matter of sin had been dealt with. The offerer could bring an unblemished male or female animal, such as a cow, sheep, or goat. In the ritual, the fatty portions—considered the best parts of the animal—were burned on the altar as God’s portion, while the remaining meat was shared in a communal meal between the priests and the offerer. This offering was unique in that it symbolized sharing and fellowship, expressing a sense of wholeness and unity with God. Once atonement had been made for sin, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and to celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was designated specifically for unintentional or inadvertent sins. When an individual or the community realized that they had sinned unintentionally, they could present a sin offering for cleansing and forgiveness. The type of animal presented varied according to the status of the person involved: a bull for a priest or the whole congregation, a male goat for a leader, and a female goat or lamb for an ordinary individual. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the sacrifice before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and, if it was a communal sin, the rest of the animal would be disposed of outside the camp. This offering underscored the seriousness of unintentional sins and provided a means for the people to remain clean and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins involving breach of trust, harm done to another, or desecration of sacred things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was typically connected to offenses where amends were necessary, such as property violations or profaning holy items. For this offering, the offender brought an unblemished ram. If the sin had harmed a person or property, the offender had to return what was taken and add 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering emphasized acknowledging one’s fault and taking practical steps to make amends. It highlighted the relational and communal nature of sin, stressing that true atonement involves not only seeking God’s forgiveness but also setting things right with those affected by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Corporate Sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to sacrifices designated for individual sin, there were also daily, weekly, and monthly offerings and vows for the nation’s corporate sin, but the most important of these was the **Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16, instituted to cleanse the corporate sins of the entire Israelite community from the previous year. This unique observance took place once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, and it atoned for the sins of the nation rather than individual lapses. Its purpose was to purify the tent of meeting, the priesthood, and the people, embodying both atonement and purification for the whole community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the center of the Day of Atonement were the **two goats**, representing both sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest would cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat, designated “for the Lord,” was slaughtered as a sin offering, and its blood was carried by the high priest into the Most Holy Place. There the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat—the cover of the ark of the covenant—as a sign of atonement for the people’s sins and their reconciliation with God. This act of sprinkling was of utmost importance, for it represented the purification of the sanctuary and assured that God’s presence would remain among His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The other goat, called the goat for Azazel (Azazel), would symbolically take upon itself the sins of the congregation. The high priest would lay his hands on this goat, confess the collective sins of Israel, and thus transfer those sins to the animal. Then the goat would be sent into the wilderness to die alone, which was a symbol of the complete removal of sin from the tabernacle. This outward act was a powerful example of both atonement and release from sin, showing that God not only forgives but also removes the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God’s forgiveness and purification, which allowed the nation to postpone the consequences of sin and to defer judgment each year. This **atonement did not permanently forgive** the nation’s sins, but rather postponed God’s judgment.

## We have all sinned

<!-- p86 -->
The books of the Bible address the problem of sin in different ways. When Moses recorded the law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he listed specific penalties for specific sins/crimes. Here I use the word "crime" synonymously, because the books of the law were the legal system of the children of Israel in that time.

<!-- p87 -->
In the Prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in terms of the fate of the whole nation. When the kings and the people disobeyed God, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, defeated in war, and in some cases taken into captivity.

<!-- p88 -->
In Proverbs, the author writes to his son like a compassionate father, warning him about the personal dangers of sin and a lifestyle against God’s commands. In the Psalms, many of David’s writings concern the personal and emotional consequences of committing sin.

<!-- p89 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns against sin, calls people to personal repentance, and offers the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the apostle Paul presents a great argument about the plan God made to deal with sin, and how we can be set free from its grip.

<!-- p90 -->
The fact is that we are all guilty of sin, and we must pay the price for our rebellion.

<!-- p91 -->

It can be easy for us to try to justify our actions and to say that we have not committed very serious sins such as murder or adultery and that we are generally moral and good people. This false attempt at self-justification overlooks the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and against whom we have sinned, and the reality of a holy and perfect God. The Word says that if we deny that we sin, we are liars and the truth is not in us!
