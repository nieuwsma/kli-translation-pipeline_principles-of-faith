# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
The Holy Scriptures tell us many stories about sin—its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin is to fall short of God's standard, or to “miss the mark”—an archery term that describes an archer failing to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old Testament and the New Testament use various terms for sin, highlighting aspects such as its kind, severity, intent, the offended party, nature, and the requisite penalty. The Scriptures mention a few broad categories to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against other people, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Intentional sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- Momentary sin, habitual sin

<!-- p6 -->
- Sins whose severe penalty is death, sins for which financial restitution is required

<!-- p7 -->
Modern criminal justice systems make similar distinctions: degrees of crime, crimes committed with premeditation or in the heat of passion, offenses that require financial restitution, or imprisonment, or even the death penalty. An act can be both a crime and a sin at the same time, but crime is a human classification; sin is God's.

<!-- p8 -->
The various Greek and Hebrew terms for sin overlap considerably. All sin is broadly described under “missing the mark,” ***Hamartia*** in Greek, and ***Chata’*** in Hebrew. This umbrella term is used for very serious sins—adultery, murder—as well as for less serious sins, such as showing favoritism to the rich in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## Sin Demands Sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God kills animals and makes garments for them from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first instance of blood being shed to cover the consequence of sin. There are a few other similar instances in Genesis, but God formally instituted a system of sacrifice for sin in the book of Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general sacrifice for atonement and dedication before God, often symbolizing complete surrender. This offering addressed sin in a broad sense, covering both known and unknown sins. The ritual involved offering a male animal without blemish - typically a bull, sheep, goat, or bird. The offerer would lay a hand on the animal’s head, signifying identification with the sacrifice, and then the whole animal would be burned on the altar. This act of completely burning the offering represented the removal of sin and the complete devotion to align one’s life with God. The burnt offering highlighted the seriousness of sin and the need for total consecration, demonstrating reverence and repentance by presenting something costly.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, called ***Minchah***, was an offering of thanksgiving and dedication rather than a direct sacrifice for sin. This offering accompanied other sacrifices, such as burnt and peace offerings, and was an expression of gratitude, worship, and devotion to God. Consisting of fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, the grain offering contained no blood. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar as an offering to God, while the remainder was given to the priests for food. Although it was not specifically for sin, the grain offering was an important part of corporate worship, symbolizing the offerer’s commitment and gratitude for God’s provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized reconciliation with God. This offering often followed sin and atonement offerings, to celebrate a restored relationship with God once sin had been dealt with. The offerer could bring an unblemished male or female animal, such as a cow, sheep, or goat. The ritual involved burning the fat portions - considered the best parts of the animal - on the altar as God’s portion, while the remaining meat was shared in a communal meal between the priests and the offerer. The peace offering was unique because it symbolized sharing and fellowship, reflecting a sense of wholeness and unity with God. After atonement for sin, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and to celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was designated for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When an individual or the community realized that they had sinned unintentionally, they could present a sin offering for purification and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the status of the person involved: a priest or the community offered a bull, a leader offered a male goat, and an ordinary person a female goat or a lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the offering before the LORD, burn the fat on the altar, and if it was a communal sin, the remainder of the animal was disposed of outside the camp. This offering highlighted the seriousness even of unintentional sins and provided a means for the people to remain clean and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, dealt with sins involving breach of trust, harming others, or desecrating sacred things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins that necessitated making amends, such as offenses related to property or violations of sacred objects. For this offering, the offender brought an unblemished ram for sacrifice. If the sin involved harm to a person or property, the guilty party also had to make restitution by returning the property and adding an additional 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering emphasized the importance of acknowledging one’s fault and taking practical steps to correct the wrong. It highlighted the relational and communal nature of sin, stressing that true atonement involves not only seeking God’s forgiveness but also making amends with those affected by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Corporate Sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific sacrifices for individual sin, there were also daily, weekly, and monthly sacrifices and offerings for the nation’s corporate sin, but the most important of these is the Day of Atonement.

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16, intended to cleanse the entire congregation of the children of Israel from the corporate sins of the previous year. This unique ceremony took place once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, and pertained to the sins of the nation rather than individual transgressions. It purified the tent of meeting, the priesthood, and the people, serving as a symbol of both atonement and purification for the whole congregation.

<!-- p79 -->
The central part of the Day of Atonement involved two goats, used to represent both sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest cast lots to determine each goat’s role. The first goat, designated “for the LORD,” was slaughtered as a sin offering, and the high priest took its blood into the Most Holy Place. There the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat - the cover of the ark of the covenant - as a sign of atonement for the people’s sins and their reconciliation with God. This act of sprinkling the blood was highly significant, because it represented the purification of the holy place and ensured that God’s presence remained with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, called the **scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the congregation. The high priest would lay his hands on this goat, confess the collective sins of Israel, and thus transfer them to the animal. Then the goat would be sent into the wilderness to die alone, which was a symbol of sin being completely removed from the tabernacle. This visible act was a powerful picture of both atonement and release from sin, showing that God not only forgives but also removes the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God's forgiveness and purification, allowing the nation to defer the consequences of sin and postpone judgment each year. This atonement did not permanently forgive the nation's sins, but rather delayed God's judgment.

## We Have All Sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible address the problem of sin in different ways. When Moses recorded the Law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he prescribed specific punishments for specific sins/crimes. I use the word "crime" interchangeably here, because the books of the Law served as a legal system for the Israelites at that time.

<!-- p89 -->
In the Prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in terms of the fate of the entire nation. When the king and the people disobeyed God, Israel was oppressed by its neighbors, defeated in war, and in some cases taken into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs, the author writes to his son like a loving father, warning him about the personal dangers of sin and a lifestyle against God's commands. In the Psalms, many of David's writings deal with the personal and emotional consequences of sin.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns against sin, calls people to personal repentance, and offers the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In the letter to the Romans, the apostle Paul explains in detail how God has planned to deal with sin, and how we can be set free from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is that we are all guilty of sin, and we will have to pay the price for our rebellion.

This false attempt at self-justification ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and sinned against, and the reality of a holy and perfect God. Scripture says that if we say that we do not sin, we are liars, and the truth is not in us!

From the first sacrifice in Genesis to the cross of Christ, God's plan has always been to redeem us and restore our relationship with Him. No sin is too great, and no distance too far, for God's grace to reach us.
