# Chapter 4 - Jesus - Messiah

<!-- p1 -->
Moving forward in our study of sin, we reached the conclusion that sin exacts a serious cost—on those against whom sin is committed and whose restoration is necessary, and on those who sin and are required to make restitution. The cost of sin must be paid. In the Old Testament, God appointed a series of sacrifices that were offered by priests, individuals, and communities to deal with sin, resolve it, and render judgment upon it.

<!-- p2 -->
Despite all the sacrifices for sin, they were not sufficient—not because God failed, but because people continued to choose sin. Although animal sacrifices could provide limited atonement, no amount of animal sacrifice was able to change the sinful nature of human beings.

<!-- p3 -->
God had a magnificent plan, namely the <u>Messiah</u> (in Hebrew, ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) also known as the <u>Christ</u> (in Greek, ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**) meaning “Anointed one"; someone who has been set apart for a particular role (e.g., a king, a priest, or a savior).

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it is helpful to see what the people of His time were expecting of the Messiah. These expectations shaped how they viewed Jesus and, in many cases, why they struggled to recognize Him as the fulfillment of God’s plan.

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical background

<!-- p6 -->
The Jewish people had long awaited the coming Messiah. In the period just prior to Jesus, Jewish ideas about the Messiah were shaped by Old Testament prophecies, whose interpretations varied and lacked universal agreement. Most Jews expected that the Messiah would be a political and military leader who would deliver Israel from foreign oppression (primarily Roman rule) and restore the Davidic kingdom. Here is an overview of common messianic expectations and how they did (or did not) align with biblical prophecy:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish expectations about the Messiah before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A political and military deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would be a king like David, who would lead Israel in a successful uprising against foreign powers and restore Israel’s freedom.

<!-- p10 -->
- **Scriptures cited:** <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="9" data-verses="6-7" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Isaiah 9:6-7</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JER" data-chapter="23" data-verses="5-6" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Jeremiah 23:5-6</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="ZEC" data-chapter="9" data-verses="9-10" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Zechariah 9:9-10</span>.

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical background:** Under Roman rule, the Jews were oppressed and burdened with heavy taxes, which gave rise to the hope that a Messiah would come who would topple Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p12 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus is a King, but His kingdom is not earthly (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="18" data-verses="36" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 18:36</span>). Through His first coming He established a spiritual kingdom, but He will return as a conquering King to judge and to reign in righteousness, as described in the book of Revelation (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="REV" data-chapter="19" data-verses="11-16" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Revelation 19:11-16</span>).

<!-- p13 -->
### **2. Restorer of the Davidic kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would reestablish the Davidic kingdom and inaugurate a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace.

<!-- p15 -->
- **Scriptures cited:** <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="7" data-verses="12-16" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Samuel 7:12-16</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="AMO" data-chapter="9" data-verses="11" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Amos 9:11</span>.

<!-- p16 -->
- **Interpretation:** The focus often remained on national restoration rather than spiritual renewal.

<!-- p17 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus, being of the lineage of David, fulfills this promise by establishing an eternal kingdom. Although this kingdom is presently spiritual in nature, it will culminate in a physical rule during His second coming (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-verses="32-33" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-verses="4-6" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Revelation 20:4-6</span>).

<!-- p18 -->
### **3. A prophet like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief:** Some Jews believed that the Messiah would be a prophet like Moses, who would speak directly with God and speak on His behalf.

<!-- p20 -->
- **Referenced verses:** <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="DEU" data-chapter="18" data-verses="15-19" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Deuteronomy 18:15-19</span>.

<!-- p21 -->
- **Expectation:** Although many people expected a prophetic figure, they often did not see this role as the Messiah’s central office but understood it as an additional aspect.

<!-- p22 -->
- **Reality:** Jesus fulfilled this prophecy as the final prophet, who speaks God’s word and directly reveals the Father (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-verses="18" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 1:18</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-verses="10" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 14:10</span>). His prophetic role surpasses that of Moses, because he is the mediator of a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-verses="6-7" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### **4. A leader who will bring justice**

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah will judge the wicked, uphold the righteous, and bring justice for the oppressed.

<!-- p25 -->
- **Referenced verses:** <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="11" data-verses="3-5" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Isaiah 11:3-5</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="72" data-verses="1-4" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Psalm 72:1-4</span>.

<!-- p26 -->
- **Interpretation:** Justice was understood primarily as political and social rather than spiritual.

<!-- p27 -->
- **Reality:** Jesus brings perfect justice. At his first coming he granted spiritual redemption and justice through the cross (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-verses="13-15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Colossians 2:13-15</span>). At his second coming he will execute judgment upon the wicked and establish perfect justice forever (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-verses="11-15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Revelation 20:11-15</span>).

<!-- p28 -->
### Diverse Messianic beliefs

<!-- p29 -->
Just as Christianity today is divided into various denominations, schools, or doctrinal frameworks, so too were the Jews of Jesus’s time divided. In Jesus’s day four major Jewish sects shaped Israel’s religious and political landscape, each with distinct beliefs and expectations about the Messiah. Some understanding of the background of these different groups can provide meaningful context for the study of the New Testament Scriptures.

<!-- p30 -->
**Pharisees** held the greatest influence among the common people and were known for strict adherence to the Law (Torah) and oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, and emphasized personal piety and obedience to God’s commandments. Their messianic expectation centered on a figure who would uphold the Law, bring justice, and restore Israel both politically and religiously.

<!-- p31 -->
In contrast, the **Sadducees** were a wealthy and politically influential group who controlled the Temple and the priesthood. They rejected oral traditions and recognized only the Torah as authoritative Scripture. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, and instead focused on maintaining their power and collaborating with Rome for political stability. Consequently they placed less emphasis on the Messiah, because their primary concern was preserving the status quo.

<!-- p32 -->
The **Essenes** were a separatist group who withdrew from mainstream Jewish society to live in isolated settlements such as Qumran. They placed strong emphasis on ritual purity, apocalyptic expectations, and an ordered way of life. The Essenes believed in two messianic figures: a priestly Messiah who would restore the sanctity of the Temple, and a royal Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness. This dual-Messiah concept reflected their hope for spiritual and political renewal. The Essenes are not mentioned directly in the Scriptures.

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, the **Zealots** were militant nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent revolt. They rejected Roman authority and were deeply committed to restoring Israel’s sovereignty. Their messianic expectation was of a warrior-king Messiah, like David, who would lead them in battle against their oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p34 -->
Though these groups were focused on earthly expectations, Jesus’s mission revealed a far greater plan—one that addressed not only political oppression but also humanity’s spiritual bondage to sin.

<!-- p35 -->
## The reality of the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### Jesus’s anointing

<!-- p37 -->
The Pharisaic and Zealot conception of the Messiah reflects a recurring pattern in human history—seeking salvation through earthly means while rejecting God’s greater plan. As we have seen throughout the Scriptures, people often turn away from God—even though he always remains faithful. Even when God remains faithful and receives us, he is often forgotten and rejected, giving rise to the cycles of disobedience and decline that have destroyed earthly kingdoms.

<!-- p38 -->
While many were expecting a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a far greater purpose for his anointing—one that meets spiritual and eternal needs. If Jesus had come as a conquering military leader, as the Jews hoped and expected, it would only have been a matter of time before the kingdom fell into corruption due to human disobedience.

<!-- p39 -->
Rather, Jesus established a kingdom that cannot be shaken. His mission was not based on military might or political dominance but on divine purpose. As the Anointed One, Jesus declared what he was anointed to do and how he would fulfill it:

<!-- p40 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="4" data-verses="16-21" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">

<!-- p41 -->
> **<u>Luke 4:16-21</u>** (NKJV)
>
>***16** So He came to Nazareth, where He had been brought up. And as His custom was, He went into the synagogue on the Sabbath day, and stood up to read.*
>
>***17** And He was handed the book of the prophet Isaiah. And when He had opened the book, He found the place where it was written:*
>
>***18**  "The Spirit of the Lord is upon Me, because He has anointed Me to preach the gospel to the poor; He has sent Me to heal the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives and recovery of sight to the blind, to set at liberty those who are oppressed;*
>
>***19**  to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord."*
>
>***20** Then He closed the book, and gave it back to the attendant and sat down. And the eyes of all who were in the synagogue were fixed on Him.*
>
>***21** And He began to say to them, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing."*

<!-- p42 -->
</span>

<!-- p43 -->
Jesus was anointed by God—set apart by God—and sent with a divine commission filled with purpose and power.

<!-- p44 -->
Jesus proclaimed:

<!-- p45 -->
- The good news of the Kingdom (Gospel).

<!-- p46 -->
- Release - pardon, forgiveness of sin, freedom.

<!-- p47 -->
- The acceptable year (season) of the Lord.

<!-- p48 -->
The people to whom He came were:

<!-- p49 -->
- Brokenhearted - shattered emotionally and spiritually.

<!-- p50 -->
- Blind - spiritually, and sometimes physically.

<!-- p51 -->
- Poor - needy, impoverished (spiritually and/or physically).

<!-- p52 -->
- Captives - meaning ‘taken at spear-point’ which represents those who are bound in slavery against their will.

<!-- p53 -->
Jesus transformed these broken people in this way:

<!-- p54 -->
- Restoration of sight

<!-- p55 -->
- Establishing in a state of freedom

<!-- p56 -->
- Binding up, healing, restoring

<!-- p57 -->
The purpose of Jesus’ service and ministry was both individual and global, both immediate and enduring. His purpose does not have a single focus but is part of God’s divine plan by which humanity may be reconciled to God. When sin entered the world, it created separation between God and man. God had to remove man from the garden and from His presence. Throughout the Old Testament, man could be with God, but only partially. It was very rare that the Spirit of God was given to people, and most people remained cut off from God. He did raise up a nation among the Jews, but He wanted a permanent, certain, and complete way to restore His relationship with humanity.

<!-- p58 -->
In the time of Jesus, God’s presence on earth was generally limited to the innermost part of the temple, the Holy of Holies. There, behind the thick veil that separated the inner part from the Holy Place, God’s presence rested with the Ark of the Covenant. Man was separated from God, and God was apart from His creation.

<!-- p59 -->
God sent Jesus to correct, rebuild, restore, and reconcile humanity to Himself. (2 Corinthians 5:17-21)

<!-- p60 -->
### The Essential Work of Jesus

<!-- p61 -->
To fully reconcile humanity to God, Christ’s mission required not only preaching and healing but also the final act of atonement. Jesus had to become the perfect and complete sacrifice through His death on the cross.

<!-- p62 -->
The book of Hebrews, in its 13 chapters, explains in detail how Jesus became the perfect sacrifice, and that He was both the sacrifice for sin and the High Priest who offered it (Hebrews 3 and 4). Jesus’ priesthood was not from the Levitical line (the tribe of Levi), but was like that of Melchizedek, about whom it is said that he has neither beginning nor end, that is, he is not bound by human ways and customs (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="7" data-verses="3" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Hebrews 7:3</span>). The limit of the Levitical priesthood is that it depends on the blood of bulls and goats (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-verses="12-13" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Hebrews 9:12-13</span>), whose sacrifices are insufficient for permanent and lasting forgiveness of sins. From our study of sin and sacrifice we know that the consequences of people’s sins could be deferred for a year (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-verses="3" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Hebrews 10:3</span>) during the Day of Atonement, but this sacrifice had to be repeated again and again.

<!-- p63 -->
By laying down His life on the cross (as recorded in Matthew 27, Mark 15, John 19, and Luke 23), Jesus shed His blood and paid the price for the sins of all humanity—past, present, and future. Isaiah 53 portrays Jesus as the Suffering Servant who bore the full weight of punishment and anguish Himself. His once-for-all death became the final, ultimate sacrifice. Jesus is the Lamb of God (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-verses="29" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 1:29</span>), without stain or wrinkle (sin); because He was perfect, complete, and holy, the shedding of His innocent blood makes atonement possible. **No further sacrifices are required or acceptable. The old sacrifices were only temporary—but Jesus’ sacrifice was complete and final.**

<!-- p64 -->
Through death and resurrection, we received a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-verses="6" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Hebrews 8:6</span>), established by the blood of God’s own Son. Jesus is the mediator, the reconciler, of this covenant. Through Christ’s sacrifice we are saved, and He is *‘eternal salvation for all who are obedient to Him’* (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="5" data-verses="9" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Hebrews 5:9</span>).

<!-- p65 -->
In Greek the word for salvation is **Sōtēria** (*so-tay-REE-ah - **G4991)**.* Primarily, it means **salvation, deliverance, or protection**. It encompasses the process of being saved, whether in a spiritual sense (eternal salvation through Christ) or in a physical context (rescue or preservation from danger or harm). The term conveys the notion of safety, completeness, and restoration.

<!-- p66 -->
**Salvation or deliverance:**

<!-- p67 -->
- Refers to God’s delivering humanity from sin and its consequences through Jesus Christ.

<!-- p68 -->
- Used in the context of eternal salvation (justification, sanctification, and glorification).

<!-- p69 -->
**Protection:**

<!-- p70 -->
- Conveys the sense of being kept safe, protected, or preserved from danger.

<!-- p71 -->
**Wholeness or healing:**

<!-- p72 -->
- Includes restoration to a state that is whole or sound.

<!-- p73 -->
Jesus’ sacrifice paid the price for our forgiveness and opened the way for us to have a relationship with God, and set for us a way of remaining with Him that depends on our obedience, not on our perfection or on our ability to refrain from sin.

<!-- p74 -->
When we accept Jesus by faith (which will be discussed later), we receive His salvation. This salvation connects us to God and records our name in the ***Lamb’s Book of Life*** (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-verses="11-15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Revelation 20:11-15</span>). The Gospel of John says:

<!-- p75 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-verses="16" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">

<!-- p76 -->
> **<u>John 3:16</u>** (NKJV) For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life.*

<!-- p77 -->
</span>

<!-- p78 -->
To perish means to be destroyed—just like fruit that goes bad before it can be used. It is a life that is deprived of its purpose. Jesus came to give us eternal—that is, everlasting—life and to save us from perishing. Eternal life is not a life that begins after death, but the life that begins at the very moment we accept Jesus as our salvation, when we are born again.

<!-- p79 -->
### Being Born Again

<!-- p80 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="5" data-verses="17" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">

<!-- p81 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 5:17</u>** (NKJV) *Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; old things have passed away; behold, all things have become new.*

<!-- p82 -->
</span>

<!-- p83 -->
When we accept Jesus, we become ‘born again.’ This phrase was used by Jesus to describe a perspective of transformation, where we are born of the Spirit (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-verses="1-15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 3:1-15</span>). This happens through faith—by choosing to trust Him completely, like entering into wedding vows, with both heart and mouth.

<!-- p84 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-verses="9-10" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">

<!-- p85 -->
> **<u>Romans 10:9-10</u>** (NKJV) ***9** If you confess with your mouth the Lord Jesus and believe in your heart that God has raised Him from the dead, you will be saved.* ***10** For with the heart one believes unto righteousness, and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation.*

<!-- p86 -->
</span>

<!-- p87 -->
When we were born on earth (born of flesh and water), many things took place: we took our first breaths and were declared alive, we were accepted by our parents, we were given a name, we became heirs, we received food and protection, and we continued to grow.

<!-- p88 -->
When we put our trust in Jesus and receive Him, we are born again, not of the flesh but of the Spirit. We become spiritually alive! Just as many things happen with physical birth, so too many things are involved with spiritual birth:

<!-- p89 -->
1.  When we accepted Jesus, God wiped away our sins and declared us forgiven (**justified**). He gave us a new beginning. The wall that stood between us and Him was removed. (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="TIT" data-chapter="3" data-verses="5" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Titus 3:5</span>)

<!-- p90 -->
2.  We were **born anew** spiritually and received the indwelling of the Holy Spirit. (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="1CO" data-chapter="6" data-verses="19" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">1 Corinthians 6:19</span>).

<!-- p91 -->
3.  We were **adopted** into God’s family and became heirs. (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="1" data-verses="5" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Ephesians 1:5</span>)

<!-- p92 -->
4.  We receive a source of spiritual nourishment and eternal protection. (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="1PE" data-chapter="2" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">1 Peter 2:2</span>)
