# Chapter 12 - Discipleship

<!-- p1 -->
Discipleship means following Jesus, learning from Him, and becoming like Him in how we live and love. It involves a lifelong journey of obedience, transformation, and service, guided by the Holy Spirit and rooted in God’s Word.

<!-- p2 -->
Discipleship is the central command in the Great Commission. Jesus defined this mission as follows:

<!-- p3 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-verses="18-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">

<!-- p4 -->
> **<u>Matthew 28:18-20</u>** (NKJV) ***18** And Jesus came to them and said, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me."* ***19** Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,* ***20** And teach them to observe all that I have commanded you; and behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the age." Amen.*

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

<!-- p6 -->
We, His followers, are to *“go and make disciples of all nations.”* Discipleship is both personal growth and the process of multiplying that growth, that is, teaching others to follow Christ. Discipleship is the means by which we build the kingdom of God; the teachings of Jesus are the message.

<!-- p7 -->
The call to become a disciple is not limited to merely attending church or learning Bible verses -it is an invitation to live like Jesus and, together with Him, to advance the kingdom of God. True discipleship helps believers become spiritually mature and effective for ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-verses="13" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Ephesians 4:13</span>). It shapes both the heart and the skills needed to serve others and share the gospel. As Paul instructed Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, discipleship follows a pattern of spiritual multiplication, where trained disciples keep raising up new followers to bring others to Christ.

<!-- p8 -->
Discipleship is both an individual calling and a global mission. On a personal level, it involves growing in faith, cultivating a vibrant spiritual life, and living in relationship with others and with God. At the same time, it strengthens the church’s mission to bring the gospel to every nation, so that the message of Christ spreads from generation to generation and lives continue to be transformed.

<!-- p9 -->
## Why Discipleship Matters

<!-- p10 -->
The impact of discipleship goes beyond personal transformation and also affects the kingdom. Paul reminded Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 2:2</span> that what he had learned he should entrust to faithful people who would then teach others also. This generational pattern shows that discipleship is the driving force that propels the church’s growth and sustainability. Discipleship is important because it was Jesus’ chosen method by which the gospel would spread and His church would be built.

<!-- p11 -->
Rather than focusing on large crowds, Jesus intentionally invested in a small group of followers, taught them, modeled faithfulness, and prepared them to carry His mission forward. This personal, relational approach ensured that His message would continue to multiply, because each disciple was equipped to make more disciples.

<!-- p12 -->
At its core, discipleship is central to fulfilling the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-verses="19-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). Jesus did not call us merely to make new converts but to make disciples—followers who are rooted in God’s Word, empowered by the Holy Spirit, and equipped for ministry. Through discipleship, believers grow in knowledge of the Word, develop spiritual practices, and learn to walk in obedience to Christ. It prepares the church to impact the world, advance the gospel, and reflect God’s glory among all nations.

<!-- p13 -->
### Nature vs. Nurture

<!-- p14 -->
In the study of biology, psychology, and anthropology, we often consider the effects of nature and nurture. The question is whether a person is more the result of their innate, inborn genetic heritage, or more the result of their environment and upbringing. The truth is that every person is a combination of nature and nurture. The strong interplay of both elements shapes the developmental trajectory of a human soul.

<!-- p15 -->
When you received Jesus, the miracle of salvation caused you to be born again from incorruptible seed. You received a divine nature! Your spiritual DNA was rewritten! This divine nature creates in our hearts a longing to be near our heavenly Father, to know Him, to experience Him.

<!-- p16 -->
Just as a newborn, whose wonderful, beautiful, innocent nature must be shaped by loving parents in order to become a healthy and mature person, so we too need to be formed and nurtured in order to thrive.

<!-- p17 -->
Discipleship is the process of spiritual growth that builds upon our divine nature and makes us spiritually mature. By investing in discipleship, we ensure that faith is passed on, leaders are developed, and the church remains healthy and vibrant—both locally and globally.

<!-- p18 -->
### Pedagogical Model vs. Discipleship Model

<!-- p19 -->
In modern Western education, the learner’s primary engagement is with content. The student’s learning focus is on content, books, videos, assignments, etc. Educational change occurs through curriculum. This method of teaching is called the pedagogical model.

<!-- p20 -->
The instructional model paradigm has not always been the prevailing way. In Jesus’ time, the model and method used was the *discipleship model.* This learning model emphasizes that knowledge is mostly ‘caught rather than taught’ and gives more attention to the student–teacher relationship than to the curriculum.

<!-- p21 -->
The instructional model gradually became the established approach over roughly a millennium, from the early medieval universities of Europe in the 11<sup>th</sup> - 13<sup>th</sup> centuries, to the invention of the printing press in the 15<sup>th</sup> century, the Enlightenment of the 17<sup>th</sup> & 18<sup>th</sup> centuries, and the impact of the Industrial Revolution on information and education in the 19<sup>th</sup> & 20<sup>th</sup> centuries.

<!-- p22 -->
The advantage of the instructional model is that it can scale more effectively than the discipleship model. Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) can host thousands of students at the same time, whereas even a highly skilled teacher can perhaps have only a few dozen disciples at once.

<!-- p23 -->
The result of the instructional model is that the student masters the material; however, the student does not become like the teacher. The teacher is a facilitator for the curriculum. The teacher transfers knowledge.

<!-- p24 -->
In the discipleship model the teacher does not merely share knowledge; he passes on wisdom. The result of the discipleship model is that the disciple becomes so much like the teacher that he can reproduce the teacher’s work and can also make his own disciples.

<!-- p25 -->
The instructional model has its usefulness and place in the kingdom of God; a good example is this book or any Christian educational material. Jesus, as a rabbi who was skilled and trained in the rabbinic method, primarily practiced the discipleship model with his disciples.

<!-- p26 -->
Jesus describes the discipleship model like this:

<!-- p27 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="6" data-verses="40" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">

<!-- p28 -->
> **<u>Luke 6:40</u>** (NKJV) *A <u>disciple</u> is not above his <u>teacher</u>, but everyone who is <u>perfectly trained</u> will be like his teacher.* (emphasis added)

<!-- p29 -->
</span>

<!-- p30 -->
In the instructional model the student becomes a bearer of knowledge, whereas in the discipleship model the student becomes like the teacher. Let us study the key terms of this passage in Luke.

<!-- p31 -->
[See Table 6](../tables/table6.md)

<!-- p32 -->
In summary, the purpose and aim of the discipleship model used in discipleship consist of two aspects:

<!-- p33 -->
**First, the disciple becomes perfectly trained**, like the teacher. This does not mean that the disciple imitates everything about his teacher. We are not commanded to produce identical replicas, but to develop mature men and women who can carry Jesus’ message to the ends of the earth.

<!-- p34 -->
**Second, the disciple now becomes a teacher for others**. The disciple should pass on what he has learned to someone else; otherwise the message of the kingdom ends with him. This does not mean that his gifts will necessarily be exactly like his teacher’s, or that he will enter full-time ministry, but it does mean that for his training to be complete he must raise up new disciples.

<!-- p35 -->
Every living thing that does not reproduce is barren, and its line is cut off. An animal that cannot reproduce is not healthy; likewise a church that cannot reproduce is also unhealthy.

## Jesus’ discipleship model and practice

<!-- p37 -->
Jesus presented a mature and complete example of discipleship with his chosen disciples in the Gospels. We should broadly use these stages as the basis for becoming and making disciples in our lives.

<!-- p38 -->
In the discipleship model there are two roles: teacher and disciple. Every teacher was once a disciple, and we hope that every disciple will become a teacher. I will use the term *disciple* for the learner and *disciple-maker* for the teacher/instructor.

<!-- p39 -->
Every discipleship relationship progresses through eight stages. The stages, or steps, are not completely distinct from one another; often the relationship moves back and forth among these stages. In each stage there is a particular emphasis for both the disciple and the disciple-maker. Later in this chapter we will study in detail the characteristics of disciple-makers.

<!-- p40 -->
The eight stages of discipleship are:

<!-- p41 -->
1.  Invitation/Call

<!-- p42 -->
2.  Teaching

<!-- p43 -->
3.  Demonstration

<!-- p44 -->
4.  Involvement/Attempt

<!-- p45 -->
5.  Correction

<!-- p46 -->
6.  Empowerment/Delegation of Authority

<!-- p47 -->
7.  Multiplication

<!-- p48 -->
8.  Staying Connected

<!-- p49 -->
### Invitation/Call

<!-- p50 -->
Discipleship begins with a personal invitation from Jesus to follow Him. In <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-verses="19" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 4:19</span>, Jesus called His first disciples and said, *“Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.”* This invitation was both relational and transformative—it required leaving behind old ways of life and stepping into a new purpose at the heart of God’s mission. Jesus’ call was not to become spectators but to participate in His ministry and the work of the kingdom.

<!-- p51 -->
Modern disciple-makers should invite others into genuine relationships that encourage spiritual growth and obedience. The call to discipleship is deeply personal. Jesus meets people where they are, taking into account their unique needs and circumstances; yet He also calls them to go deeper—to grow, to serve, and to become like Him. This approach reveals the relational nature of discipleship, where the focus is not merely on knowledge but on a life-transforming relationship with Christ.

<!-- p52 -->
### Teaching

<!-- p53 -->
Teaching lies at the heart of discipleship. Jesus devoted a large portion of His ministry to teaching the principles of the kingdom of God, especially in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7). His teachings provided the foundation for living righteously, loving others, and walking in obedience to God. Even today, teaching from God’s Word is central to discipleship, because Scripture is the primary means for spiritual growth, correction, and training in righteousness (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-verses="16-17" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p54 -->
Disciple-makers should commit to faithfully teaching the Bible and help others understand it and apply it to their lives. Teaching is not merely the transfer of knowledge—it requires modeling a Christlike life so that disciples can see faith in action. Jesus embodied the truths He taught, demonstrating that effective teaching is both spoken and visible. Likewise, disciple-makers should live what they teach, providing practical examples of obedience and faithfulness.

<!-- p55 -->
### Demonstration

<!-- p56 -->
Jesus not only taught His disciples—He also showed how to live out the truths He proclaimed. Jesus practically demonstrated what it means to love, to serve, and to carry out sacred ministry. A few examples in the Gospels include: Jesus washing the disciples’ feet, an act of humility and service (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="13" data-verses="12-15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 13:12-15</span>), healing the sick, and feeding the hungry. Through His actions He modeled compassion, faith, and courage, leaving a clear example for His followers to imitate.

<!-- p57 -->
For discipleship, it is not enough to tell; it is also necessary to show. Today’s disciple-makers should be transparent about their journey of faith—including both struggles and victories—so that others can see how faith works in real life. Whether praying with someone in need, sharing testimonies, or serving the community, discipleship flourishes when it is rooted in practical demonstration. Often actions speak louder than words; therefore, demonstration is a powerful means of training others in the faith.

<!-- p58 -->
### Involvement/Attempt

<!-- p59 -->
Jesus did not allow His disciples to remain on the sidelines—rather, He actively involved them in ministry experiences. In <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="9" data-verses="1-6" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Luke 9:1-6</span> and <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="10" data-verses="5-15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 10:5-15</span>, Jesus sent the disciples to proclaim the good news, heal the sick, and cast out demons. Such hands-on opportunities allowed the disciples to learn by doing, and under His guidance their confidence and skills increased.

<!-- p60 -->
Contemporary discipleship should also include practical involvement. Disciple-makers should invite disciples to take part in service, whether through leading a prayer group, sharing testimonies, or serving at outreach events. Providing safe opportunities for hands-on practice allows disciples to grow in boldness while receiving support and encouragement. The most effective learning happens when it is applied, and participation is the key to turning theory into practice.

<!-- p61 -->
### Correction

<!-- p62 -->
Growth often requires correction, and Jesus lovingly corrected His disciples when they misunderstood His teachings or failed in their actions. <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="9" data-verses="33-37" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Mark 9:33-37</span> He addressed their pride by teaching about servant leadership. <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="22" data-verses="31-32" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Luke 22:31-32</span> He prayed for Peter, preparing him to recover from failure and strengthen others.

<!-- p63 -->
In discipleship, correction should be grace-filled and constructive - rooted in Scripture and aimed at restoration (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="4" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 4:2</span>). It is not about rebuke but about helping disciples grow and avoid stumbling. Disciple-makers should provide accountability, guide using God’s Word, and encourage progress, while modeling humility in receiving correction themselves.

<!-- p64 -->
### Empowering/Authorizing

<!-- p65 -->
Jesus did not only teach and correct - He empowered His disciples to fulfill His commission. He gave them authority to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-verses="18-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 28:18-20</span>). After His resurrection He promised the Holy Spirit as the source of power for their ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-verses="8" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 1:8</span>).

<!-- p66 -->
Contemporary discipleship should include empowering others to operate in their spiritual gifts and serve effectively. This involves identifying gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-verses="11-13" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Ephesians 4:11-13</span>), encouraging their use, and sending disciples into ministry. Empowerment also emphasizes dependence on the Holy Spirit for power, boldness, and wisdom, so that disciples are spiritually equipped for the work to which God has called them.

<!-- p67 -->
### Multiplication

<!-- p68 -->
The goal of discipleship is not merely personal growth - it is multiplication. Jesus modeled this by training disciples to make more disciples (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-verses="19-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). After His resurrection the apostles continued this multiplying mission, spreading the gospel and establishing churches throughout the known world. <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 2:2</span> Paul’s instructions reinforce this principle, urging Timothy to teach those who will teach others.

<!-- p69 -->
Discipleship involves enabling disciples to make other disciples, creating a chain reaction of faith that expands God’s kingdom. This process requires intentionality - teaching disciples how to mentor, teach, and train others, while sending them with confidence and purpose (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-verses="15" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Romans 10:15</span>).

<!-- p70 -->
### Stay Connected

<!-- p71 -->
Discipleship does not end when someone is sent. Jesus modeled ongoing connection with His disciples, continuing to encourage and teach them even after His resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="21" data-verses="15-19" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 21:15-19</span>). The early church maintained this pattern as well, gathering regularly for teaching, fellowship, and prayer (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-verses="42-47" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 2:42-47</span>).

<!-- p72 -->
Contemporary discipleship should also cultivate ongoing relationships, so that as disciples continue to grow and lead they have accountability, encouragement, and support. Maintaining unity within the body of Christ ensures that disciples are never alone but remain strong and courageous in their journey of faith.

<!-- p73 -->
## Who does discipleship?

<!-- p74 -->
Every person in the body of Christ has the responsibility first to be a disciple themselves, and then to contribute to the process of making disciples. There is diversity in gifts and ministry roles, so our part may look different, but we are all responsible. In the local church the leaders are ultimately most responsible for the state and health of discipleship (**elders and servants/deacons - <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="14" data-verses="23" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 14:23</span>**), but everyone plays a part.

<!-- p75 -->
Discipleship is a living, growing process - much like shaping a child to maturity. Healthy discipleship brings strength and life to people. Just as parents strengthen and nourish a child and thus make them healthy and mature, so disciple-makers help to nourish, strengthen, and mature disciples. The ultimate goal of discipleship is not the ‘transfer of knowledge’ but transformation.

<!-- p76 -->
We all must become perfect (mature) and make disciples. Although our gifts will differ, all disciple-makers share certain common traits.

<!-- p77 -->
### The characteristics of a disciple-maker

<!-- p78 -->
A disciple-maker is one who not only follows Christ but is also committed to helping others grow in their faith. This role requires specific qualities that reflect both spiritual maturity and practical dedication. Disciple-makers must continually rely on God’s power, wisdom, and grace to overcome the challenges that come with guiding others on their spiritual journey.

<!-- p79 -->
#### Rooted in Christ

<!-- p80 -->
The foundation of a disciple maker’s life must be deeply rooted in Christ. Jesus said, *“I am the vine; you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="15" data-verses="5" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 15:5</span>). Effective disciple makers know that the ability to grow and to help others comes from staying close to Jesus every day. However, resistance to personal growth and spiritual transformation can hinder this dependence. Disciple makers must continually cultivate their personal fellowship with Jesus through prayer, the study of Scripture, and obedience, so that they can model abiding in Christ for those they are discipling.

<!-- p81 -->
#### Commitment to the Mission

<!-- p82 -->
A disciple maker must remain committed to the mission of making disciples as commanded in Jesus’ Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-verses="19-20" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). This mission requires intentional focus and priorities, recognizing that making disciples is not optional but the church’s central purpose. Even so, time constraints, busyness, and personal distractions can make it difficult to stay focused. Disciple makers should deliberately set aside time to mentor, teach, and build relationships, and view disciple making not as an occasional activity but as a way of life (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="19" data-verses="10" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Luke 19:10</span>).

<!-- p83 -->
#### Led by the Spirit

<!-- p84 -->
Making disciples is not possible by human strength alone; it requires the guidance and power of the Holy Spirit. Jesus promised His disciples that when the Holy Spirit came upon them, they would receive power (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-verses="8" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 1:8</span}), equipping them to be witnesses and ministers of the gospel. Disciple makers should remain sensitive to the leading of the Spirit, relying on His wisdom in complex situations, cultural barriers, and difficult conversations. This dependence on the Holy Spirit enables disciple makers to walk in love, boldness, and spiritual discernment, so that their efforts are not their own but are in step with the Spirit’s guidance.

<!-- p85 -->
#### Humble and Teachable

<!-- p86 -->
Humility is the mark of an effective disciple maker. As Paul instructed, *“Let nothing be done through selfish ambition or conceit, but in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than himself”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="2" data-verses="3" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Philippians 2:3</span>). Disciple makers acknowledge that they too are lifelong learners who must remain teachable and accountable. They welcome correction and feedback, and model humility for those they lead. Pride can become a major obstacle to growth, both for disciple makers and for disciples. Embracing accountability and maintaining a servant’s heart fosters trust and creates an environment of growth and transparency.

<!-- p87 -->
#### Patient and Steadfast

<!-- p88 -->
Discipleship is rarely immediate-it is a long-term process that requires patience and perseverance. Paul reminds believers, *“Let us not grow weary while doing good, for in due season we shall reap if we do not lose heart”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-verses="9" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Galatians 6:9</span>). Growth in faith often involves setbacks, questions, and failures, yet disciple makers are called to walk alongside others step by step, offering encouragement and support at every stage. When progress seems slow, it is easy to become discouraged, but disciple makers trust that God is at work, even when the results are not immediately visible.

<!-- p89 -->
Being a disciple maker requires a combination of spiritual dependence, commitment, and humility. It is a calling that demands patience and perseverance, while also remaining Spirit-led and teachable. Disciple makers are not expected to be perfect, but they are to be faithful-abiding in Christ, investing in others, and trusting God to bring growth and transformation in His time. These qualities reflect the love of Christ and help others become mature disciples who can, in turn, disciple others.

## Practical Resources for Discipleship

<!-- p91 -->
Effective discipleship requires intentional resources and methods that foster spiritual growth, accountability, and community. These resources help disciples build a strong foundation of faith, grow in closeness with God, and be equipped to disciple others. Practical approaches such as studying Scripture, prayer, small groups, and mentoring provide structure and support for ongoing growth.

<!-- p92 -->
### Study of Scripture

<!-- p93 -->
Deep engagement with the Word is the foundation of discipleship. Emphasizing the importance of God’s Word for spiritual growth, Jesus said, *“Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-verses="4" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 4:4</span>). Regular Bible study and Scripture memorization enable disciples to internalize truth, counter false ideas, and apply God’s wisdom in daily life.

<!-- p94 -->
Disciples should be encouraged to immerse themselves in Scripture, not merely as an academic exercise but to know God and have their hearts transformed (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="119" data-verses="105" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Psalm 119:105</span>). Practical methods such as study plans, guidebooks, and group discussions provide opportunities to explore doctrine and discuss application in community. By establishing regular study habits, disciples lay a strong foundation for spiritual maturity and fruitful ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-verses="16-17" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p95 -->
### Prayer

<!-- p96 -->
Prayer is the lifeline of the discipleship life, connecting believers to God’s presence, power, and guidance. Jesus taught His disciples to pray in <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="11" data-verses="1-4" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Luke 11:1-4</span>, emphasizing dependence on God and spiritual alignment with His will. Through prayer, disciples learn to seek God’s wisdom, intercede for others, and receive strength for ministry.

<!-- p97 -->
Teaching disciples to pray both individually and corporately—equips them to cultivate intimacy with God and discern His guidance (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="4" data-verses="6-7" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Philippians 4:6-7</span>). Prayer journals, prayer groups, and the practice of fasting cultivate spiritual discipline and vitality, ensuring that disciples remain Spirit-led and mission-focused.

<!-- p98 -->
### Small Groups and Community

<!-- p99 -->
Discipleship thrives in community. The early church modeled communal discipleship through fellowship, prayer, and the breaking of bread together (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-verses="42-47" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Acts 2:42-47</span>). Small groups and accountability partnerships provide disciples with a safe space to grow together, share their struggles, and celebrate victories.

<!-- p100 -->
The local church serves as the central hub for discipleship relationships, providing opportunities for group Bible studies, prayer gatherings, and fellowship events. These gatherings give disciples the opportunity to put love into practice, to bear one another’s burdens (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-verses="2" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Galatians 6:2</span>), and to encourage one another toward spiritual maturity (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-verses="24-25" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Hebrews 10:24-25</span>). Small groups also provide a framework for developing leaders, as mature disciples train and send others to lead groups and mentor new believers.

<!-- p101 -->
### Mentorship

<!-- p102 -->
Mentorship provides a personal and intentional approach to discipleship. Jesus set the example by investing deeply in the twelve disciples, walking with them through teaching, correction, and encouragement. Likewise, one-to-one relationships allow disciple-makers to tailor guidance to the individual’s needs, enabling disciples to grow in faith, character, and leadership.

<!-- p103 -->
Mentors serve as accountability partners, providing support, prayer, and wisdom during times of growth and struggle. Practical tools such as spiritual assessments, goal setting, and regular reviews help ensure that disciples remain focused and committed. Peer relationships within mentoring structures also foster mutual growth, as both mentor and disciple learn from shared experiences (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="PRO" data-chapter="27" data-verses="17" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Proverbs 27:17</span>).

5.  Think about the people in your life. Whom might God be calling you to disciple? How can you take the first step in forming that relationship?
