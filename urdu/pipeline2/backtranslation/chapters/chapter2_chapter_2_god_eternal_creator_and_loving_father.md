# Chapter 2 - God-Eternal Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is essential that we establish a firm foundation in the knowledge of who God is. Through study we can understand:

<!-- p2 -->
- His desires, intentions, and actions,

<!-- p3 -->
- His nature, character, and attributes,

<!-- p4 -->
- What is said about Him and what He says about Himself.

<!-- p5 -->
Scripture uniquely reveals God’s characteristics, and each facet deepens our understanding of the unchanging, eternal God.

<!-- p6 -->
It is difficult, if not impossible, to reduce God to a single word, for He is so vast and multidimensional that He cannot be simplified. Although we can and will make great efforts to explain, describe, and portray God, ultimately, like art and music, He must be experienced to be understood.

<!-- p7 -->
When one listens to music, one describes it by how it affects them-how it resonates within, going beyond mere notes and rhythms. A song may carry warmth or intensity, with a throbbing beat like a heartbeat, or with rising swells that evoke a sense of ecstasy or yearning. Music sometimes soothes like a gentle wave or electrifies like a storm, lifting the listener’s spirit, steadying their thoughts, or transporting them to an entirely different place. The experience is far more than a sequence of sounds; it is the soul of the music that lingers, reflected in how it shapes the listener’s emotions and personal journey.

<!-- p8 -->
Similarly, the viewer of a painting looks beyond colors or brushstrokes, examining what these elements embody. A landscape may evoke a sense of peaceful escape, while an abstract creation may stir curiosity or introspection. Certain colors can bring warmth, while a composition can create a sense of calm, disarray, or beauty. The painting becomes a living experience, a response that shifts perspective or births a deep emotional state that words alone cannot contain. Thus, both music and art transcend their physical forms-they become encounters that touch something essential, transforming the listener or viewer in a profound and personal way.

## Exploring God through the Old Testament

<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: The God who dwells with His creation

<!-- p11 -->
A central theme in God’s story is His desire to dwell with His creation. This is evident in the narratives of Genesis. Its fifty chapters and the genealogies from Adam (the first human) to Joseph (the final major figure in the book of Genesis, the last significant leader of God’s people before Moses) span approximately ~2,400 years. In these stories we see the constancy of God’s goodness despite human wickedness.

<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God establishes relationships with a few chosen individuals.

<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - The first humans, whom God created and placed in the Garden of Eden. God walked and conversed with them, giving them instruction and establishing a relationship of mutual love and obedience. However, when they ate from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, their disobedience affected that relationship (Genesis 1-3).

<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - Although Cain ultimately rebelled by killing his brother Abel, God still spoke to him directly, warning him of the danger of sin and giving him guidance before the murder. Even after Cain’s punishment, God placed a mark on him to protect him from harm (Genesis 4).

<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - God formed a unique relationship with Noah, a righteous man in a time when corruption was widespread. God commanded Noah to build an ark to save his family and the animals from the flood. Afterwards, God made a covenant with Noah, promising never again to destroy the earth by a flood, and set the rainbow as the sign of that covenant (Genesis 6-9).

<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - One of the most important relationships in Genesis; God called Abram to leave his homeland and promised to make him the father of a great nation. God made a covenant with Abram, promising him land, offspring, and blessings for all nations through him. Abram’s faith and obedience become central themes, and as part of the covenant his name is changed to Abraham (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22).

<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - An Egyptian servant of Sarah, who encountered God in the wilderness after mistreatment. God comforted her, promised a future for her son Ishmael, and directed her to return to Abraham and Sarah. Hagar calls God “El Roi” (“the God who sees me”), acknowledging His care for her (Genesis 16).

<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - The son of Abraham and Sarah, who inherits the covenant promises made to his father. God confirms those promises directly to Isaac, including the promise of descendants and blessing. God guides Isaac during famine, protects him, and upholds the covenant relationship (Genesis 26).

<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - Although her relationship with God is indirect, God answers Rebekah’s prayer about her difficult pregnancy and reveals that her twin sons, Jacob and Esau, will become two nations with destinies that differ from one another (Genesis 25).

<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God establishes a personal relationship with Jacob, appearing to him in a dream and confirming the covenant promises of land, offspring, and blessings. Jacob met God multiple times, including the famous wrestling match where God changed his name to Israel, which is a sign of his transformation and of Israel being established as a nation (Genesis 28, 32, 35).

<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Although Joseph’s relationship with God is not based on direct appearances or covenants like Abraham’s or Jacob’s, God is deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, divine favor, and divine providence, God guides Joseph’s journey, from being sold into slavery to becoming a powerful leader in Egypt. Joseph’s faith in God’s sovereignty enables him to forgive his brothers, recognizing that God used his suffering for good (Genesis 37, 39-50).

<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this whole account, God, who desires to dwell with his creation, has to be content to dwell with specific individuals, and finally establishes a nation through the covenant with Abraham (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="17" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 17:7</span>)۔

<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: The God who dwells with his people

<!-- p24 -->
God’s desire to dwell with his people is also clearly seen in the second book of the Bible, Exodus. In the book of Exodus, the Israelites, who for a time had lived in Egypt with relative peace and harmony, are now subject to the oppression of a Pharaoh who did not know Joseph and feared the Hebrews (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 1:6-8</span>)۔ God’s people lived in Egypt for approximately ~400 years (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="40-41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 12:40-41</span>), of which the latter portion was spent in slavery (there is no complete consensus on how long the Israelites were bound in Egypt).

<!-- p25 -->
In the powerful presence of the burning bush that did not burn up, God reveals himself to a man who was born a Hebrew, raised Egyptian, is a murderer in exile, and who is shepherding his father-in-law’s sheep in the wilderness — Moses۔

<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, 'I AM has sent me to you.'"
>

<!-- p28 -->
</span>

<!-- p29 -->
The phrase “I AM WHO I AM” (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is one of the most profound revelations of selfhood in Scripture. When Moses asks about God’s name, God answers with this phrase, which can be translated "I AM WHO I AM," "I WILL BE WHO I WILL BE," or simply "I AM." This declaration may seem somewhat perplexing, yet it carries deep meaning. Can you imagine introducing yourself to a new person by saying “I AM”? When God reveals himself as “I AM WHO I AM,” he is testifying to his own being, his eternal presence, his sovereignty. “I AM” is limitless in meaning and purpose۔

<!-- p30 -->
God reveals himself to Pharaoh through Moses:

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

</span>

<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh that God is “I AM” and that God demands that the Israelites be released so that they may worship him in the wilderness۔

<!-- p35 -->
Plagues and calamities descend, but eventually Pharaoh yields and lets the Israelites go۔

<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="31" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p38 -->
</span>

<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness, God miraculously delivers his people from the vengeful Egyptian army (Exodus 14), provides a pillar of fire to protect from the sun and to keep them warm in the cold of night (Exodus 13), food from heaven (Exodus 16), and water to drink (Exodus 17).

<!-- p40 -->
God brings His people to Mount Sinai, where God commands Moses to consecrate the people and gives them the Law (including the 10 Commandments) and establishes His covenant with them (the Old Covenant) (Exodus 19, 20, 24). This covenant (the Mosaic covenant) is built upon the Abrahamic covenant and remains in effect from Moses to Jesus.

<!-- p41 -->
Despite binding a covenant with His people, the relationship is that of a loving and jealous God who pursues and remains with a very stubborn and ungrateful people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="32" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 32:9</span>). Ignoring God’s miraculous intervention, they persist in rebellion for much of the narrative. God even brings them to the Promised Land and says they may take it as an inheritance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="33" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 33:3</span>), but because of fear and disobedience they disregard the supernatural deliverance that God had given them, and as a result they must wander in the wilderness for 40 years, until nearly all of that generation died (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-34" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).

<!-- p42 -->
### The remainder of the Old Testament

<!-- p43 -->
In the ~1,400 years between the death of Moses and the birth of Christ, Israel experienced a recurring cycle: devotion that brings God’s favor and blessings, then rebellion, God’s judgment, followed by their repentance, and finally God’s deliverance. This pattern is especially prominent in the books of Judges, Kings, and Chronicles, which recount cycles in which the people turn away from God, often by worshiping other gods, resulting in periods of oppression or exile. In response to their cries, God repeatedly raised up judges, prophets, or leaders to deliver them.

<!-- p44 -->
During those years, despite Israel’s repeated failures to keep their covenant with Him, God remains faithful, showing both justice and mercy. He continually saves and forgives His people, revealing His enduring commitment and love.

<!-- p45 -->
As we see in Scripture, God reveals Himself through His works, relationships, and even through names that disclose His character. Each name represents a particular aspect of His complete character. These names were given as part of the narrative of how God revealed Himself to His people. For your personal study, a reference table is included in the appendix: *Table 10 - The names of God in the Old Testament.* Read these names and study the reference verses. Each reference is a story that precisely shows how God saved, brought salvation, and delivered His people.

<!-- p46 -->
The appendix also includes further derived attributes that describe His nature, with explanations of His works and intentions: *Table 11 - The attributes of God.* Although these attributes are somewhat more abstract than the names of God, they are by no means less authoritative!

<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand God—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (the Trinity)—working together as one. In our ongoing study we will examine the names and attributes of Jesus and the Holy Spirit in greater depth.

<!-- p48 -->
God’s character and works are fully revealed in the unity of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. Scripture teaches that God is one in essence, yet exists as three distinct persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Scripture affirms God’s oneness, stating that “there is no God besides Me” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="45" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 45:5</span>). Yet the Godhead is understood as a compound unity—three distinct persons who work together with one mind and purpose, and are truly united. Each person of the Trinity has a distinct role: the Father creates, the Son redeems, and the Holy Spirit sanctifies. The word “Trinity” is not found in the Bible; it was coined as a theological term to describe this concept, which is revealed through Scripture.

<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can be illustrated with examples from nature that help us understand this concept of unity in plurality. Like water, which can be liquid, ice, or steam yet remains H₂O, so each person of the Trinity is fully God, yet distinct in person. Or consider a triangle, which has three sides and three angles and forms a single complete shape; if one side is removed, it is no longer a triangle. Likewise, the nature of God is incomplete without all three persons, who work together in perfect unity.

<!-- p50 -->
- **God the Father**: He is the Creator and Sustainer of all things, and is often understood as the initiator within the Godhead. His love for humanity is a central theme of the biblical story, exemplified in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:16</span>. In our study we will see God the Father as the loving Father and righteous Judge, who made a way for our redemption.

<!-- p51 -->
- **God the Son**: Co-eternal and co-everlasting with the Father, Jesus took on human nature, was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary. He lived a sinless life, gave His life to redeem humanity, and then rose again, conquering sin and death. He now sits at the right hand of the Father, interceding for us (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In our study we will see Jesus as the perfect atoning sacrifice, the risen Lord, and the head of the Church.

<!-- p52 -->
- **God the Holy Spirit**: The Holy Spirit, co-existent and equal with the Father and the Son, is sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world concerning sin and to prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. He empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In our study we will understand the Holy Spirit as the Comforter, given by God to prepare us, empower us, sanctify us, and be with us.

<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct persons, the triune nature of God is revealed as a perfect unity in purpose, will, and love, where each person plays a vital role in the divine mission of creation, redemption, and sanctification. The Trinity invites believers to experience God fully—as a loving Father, a redeeming Savior, and a transforming Spirit.

<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion

<!-- p55 -->
We can be uniquely identified by our titles: husband, father, son, brother, friend. Each of these declares how we are related to someone else. We can also be known by our attributes: faithful, patient, kind, intelligent, humorous. In the same way we can know God by His attributes and names—He is our Healer, Provider, Shepherd, and Redeemer. However, since titles and words are only a small part of our being, for others to truly know us they must experience us. This is even more true with God. To know Him truly and completely, we must experience Him.

<!-- p56 -->
The names of God reveal His character, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of what those names mean. For example, “God my Healer” takes on a richer and deeper meaning when we grasp the depths of sickness, despair, or helplessness out of which God brings us. Knowing Him as our Provider changes when we have felt real need and found Him faithful.

<!-- p57 -->
Scripture provides the context for these experiences, showing us God’s faithfulness to His people across time and circumstances, and reinforcing that His names and attributes are not merely titles but realities He brings to life, experienced in the lives of individuals. Through the interplay of this personal experience and the biblical narrative we come to know God—not merely as a distant description but as a present, active fellowship in every season of our lives.
