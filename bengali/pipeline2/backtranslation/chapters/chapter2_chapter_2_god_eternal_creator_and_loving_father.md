# Chapter 2 - God-Eternal Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is extremely important to establish a firm foundation in the knowledge of who God is—We can understand through study:

<!-- p2 -->
- His will, purposes, and works,

<!-- p3 -->
- His nature, character, and attributes,

<!-- p4 -->
- What has been said about Him and what He has said about Himself।

<!-- p5 -->
Scripture uniquely reveals the attributes of God; each facet deepens our understanding of our unchanging, eternal God।

<!-- p6 -->
To sum up God in a single word is difficult, if not impossible—because He is exceedingly vast and multidimensional, not captured by simplification। Though we will and certainly do make many attempts to explain, describe, and depict God, in the end, as with art and music, to understand Him one must have experience।

<!-- p7 -->
When someone listens to music, they say how it moves them-how it resonates within, beyond mere pitch and rhythm। A melody may carry warmth or intensity, a beat may have a pulse like a heartbeat, or a crescendo that creates a sense of elation or longing। Music can soothe like gentle waves, and it can also electrify like a storm, lifting the listener’s spirit, steadying thought, or transporting them to a completely different place। This experience is far more than the arrangement of sounds; it is the spirit of the music that remains, which is captured in how it shapes the listener’s emotions and personal journey।

<!-- p8 -->
Similarly, a viewer of a painting looks beyond colors or brushstrokes, exploring what these elements convey। A landscape may bring a peaceful refuge, while an abstract composition may arouse curiosity or introspection। Certain shades can bring warmth, while a composition can create a sense of calm, chaos, or beauty। The painting becomes a living experience, shifting perspective or awakening a profound emotional response that cannot be captured by words alone। Both music and art then become more than their physical form-it becomes an encounter that touches something fundamental, transforming the listener or viewer in a deep and personal way।

## Exploring God through the Old Testament

<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: The God who dwells with His creation

<!-- p11 -->
One central theme of the description of God is His desire to dwell with His own creation। This is evident in the narratives throughout the book of Genesis। Its fifty chapters and the genealogies from Adam (the first human) to Joseph (the final major figure in the book of Genesis, the last significant leader of God’s people before Moses) cover a period of approximately ~2,400 years। Through these accounts we see the continuity of God’s goodness even amid human wickedness।

<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God establishes relationships with several chosen individuals।

<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - the first humans, whom God created and placed in the Garden of Eden। God walked and talked with them, gave them instruction, and established a relationship of mutual love and obedience। However, this relationship is affected by their disobedience in eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 1-3)।

<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - although Cain ultimately rebels by killing his brother Abel, God speaks to him directly, warns him about the danger of sin, and gives him guidance before the murder। Even after Cain’s punishment, God gives him a sign so that he would be protected from harm (Genesis 4)।

<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - in a time of widespread depravity God forms a special relationship with a righteous man named Noah। God commands Noah to build an ark to save his family and the animals from the flood। Later, God makes a covenant with Noah, promising that He will never again destroy the earth with a flood and setting the rainbow as the sign of that covenant (Genesis 6-9)।

<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - one of the most significant relationships in Genesis, where God calls Abram to leave his homeland and promises to make him the father of a great nation। God establishes a covenant with Abram—promising land, descendants, and blessing for all nations through him। Abram’s faith and obedience become central themes, and as part of the covenant his name is changed from Abram to Abraham (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22)।

<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - Sarah’s Egyptian servant, Hagar encounters God in the wilderness after suffering mistreatment। God comforts her, promises a future for her son Ishmael, and instructs her to return to Abraham and Sarah। Hagar refers to God as “El Roi” (“the God who sees me”), acknowledging His care for her (Genesis 16)।

<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - Isaac, the son of Abraham and Sarah, inherits the covenant promises given to his father। God reaffirms these promises directly to Isaac, including the promise of descendants and blessing। God guides Isaac during a famine, protects him, and establishes a covenantal relationship (Genesis 26)।

<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - though her relationship with God is indirect, God answers Rebekah’s prayer concerning her difficult pregnancy and reveals that her twins, Jacob and Esau, will become two nations with distinct destinies (Genesis 25)।

<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God establishes a personal relationship with Jacob, appearing to him in dreams and confirming the promise of the covenant of land, descendants, and blessing. Jacob encountered God many times, including the famous wrestling incident in which God changed his name to Israel—symbolizing his transformation and the establishment of the nation of Israel (Genesis 28, 32, 35).

<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Although Joseph’s relationship with God is not based on direct appearances or covenants like Abraham’s or Jacob’s, God is nevertheless deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, divine favor, and providence, God guided Joseph from being sold into slavery to becoming a powerful leader in Egypt. Joseph’s faith in God’s sovereignty enabled him to forgive his brothers, as he realized that God had used his suffering for good (Genesis 37, 39-50).

<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this entire narrative, God, who desires to dwell with creation, must be content to dwell with only a chosen few individuals, and ultimately, through the covenant with Abraham, He establishes a nation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="17" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 17:7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: The God who dwells with His people

<!-- p24 -->
God’s desire to dwell with His people is clearly seen in Exodus, the second book of the Bible. In the book of Exodus, the people of Israel, who once lived for a time in relative peace and harmony in Egypt, are now being oppressed by a Pharaoh who did not know Joseph and feared the Hebrews (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 1:6-8</span>). God’s people lived in Egypt for about ~400 years (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="40-41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 12:40-41</span}), the latter portion of which was spent as slaves (there is no universal consensus on exactly how long Israel was enslaved in Egypt).

<!-- p25 -->
In the powerful presence of the burning bush that was not consumed, God revealed Himself to Moses, a Hebrew-born, Egypt-raised, exiled murderer who was tending his father-in-law’s flock in the wilderness.

<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Then God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, 'I AM has sent me to you.' "
>

<!-- p28 -->
</span>

<!-- p29 -->
**“I AM WHO I AM”** (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is one of the most profound self-revelations in Scripture. When Moses asks to know God’s name, God answers with this phrase, which can be translated as "I AM WHO I AM", "I WILL BE WHAT I WILL BE", or simply "I AM". Though this declaration may seem somewhat perplexing on the surface, it carries deep meaning. Can you imagine meeting someone new and introducing yourself as ‘I AM’? When God reveals Himself as ‘I AM WHO I AM’, He is bearing witness to His nature, His eternal presence, and His sovereignty. ‘I AM’ is infinite in meaning and purpose.

<!-- p30 -->
God uses Moses to reveal Himself to Pharaoh:

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Exodus 7:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And you shall say to him, 'The Lord God of the Hebrews has sent me to you, saying, "Let My people go, that they may serve Me in the wilderness"; but indeed, until now you have not been willing to listen!'
>

<!-- p33 -->
</span>

<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh that God is ‘I AM’ and that God demands the Israelites be set free so that they may worship Him in the wilderness.

<!-- p35 -->
Various plagues and calamities come, but in the end Pharaoh relents and lets the Israelites go.

<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="31" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p37 -->
> **<u>Exodus 12:31</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Then he called for Moses and Aaron by night, and said, "Rise, go out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel. And go, serve the Lord as you have said."
>

<!-- p38 -->
</span>

<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness God miraculously delivers His people from the hand of the vengeful Egyptian army (Exodus 14), shields them from the heat of the sun and gives them a pillar of fire to keep them warm in the cold nights of the desert (Exodus 13), provides food from heaven (Exodus 16), and gives them water to drink (Exodus 17).

<!-- p40 -->
God brings His people to Mount Sinai, where God commands Moses to consecrate the people and gives them the law (including the Ten Commandments) and establishes His covenant (the Old Covenant) with them (Exodus 19, 20, 24). This covenant (the Mosaic covenant) is built upon Abraham’s covenant and remains in effect from Moses to Jesus.

<!-- p41 -->
Even after establishing a covenant with His people, the relationship is that of a loving and jealous God who pursues and dwells among a very stubborn and ungrateful people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="32" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 32:9</span>). Ignoring God’s wondrous interventions, they maintain a rebellious attitude across many parts. God even brings them to the Promised Land and says that they may inherit it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="33" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 33:3</span>), but in fear and disobedience they reject the miraculous deliverance God had given, and instead wander in the wilderness for 40 years until nearly all of that generation dies (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-34" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).

<!-- p42 -->
### The rest of the Old Testament

<!-- p43 -->
From Moses’ death to Christ’s birth—about 1,400 years—the Israelites experience repeated cycles of devotion, which bring God’s grace and blessing; followed by rebellion, God’s judgment, their subsequent repentance, and finally God’s deliverance. This pattern is especially evident in the books of **Judges, Kings, and Chronicles**, where we repeatedly see the people turning away from God, often through the worship of other gods, resulting at times in oppression or exile. In response to their cries, God repeatedly raised up judges, prophets, or leaders to deliver them.

<!-- p44 -->
Throughout these years, even though the Israelites repeatedly failed to keep their covenant with Him, God remained faithful, displaying both justice and mercy. He continually rescued and forgave His people, proving His enduring commitment and love.

<!-- p45 -->
In Scripture we see that God reveals Himself through His works, relationships, and even through names that disclose His character. Each name represents a specific aspect of His entire character. These names were part of a narrative that described how God revealed Himself to His people. For your personal study a reference table is included in the appendix: *Table 10 - Names of God in the Old Testament.* Read these names and study the Scripture references. Each reference is a story that accurately portrays how God has protected, redeemed, and delivered His people.

<!-- p46 -->
The appendix also includes another set of attributes that describe His nature, works, and purposes: *Table 11 - Attributes of God.* These attributes, though somewhat more abstract than the names of God, are no less true!

<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand that God—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (the **Trinity**)—are working together in unity. In our ongoing study we will examine the names and attributes of Jesus and the Holy Spirit more deeply.

<!-- p48 -->
God’s character and works are fully revealed in the unity of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. Scripture teaches that God is one in essence, but exists in three distinct persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Scripture affirms God’s oneness by saying, “There is no other God besides Me” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="45" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 45:5</span>). However, the Godhead is understood as a **compound unity**—three distinct persons who cooperate with a single mind and purpose, truly united. Each person of the Trinity has a unique role: **the Father creates**, **the Son redeems**, and **the Holy Spirit sanctifies**. The word “Trinity” is not found in the Bible; it developed as a theological expression to describe this concept as revealed through Scripture.

<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can be explained with examples from nature, which clarify the idea of unity in diversity. Just as water, which can be liquid, ice, or vapor, yet remains H₂O—the persons of the Trinity are each fully God, yet distinct in personal identity. Or think of a triangle, whose three sides and three angles together form one complete shape; remove one side and it is no longer a triangle. Similarly, **God’s nature is incomplete without the three persons**, who work together in perfect unity.

<!-- p50 -->
- **God the Father**: He is the Creator and Sustainer of all things, often understood as the initiator within the Godhead. His love for humanity is central to the biblical narrative, exemplified in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:16</span>. In our study we will examine God the Father as a loving Father and righteous Judge, who has made the way for our salvation.

<!-- p51 -->
- **God the Son**: Co-existent and co-eternal with the Father, Jesus takes on human nature, conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the virgin Mary. He lives a sinless life, dies to redeem humankind, and rises again, conquering sin and death. He is now seated at the right hand of the Father, interceding for us (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In our study we will examine Jesus as the perfect atoning sacrifice, the risen Lord, and the Head of the Church.

<!-- p52 -->
- **God the Holy Spirit**: Co-existent and equal with the Father and the Son, the Holy Spirit is sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world concerning sin and to prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. He empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In our study we will examine the Holy Spirit as the Comforter, who equips, empowers, sanctifies, and abides with us on God’s behalf.

<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct persons, God’s triune nature is revealed as a unity full of purpose, will, and love; each person plays a vital role in the divine venture of creation, redemption, and sanctification. The Trinity invites believers to experience God fully—as a loving Father, a redeeming Savior, and a transforming Spirit.

<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion

<!-- p55 -->
We can be known in particular by our names: husband, father, son, brother, friend. Each of these is a declaration of who we are in the context of a relationship with someone else. We can also be known by our attributes: faithful, patient, kind, intelligent, witty. Similarly, we can know God by His attributes and names- He is our Healer, Provider, Shepherd, and Redeemer. However, just as labels and words reveal only a small part of us, to be truly known by others we must be experienced. All the more so with God. To know Him truly and fully, we must experience Him.

<!-- p56 -->
God’s names reveal His character, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of what these names actually mean. For example, “God my Healer” takes on a richer, deeper meaning when we understand the depths of sickness, despair, or helplessness from which God rescues. Knowing Him as our Provider is transformed when we have felt real need and found Him faithful.

<!-- p57 -->
Scripture gives context to these encounters, showing us God’s faithfulness to His people across time and circumstances; this confirms that His names and attributes are not merely titles, but realities that He brings to life, experienced in the lives of individuals. Through this interplay of personal experience and biblical narrative we come to know God—not as some distant description, but as a present, active relationship in every season of our lives.
