# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
Scripture tells us many stories about sin—its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin means to fail to meet God’s standard, or to “miss the mark,” an archery term in which the archer fails to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
In the Old and New Testaments, various words are used to denote sin; each term highlights aspects such as the kind of sin, its severity, intent, victim, nature, and the punishment it deserves. Scripture uses several broad categories to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against others, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Intentional sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- Sins committed in a moment, sins committed habitually

<!-- p6 -->
- Sins requiring severe punishment such as death, sins requiring financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
Our modern criminal justice systems also make similar distinctions: degrees of offenses, premeditated or crimes of passion, offenses that require financial restitution, or imprisonment, even the death penalty. An act can be both a crime and a sin, but crime is a human classification, sin is God’s.

<!-- p8 -->
There is broad overlap among the various Greek and Hebrew terms used for sin. All sin is broadly described under the umbrella term “missing the mark”—in Greek, ***Hamartia***, and in Hebrew, ***Chata’***. This umbrella term is used for the most serious sins—such as adultery or murder—as well as for relatively less serious ones—such as showing favoritism to the rich in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## Sacrifice is required for sin

<!-- p64 -->
In the account of Adam and Eve, God killed animals and made garments for them from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first instance of bloodshed to cover the consequences of sin. There are several similar accounts in Genesis, but in Leviticus God formally established a system of sacrifices for sin.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general offering for atonement and devotion to God, often symbolizing total surrender. This offering addressed sin in a broad sense, covering both known and unknown sins. In this ritual a male animal—typically a bull, sheep, goat, or bird—without defect was offered. The offerer laid a hand on the animal’s head, signifying identification with the sacrifice, and then the entire animal was burned on the altar. This act of burning the whole offering represented complete consecration in removing sin and aligning one’s life with God. The burnt offering emphasized the gravity of sin and the necessity of full devotion, displaying reverence and repentance by presenting something costly.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, known as ***Minchah***, was an offering of gratitude and dedication rather than a direct offering for sin. This offering was associated with other sacrifices such as the burnt and peace offerings, and was intended to express gratitude, worship, and devotion to God. Made from fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, this grain offering contained no blood. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar as a gift to God, and the rest was given to the priests as food. Although it was not specifically for sin, the grain offering was an important part of corporate worship, symbolically expressing the offerer’s dedication and gratitude for God’s provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This offering often followed the sin and atonement sacrifices, celebrating a restored relationship with God once the matter of sin had been dealt with. The offerer could bring a male or female animal without blemish, such as a cow, sheep, or goat. According to the ritual, the fatty portions—the best parts of the animal—were burned on the altar as God’s portion, while the remaining meat was shared between the priests and the offerer in a communal feast. The peace offering was unique in that it symbolized communion and fellowship, reflecting a sense of wholeness and unity with God. Once atonement for sin had been made, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and to celebrate the restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was designated specifically for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When an individual or community realized they had sinned unintentionally, they could offer a sin offering to obtain cleansing and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the status of the person involved: a priest or the whole assembly offered a bull, a leader offered a male goat, and an individual offered a female goat or a lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the sacrifice before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and if it was a communal sin, the remainder of the animal was disposed of outside the camp. This offering showed how serious even unintentional sin is and provided a way for people to remain clean and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins involving breach of trust, harming others, or profaning sacred things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins that needed making amends, such as property-related offenses or violations against holy things. To perform this offering, the sinner brought an unblemished ram for sacrifice. If the sin involved harm to another person or property, the offender also had to make restitution by returning the property and adding an additional 20 percent as a penalty. The guilt offering highlighted the importance of acknowledging one’s guilt and taking tangible steps to set the wrong right. It emphasized the relational and communal nature of sin, underscoring that true atonement requires not only seeking God’s forgiveness but also making things right with those affected by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Corporate Sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific offerings for individual sin, sacrifices and offerings were made daily, weekly, and monthly for the nation’s collective sins, but the most important of these was the **Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16, intended to cleanse the entire Israelite community from the collective sins of the past year. This unique ceremony took place once a year, on the 10th day of the seventh month, and addressed the sins of the nation rather than individual offenses. It served to purify the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people as symbols of atonement and cleansing for the whole community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the center of the Day of Atonement were two goats, which together represented both sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat was designated “for the Lord,” it was offered as a sin offering, and its blood was taken by the high priest into the Most Holy Place. There the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat—the cover of the ark of the covenant—symbolizing atonement for the people’s sins and reconciling them to God. This act of sprinkling the blood was highly significant, as it represented the cleansing of the holy place and ensured that God’s presence would remain with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, known as the **scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest would lay his hands on that scapegoat, confess the collective sins of Israel, and thus place those sins upon the animal. Then the goat was sent into the wilderness to die alone, which symbolized the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act served as a powerful example of both atonement and deliverance from sin, showing that God not only forgives, He also removes the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God's forgiveness and purification, which allowed the nation to delay the consequences of sin and to postpone judgment each year. This **atonement did not permanently forgive the nation's sins**, but rather delayed God's judgment.

## We have all sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible present the matter of sin in different ways. When Moses wrote down the law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he recorded specific penalties for specific sins/offenses. Here I am using the word 'offense' interchangeably, because the law books were effectively the legal system for the Israelites of that time.

<!-- p89 -->
In the prophetic and historical books, sin and its consequences are often described as the fateful outcome for the entire nation. When the kings and the people disobeyed God, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, defeated in war, and in some cases taken into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs the writer addresses his son like a loving father, warning him about the personal dangers of sin and the lifestyle that goes against God's commands. In the Psalms, many of David's writings reflect on the personal and emotional consequences when one sins.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns about sin, calls for personal repentance, and makes the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In the letter to the Romans, the apostle Paul explains how God has planned to deal with sin, and how we can be freed from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is, we are all guilty of sin, and all of us must pay the price for our offenses.

This misguided attempt at self-justification ignores the consequences of our actions, those we have hurt and those against whom we have sinned, and the reality of a holy and perfect God. Scripture says that if we say we do not sin, then we are liars, and the truth is not in us!
