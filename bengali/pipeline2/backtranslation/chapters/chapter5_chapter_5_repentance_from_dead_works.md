# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
Often in Christianity we think of repentance as merely something fundamental at the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The core meaning of repentance is much deeper than just turning from sin toward salvation. Repentance from dead works means living in such a way that we turn away from things that harm our hearts or hinder our growth—and turn toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews, in the list of foundational teachings given after the elementary principles of the Messiah (chapter 4), placed 'repentance from dead works' first.

### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever had a job knows what work is. Work is the set of tasks you do to get paid so that you can earn a living (job/employment). Employment is a kind of contract in which you exchange your talents and labor for something that has value and will help you sustain yourself. You work because you gain something from it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work matters; it can bring a sense of purpose and often a sense of identity. 'What do you do for a living?' and the answer begins with the declaration of identity, 'I am.' Work can be enjoyable and purposeful, but it also has a strong chance of being physically or mentally hard, difficult, challenging, and stressful. If your work is none of these, you may find it excessively trivial and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work a 40-hour-a-week job and sleep 8 hours every night, you spend 36% of your waking time just working—and that doesn't even include the time to get ready for work, commute, handle household tasks, and do the other necessary things to keep life on track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we work so much? The reasons may differ, but by definition it is because we get something of value from it.

<!-- p13 -->
If they stopped paying you, would you keep working at that job? For most of us, the simple and painfully clear answer is a resounding no!

<!-- p14 -->
If the work were killing you, would you keep doing it? I suppose that depends on how fast.

<!-- p15 -->
When ***nekros*** is joined with ***erga***, the context is labor or works that are dead, that have no vitality, that cannot reproduce! The author of Hebrews is warning us about such dead labor.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, several criteria are used to determine whether an organism is truly alive (for our purposes, a partial list is given here):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living entities convert energy through metabolic processes to grow, develop, and sustain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: Life involves increase and progression over time, with a defined life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living entities have the ability to reproduce; they can produce offspring or new entities.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adapt to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as temperature regulation or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living beings can adapt to their environment through genetic changes, which helps them survive under different conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this standard of ‘life’ to our works with a bit of creative license, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, cannot produce positive or desirable outcomes.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God's presence or the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Do not maintain internal stability, become out of control, are not self-disciplined.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (too rigid) and no longer help us to live.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God's will for us. God wants to give us life!

<!-- p33 -->
Dead versus alive is a binary matter. Dying is a gradual continuum. Our dead works may not be completely dead in every respect right now, but they will become so! Dead works are like poison; some have a stronger effect.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms by preventing the body from functioning normally. Some poisons have minor effects, like a headache. Others affect our thinking, sight, even whether we can conceive children (live fruitfully). Dead works are just like that. Sometimes they may be just a headache, while at other times they can bring about death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are any actions that, upon review and consideration, are seen not to be bringing the full life that Jesus promised. Getting involved in an extramarital relationship is a very strong poison; it can have complete and negative effects very quickly. Being needlessly stubborn and resentful slowly poisons a person in such a way that, in the end, he pushes everyone around him away.

<!-- p36 -->
To be freed from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
To ‘repent from dead works’ means to turn back to God and leave behind anything that is harming our walk with Him—beginning with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Keeping the comparison of dead works to poison, if you were to learn that your favorite snack was filled with toxic heavy metals that would put you in a deadly condition if you ate it, what would you do? Without hesitation we would put it away from ourselves, and never enjoy it again. Because we understand that consuming it would mean our premature, and possibly painful, death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must turn our faces away from the things in this life that are poisoning us and leading us toward both natural and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to look a little deeper into the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The word used in Hebrews is *repentance*. It is shown in contrast with the word *repent*; an example of this can be found

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
Repent (***Metanoeō***, pronunciation *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or event, meaning to change one’s mind or purpose; to turn away from sin and turn toward God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronunciation *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is the state of having repented; it means having had a change of mind; the state of having turned away from sin and turned toward God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews ‘repentance from dead works,’ He is talking about being in the state of having repented! We are to live in the continual state of ‘turning away’ from ‘dead works.’

<!-- p49 -->
How many times must we repent? Trick question! As long as we are not doing ‘dead works,’ we must continually bring about a change in our minds. Repenting is not asking for forgiveness or apologizing, although that may be involved. Repentance means turning away from the previous way of thinking.

### Methods of Change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap when it comes to changing our minds. If we do not even feel the need to change our minds, then why would we open our eyes to look for change? Again, to return to the poison analogy, if you do not even realize that the poison is killing you, then why would you stop taking it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe a person has to hit 'rock bottom' before they will look at their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that, as human beings, we are more complex. In psychology there are many theories related to behavioral change; among them, a highly esteemed theory is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People can go through a six-stage cycle for change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: No intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: Intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken toward change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: Behavior has been changed in the recent past.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: Behavior has been changed and is being maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: No desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no specific timeframe for how long each stage will last. On some matters of life, some people never meaningfully progress through the stages. In other cases, we may move from stages 1 to 6 very quickly.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotion Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father suddenly feels deep remorse after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *strict* but *loving* parent. He believes raising his voice is necessary. He is not thinking about changing. Rather, he may even justify it - "My father raised me like this too."

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long, tiring day at the office, he suddenly loses his temper and yells at his little daughter louder and more angrily than ever before. She startles. Her lips quiver. She walks away silently. In that moment… something **changes**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. His daughter's hurt face keeps surfacing in his mind. His stomach knots. He says to himself, *"I don't want to be that."* He knows he has to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes to her room, kneels, apologizes sincerely and says, *"Daddy was wrong. You didn't deserve that."* She hugs him. Motivated to make the change stick, he prays and talks with a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: From the very next day, he starts reading books on parenting to build positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: A few months pass. Yelling is no longer part of his habits. He is not perfect, but something has truly changed. He no longer justifies the old behavior - and he has no desire to go back.

### Helping Others Change

<!-- p79 -->
Much of our discussion has focused on the work and necessity of repentance from the perspective of the person who needs to change. However, we will encounter people who are desperate to change but are stuck in **Precontemplation**/**Contemplation**—blind to their need to change. Again, we will also see people who are desperate to change but completely ignorant of what to do (**Preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
While preaching to the crowds, Jesus often encountered people who did not know that they needed to change. We cannot force someone’s mind to change or repent on their behalf. That is not our job, nor is it Jesus’ example.

<!-- p81 -->
Jesus addresses this slow process and sometimes the inability to change by speaking in parables. Why did Jesus choose to speak in parables? In Matthew he reveals why:

Jesus is saying that He uses parables to slowly open the doors of the minds and eyes of those who cannot see or understand the truth. If you find yourself dealing with someone whose mind is closed, I would advise you to pray and seek God’s counsel on how to proceed. From experience and from Jesus’ example, an experience with truth full of grace can, sometimes slowly, begin to open the eyes of the blind. Remember, the work of conviction and illumination is the Spirit’s.

Once they begin to experience the transformative process of repentance, it produces a good harvest in their lives. If they have been sickened by a poison, having their eyes opened, stopping the poisoning, and taking the antidote will begin to heal them.

### Godly sorrow and God’s goodness

<!-- p92 -->
Hitting rock bottom is more of a myth than a guaranteed prerequisite for change; however, God can use sorrow to influence our minds. God will use sorrow to bring us to repentance. He will make us feel convicted about our actions and the state of our lives and hearts, and open our eyes so that we may see our total and complete need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>the sorrow or affliction experience that leads to a *metanoia*</u>, is not like worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of sorrow or affliction, but the world offers no hope, only the promise of relief and of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is again different from pain and grief.

<!-- p98 -->
Pain is the endurance of pain—physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual—especially when it is prolonged or intense.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is the multidimensional emotional response to a significant personal loss, especially relational or existential.

<!-- p100 -->
Grief, suffering, and sorrow—these are closely related concepts. In human life we will experience all three, and through these three we can learn and grow, but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
Scripture says that every good and perfect gift comes from God above (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow will cause discomfort, but His discipline is just and loving. Just as a good father would not deliberately break a child’s hand in order to ‘teach a lesson,’ likewise God will not impose sickness or disease on His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### An example of worldly sorrow vs. godly sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He had a stick in his hand, and he deliberately struck his sister, causing her to cry and leaving a red mark on her skin.

<!-- p104 -->
Their father heard the commotion and came to check, and when he learned what his son had done, he took the stick and broke it in two. He sent his son inside to think about what he had done, and he comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came inside to deal with his son, he saw that the boy was crying. When asked why he was crying, the boy said, “Because you broke my stick.”

<!-- p106 -->
The father replied, “You hit your sister, yet you’re more concerned about your stick than about her—that saddens me,” and he told him to go back to his room to think further.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later his son came out again and apologized. He said he was still sad—on the one hand because his father had broken his stick, and on the other because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example the boy experienced both—worldly sorrow, over the lost stick; and eventually godly sorrow, over his injured sister.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and God’s goodness are interconnected. God is a good Father; He will allow us to bear the consequences of our actions, yet He will be with us even as we walk through those consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
God’s steadfast goodness—that He stands ready to deliver and restore us when we come to Him—is a positive force on our path of repentance. His goodness does not mean that we continue in sin; rather, despite our sin He makes a way for our deliverance and desires to let us experience His salvation.

<!-- p112 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

> Or do you despise the riches of His goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?

Despite God’s goodness, mercy, and grace, some people do not repent and do not change; they get trapped in a cycle of frustration and pain. At the opposite extreme, for some people merely seeing the example of sin and its consequences in someone else’s life is enough to change their thinking.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a tremendous capacity for change; it is rooted at the core of our being. People are highly adaptable—we can learn and grow, and we can find better ways of doing things. However, having the capacity to change is not the same as being willing or motivated to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I don’t know why some people get stuck while others are willing to change so readily; but I can say that the entire process of transformation is in fact from God’s grace.

Repentance can be an entirely natural process, but repentance is at the same time the work of the Spirit—who calls out to us, opens our eyes to the truth, changes our hearts, and invites us to be transformed. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:1</span> reminds us that some will be ‘caught’ in transgression (sin) and need help to be set free! That help comes through mature men and women who, in a spirit of gentleness, help restore us.

(2010) showed that among clients who experienced a spiritual awakening during addiction treatment and recovery, up to 82% were completely abstinent at a 1-year follow-up, whereas among clients without a spiritual awakening it was 55%.” \[2\] People can initiate and mediate change, but we can accomplish much more if we walk with the Spirit of God.

<!-- p123 -->
### David’s Example of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps there is no richer example of repentance in Scripture than the account of King David’s sin described in 2 Samuel 11–12. It begins with David remaining in Jerusalem, not going to war; there he sees Bathsheba bathing. He knew that she was the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, yet David sent for her and they had relations, resulting in Bathsheba becoming pregnant.

<!-- p125 -->
To cover his deed, David summoned Uriah from the battlefield, hoping that he would spend time with Bathsheba and it would appear that he was the child’s father. But Uriah, out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, refused to go to his own house and be with Bathsheba. David then arranged for Uriah to be sent to the front lines, where he was ultimately killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David hurried to marry Bathsheba to conceal the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was displeased with David’s actions and sent the prophet Nathan to confront him. When the prophet confronted him, David immediately admitted, “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify his actions or make excuses. This confession was the first step of his repentance—frankly acknowledging his guilt before God.

<!-- p127 -->
David’s words in Psalm 51, “*For I know my transgressions, and my sin is always before me,*” demonstrate full ownership of his actions. He not only mourned the sin but also how it had grieved God—“*Against You, You only, have I sinned.*” David displayed “*a broken and contrite heart,*” an essential component of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This grief was not superficial; he was deeply sorrowful for the sin he had committed and the harm it caused. The depth of this contrition shows that he sought not only forgiveness but also a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David said to God, “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He desired an inner transformation that would keep him from repeating the same mistake. Rather than merely expressing sorrow, David pleaded with God to purify him, saying, “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He realized that only God could cleanse his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be spiritually renewed reflects his genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways going forward. By asking for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) he expressed the hope of aligning with God in every area of life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God put away David’s sin, the Lord struck the child and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God to spare the child; but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find, on the one hand, a personal prayer of repentance and, on the other, a public acknowledgment of his offenses. By recording those words of plea for mercy, which have become one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible, David showed that he was willing to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm has become a testimony of repentance and restoration for others to learn from, memorializing his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not merely feeling sorry; it was a profound change in his heart and life. Through confession, acceptance, and the heart transformation recorded in Psalm 51, David made his sin memorable as a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration—so that his story has become one of the most important examples of true repentance in the Bible.
