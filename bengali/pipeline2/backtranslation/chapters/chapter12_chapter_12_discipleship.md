# Chapter 12 - Discipleship

<!-- p1 -->
Discipleship means following Jesus, learning from Him, and becoming like Him in our way of life and love. It is a lifelong journey of obedience, transformation, and service, led by the Holy Spirit and rooted in the Word of God.

<!-- p2 -->
In the Great Commission, discipleship is the primary charge. Jesus defined this mission:

<!-- p3 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p4 -->
> **<u>Matthew 28:18-20</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And Jesus came and spoke to them, saying, "All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth.
>
> Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,
>
> teaching them to observe all things that I have commanded you; and behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age." Amen.
>

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

<!-- p6 -->
We, His followers, are to *"Go and make disciples of all nations."* Discipleship is about personal growth and about multiplying that growth—by teaching others to follow Christ. Discipleship is the vehicle by which we build the Kingdom of God; the teaching of Jesus is the message.

<!-- p7 -->
The call to be a disciple is not merely going to church or learning Bible verses—it is an invitation to live like Jesus and to partner with Him to advance the Kingdom of God. True discipleship helps believers become spiritually mature and effective for service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:13</span>). It develops the heart and skills necessary to serve others and share the Gospel. As Paul instructed Timothy in  <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, discipleship follows a pattern of spiritual multiplication, where trained disciples continue to form new followers so that they can bring others to Christ.

<!-- p8 -->
Discipleship is, on the one hand, a personal calling, and on the other, a global mission. At a personal level, it includes growing in faith, cultivating an active spiritual life, and being in relationship with others &amp; with God. At the same time, it fuels the Church’s mission to carry the Gospel to every nation, and Christ’s message spreads from generation to generation, continuing to transform lives.

<!-- p9 -->
## Why Discipleship Matters

<!-- p10 -->
The impact of discipleship extends beyond personal transformation—it has Kingdom implications. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> Paul reminded Timothy to entrust what he had learned to faithful people who would also teach others. This generational pattern shows that discipleship is the engine driving the Church’s growth and endurance. Discipleship matters because it is what Jesus chose for spreading the Gospel and building His Church.

<!-- p11 -->
Instead of focusing on large crowds, Jesus intentionally invested in a few followers—He taught them, modeled faithfulness, and prepared them to carry His mission forward. This personal, relational approach ensured that His message would multiply, because each disciple was equipped to make more disciples.

<!-- p12 -->
Essentially, discipleship is central to fulfilling the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). Jesus did not tell us merely to make converts, but to make disciples—followers who are grounded in the Word of God, empowered by the Holy Spirit, and equipped for service. Through discipleship believers grow in knowledge of Scripture, develop spiritual practices, and learn to walk in obedience to Christ. It prepares the Church to influence the world, advance the Gospel, and reflect God’s glory to all nations.

<!-- p13 -->
### Nature vs. Nurture

<!-- p14 -->
In the study of biology, psychology, and anthropology, we often consider the influence of nature versus nurture. The question is often asked: Is a person more the result of his or her inherent, internal genetic inheritance? Or more the result of environment and upbringing? The truth is, every person is a blend of nature and nurture. In a powerful interaction, these two elements choreograph the development of the human soul.

<!-- p15 -->
When you received Jesus, in the miracle of salvation you were born again of imperishable seed. You received a godly/divine nature! Your spiritual DNA was rewritten! This divine nature stirs in our hearts a longing to draw close to our heavenly Father, to know Him, to experience Him.

<!-- p16 -->
Like a newborn, whose wonderful, beautiful, innocent nature needs to be shaped by loving parents so that he or she may become a healthy and mature person, we likewise need to be formed and nurtured in order to flourish.

<!-- p17 -->
Discipleship is the process of spiritual development that, built upon our divine nature, brings us to spiritual maturity. By investing in discipleship we ensure that faith is passed down, leaders are raised up, and the Church remains healthy and vibrant both locally and globally.

<!-- p18 -->
### Didactic Model vs. Apprenticeship Model

<!-- p19 -->
In modern Western education, a student's primary relationship is with the material. The student's learning is centered on content, materials, books, videos, assignments, etc. Transformation in education happens through the curriculum. This approach is called the *didactic model*.

<!-- p20 -->
The instructional model paradigm has not always been the standard. In Jesus’ time the model and method used was the *apprenticeship model.* This learning model emphasizes that knowledge is ‘more caught than taught’ and focuses more on the student–teacher relationship than on the student and the curriculum.

<!-- p21 -->
The instructional model gradually became the default approach over nearly a millennium, through Europe’s first medieval universities in the 11<sup>th</sup> - 13<sup>th</sup> centuries, the invention of the printing press in the 15<sup>th</sup> century, the Enlightenment of the 17<sup>th</sup> &amp; 18<sup>th</sup> centuries, and the influence of the Industrial Revolution on information and education in the 19<sup>th</sup> &amp; 20<sup>th</sup> centuries.

<!-- p22 -->
A benefit of the instructional model is that it can scale more efficiently than the apprenticeship model. Massive open online courses (MOOCs) can accommodate thousands of students at the same time, whereas a highly skilled master may be able to have only a few dozen apprentices at once.

<!-- p23 -->
The result of the instructional model is that the student gains mastery of the subject matter, but the student does not become like the teacher. The teacher serves as a facilitator of the curriculum. The teacher imparts knowledge.

<!-- p24 -->
In the apprenticeship model the master not only shares knowledge; he imparts wisdom. The result of the apprenticeship model is that the student becomes so much like the master that the student can reproduce the master’s work and take on apprentices of their own.

<!-- p25 -->
In the Kingdom of God the instructional model also has its use and place; a good example is this book or any other Christian instructional material. Jesus, as a Rabbi who was skilled and trained in the rabbinic method, practiced the apprenticeship model as the primary model for his disciples.

<!-- p26 -->
Jesus described the apprenticeship model:

<!-- p27 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="40" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p28 -->
> **<u>Luke 6:40</u>** (NKJV)
>
> A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone who is perfectly trained will be like his teacher.
>

<!-- p29 -->
</span>

<!-- p30 -->
In the instructional model the student becomes a carrier of knowledge; in the apprenticeship model the student becomes like the teacher. Let us study the key words of that passage in Luke.

<!-- p32 -->
In summary, the apprenticeship model used in discipleship has two purposes and goals:

<!-- p33 -->
**First, the disciple is fully trained**, like the teacher. This does not mean the disciple will imitate everything about the teacher. We are not commanded to make clones, but to form mature men and women who can carry the message of Jesus to the ends of the earth.

<!-- p34 -->
**Second, the disciple now becomes a teacher for others**. What the disciple has learned must be passed on to someone else, or else the message of the Kingdom stops with them. This does not mean they will receive exactly the same gifts as their teacher, or that they will enter full-time ministry, but it does mean that for their training to be complete, they must reproduce it in others.

<!-- p35 -->
Any living thing that does not reproduce is barren, and its lineage is cut off. An organism that cannot reproduce is unhealthy; a church that cannot reproduce is likewise unhealthy.

## Jesus’ model and process of discipleship

<!-- p37 -->
In the Gospels Jesus demonstrated a mature and complete example of discipleship with his chosen disciples. We should use these steps broadly as a foundation for our own path of being and making disciples.

<!-- p38 -->
In the apprenticeship model there are two roles: the master and the learner. Every master was once a learner, and hopefully every learner will one day be a master. To refer to the learner I will use *disciple* and to refer to the master/teacher I will use *disciple-maker*.

<!-- p39 -->
Every discipling relationship progresses through eight stages. These stages, or steps, are not completely separate; often the relationship flows between the stages. In each stage there are distinct emphases for the disciple and the disciple-maker. Later in this chapter we will study the qualities of disciple-makers in depth.

<!-- p40 -->
The eight steps of discipleship are:

<!-- p41 -->
1.  Invitation/Call

<!-- p42 -->
2.  Teaching

<!-- p43 -->
3.  Demonstration

<!-- p44 -->
4.  Participation/Attempt

<!-- p45 -->
5.  Correction

<!-- p46 -->
6.  Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p47 -->
7.  Multiplication

<!-- p48 -->
8.  Staying Connected

<!-- p49 -->
### Invitation/Call

<!-- p50 -->
Discipleship begins with a personal invitation from Jesus to follow Him. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span>, Jesus called His first disciples, saying, *"Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men."* This invitation was relational and transformative—it required leaving behind old ways of life and stepping into a new purpose centered on God's mission. Jesus' call was not to remain spectators, but to participate in His ministry and the work of the kingdom.

<!-- p51 -->
Modern disciple-makers should invite others into such a genuine relationship that inspires spiritual growth and obedience. The call to discipleship is deeply personal. Jesus meets people where they are, addressing their specific needs and circumstances, but He calls them to go deeper—to grow, to serve, and to become like Him. This pattern reflects the relational nature of discipleship, where the emphasis is not only on knowledge but on a life-transforming relationship with Christ.

<!-- p52 -->
### Teaching

<!-- p53 -->
Teaching lies at the heart of discipleship. Jesus devoted a large portion of His ministry to teaching the principles of the Kingdom of God, especially in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7). His teachings provided the foundation for living righteously, loving others, and walking in obedience to God. Instruction in God's Word remains central to discipleship today, because Scripture is the primary means for spiritual growth, correction, and training in righteousness (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p54 -->
Disciple-makers should be committed to faithfully teaching the Bible so that others can understand it and apply it in life. Teaching is not merely the transfer of knowledge—it requires modeling a Christlike way of life so that disciples can see faith in action. Jesus embodied in His own life the truths He taught, showing that effective teaching is both verbal and visible. Likewise, disciple-makers should strive to live out what they teach, providing real examples of obedience and faithfulness.

<!-- p55 -->
### Demonstration

<!-- p56 -->
Jesus did not merely teach His disciples—He demonstrated how to live out the proclaimed truths. Jesus showed in practical ways what it means to love, serve, and minister. Some examples from the Gospels include Jesus washing the disciples' feet as an act of humility and service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="13" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 13:12-15</span>), healing the sick, and feeding the hungry. Through His actions He set a model of compassion, faith, and courage for His followers to emulate.

<!-- p57 -->
Discipleship requires not only telling, but showing. Modern disciple-makers should be transparent about their faith journey, including struggles and victories, so that others can see how faith works in real life. Whether praying with someone in need, sharing testimony, or serving the community—discipleship thrives when it stands on practical demonstration. Actions often speak louder than words, so demonstration is a powerful way to train others in the faith.

<!-- p58 -->
### Participation/Attempt

<!-- p59 -->
Jesus did not sit His disciples on the sidelines—He actively involved them in ministry experiences. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 9:1-6</span> and <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 10:5-15</span>, Jesus sent the disciples to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out evil spirits. These hands-on opportunities allowed the disciples to learn by doing, and helped them gain confidence and competence under His guidance.

<!-- p60 -->
Modern discipleship should also include practical engagement. Disciple-makers must invite disciples to take part in service, whether that is leading a prayer-group, sharing testimonies, or serving in outreach programs. Providing a safe space for practice helps disciples grow in boldness while they receive support and encouragement. Learning happens best when it is applied, and participation is the key to turning theory into practice.

<!-- p61 -->
### Correction

<!-- p62 -->
Growth often requires correction, and Jesus lovingly corrected His disciples when they misunderstood His teaching or failed in their actions. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-37" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Mark 9:33-37</span>, He taught them about pride, instructing them about servant leadership. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="22" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="31-32" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 22:31-32</span>, He prayed for Peter, preparing him to be restored from failure and to strengthen others.

<!-- p63 -->
In discipleship, correction should be full of grace and constructive - rooted in Scripture and aimed at restoration (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 4:2</span>). It is not about blame, but about helping disciples to grow and avoid pitfalls. Disciple-makers must provide accountability, use God’s Word to give guidance and encourage growth, and themselves set an example of humility in receiving correction.

<!-- p64 -->
### Empowerment/Granting Authority

<!-- p65 -->
Jesus not only taught and corrected - He also empowered His disciples to carry out His mission. He gave them authority to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:18-20</span>). After His resurrection, He promised them the Holy Spirit as the source of power for ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>).

<!-- p66 -->
Modern discipleship should include empowering others to walk and serve effectively in their spiritual gifts. This includes identifying the gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:11-13</span>), encouraging their use, and releasing disciples into ministry. Empowerment further emphasizes dependence on the Holy Spirit for power, boldness, and wisdom, so that disciples are spiritually equipped to do the work to which God has called them.

<!-- p67 -->
### Multiplication

<!-- p68 -->
The goal of discipleship is not merely personal growth - it is multiplication. Jesus modeled this by training disciples to make more disciples (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). After His resurrection, the apostles continued this multiplying mission, spreading the gospel and establishing churches throughout the known world. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, Paul’s instruction reinforces this principle, encouraging Timothy to teach those who will, in turn, teach others.

<!-- p69 -->
Discipleship includes equipping disciples in such a way that they can also disciple others, creating a chain reaction of faith and expanding God’s kingdom. This process involves intentionality - disciples are taught how to mentor, teach, and train, and they are sent out with confidence and purpose (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 10:15</span>).

<!-- p70 -->
### Stay Connected

<!-- p71 -->
Discipleship does not end the moment someone is sent out. Jesus modeled ongoing connection with His disciples; even after His resurrection He continued to encourage and instruct them (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="21" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 21:15-19</span>). The early church maintained this pattern as well, gathering regularly for teaching, fellowship, and prayer (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>).

<!-- p72 -->
Modern discipleship should also cultivate ongoing relationships, so that as disciples grow and lead they receive accountability, encouragement, and support. Maintaining unity in the body of Christ ensures that disciples are never isolated, but remain strengthened and encouraged in their journey of faith.

<!-- p73 -->
## Who Does Discipleship?

<!-- p74 -->
Everyone in the body of Christ bears the responsibility first to be a disciple, and then to contribute to the process of discipleship formation. There is diversity of gifts and functions, so our contributions may look different, but we are all accountable. Those who are ultimately most responsible for the state and health of discipleship in the local church are the leaders (**elders and deacons - <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 14:23</span>**), but everyone has a role.

<!-- p75 -->
Discipleship is a living, developing process - like nurturing a child into maturity. Healthy discipleship gives people strength and life. Just as a parent strengthens and nourishes a child so that they become healthy and mature, so disciple-makers help to nourish, strengthen, and mature disciples. The ultimate goal of discipleship is not ‘transfer of knowledge,’ but transformation.

<!-- p76 -->
We must all become complete (mature) and make disciples. Though our gifts differ, there are some common characteristics for all disciple-makers.

<!-- p77 -->
### Characteristics of a Disciple-Maker

<!-- p78 -->
A disciple-maker is someone who not only follows Christ but is also committed to helping others grow in their faith. This role requires certain specific qualities that reflect both spiritual maturity and practical devotion. Disciple-makers must continually rely on God’s strength, wisdom, and grace to overcome the challenges that come with leading others in their spiritual journey.

<!-- p79 -->
#### Rooted in Christ

<!-- p80 -->
The foundation of a disciple-maker’s life must be deeply rooted in Christ. Jesus said, *“I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in Me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from Me you can do nothing”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="15" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 15:5</span>). Effective disciple-makers know that growth and the ability to help others come from staying close to Jesus every day. However, resistance to personal growth and spiritual transformation can hinder this dependence. Disciple-makers must continually nurture their personal relationship with Jesus through prayer, Scripture study, and obedience, so that they can present to those they are discipling a model of what it means to abide in Christ.

<!-- p81 -->
#### Committed to the Mission

<!-- p82 -->
A disciple-maker must remain committed to the disciple-making mission Jesus gave in the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19–20</span>). This mission requires intentional focus and prioritization, because disciple-making is not an optional task but the church’s fundamental purpose. Yet challenges such as time constraints, busyness, and personal distractions can make it difficult to stay focused. Disciple-makers should intentionally set aside time for mentoring, teaching, and relationship-building, and view disciple-making not as an occasional activity but as a way of life (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 19:10</span>).

<!-- p83 -->
#### Led by the Holy Spirit

<!-- p84 -->
Disciple-making is not possible by human strength alone; it requires the guidance and empowerment of the Holy Spirit. Jesus promised His disciples that when the Holy Spirit came upon them, they would receive power (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span}), which would prepare them to be witnesses and servants of the gospel. Disciple-makers should remain sensitive to the Spirit’s leading, relying on His wisdom to navigate complex situations, cultural barriers, and difficult conversations. This dependence on the Holy Spirit enables disciple-makers to act with love, courage, and spiritual discernment, so that their efforts are not self-directed but Spirit-directed.

<!-- p85 -->
#### Humble and Teachable

<!-- p86 -->
Humility is the hallmark of an effective disciple-maker. As Paul exhorted, *“Do nothing out of selfish ambition or vain conceit. Rather, in humility value others above yourselves”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 2:3</span>). Disciple-makers acknowledge that they too are lifelong learners who must remain teachable and accountable. They welcome correction and feedback, setting a model of humility for those they mentor. Pride can be a major obstacle to growth, for both disciple-makers and their disciples. Embracing accountability and maintaining a servant-hearted posture builds trust and creates an environment for growth and transparency.

<!-- p87 -->
#### Patient and Persevering

<!-- p88 -->
Discipleship is rarely instantaneous—it is a long-term process that requires patience and perseverance. Paul reminds believers, *“Let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:9</span>). Growth in faith often involves setbacks, questions, and failures, yet disciple-makers are called to walk alongside others, offering encouragement and support at every step. It is easy to become discouraged when progress seems slow, but disciple-makers trust that God is at work, even when results are not immediately visible.

<!-- p89 -->
Becoming a disciple-maker requires a blend of spiritual dependence, commitment, and humility. It is a calling that demands patience and perseverance, while also remaining Spirit-led and teachable. Disciple-makers are not expected to be perfect, but they must be faithful—abiding in Christ, investing in others, and trusting that in God’s timing He will bring growth and transformation. These qualities reflect the love of Christ and help others become mature disciples who can in turn make disciples.

## Practical Tools for Discipleship

<!-- p91 -->
Effective discipleship requires intentional tools and practices that foster spiritual growth, accountability, and community. These tools help disciples build a firm foundation in faith, grow in intimacy with God, and be equipped to make disciples of others. Practical methods such as Bible study, prayer, meeting in small groups, and mentorship provide the structure and support necessary for ongoing growth.

<!-- p92 -->
### Bible Study

<!-- p93 -->
Deep engagement with Scripture is the foundation of discipleship. Jesus emphasized the importance of God’s Word for spiritual growth, saying, *“Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:4</span>). Regular Bible study and memorization enable disciples to internalize truth, confront lies, and apply God’s wisdom to daily life.

<!-- p94 -->
Disciples should be encouraged to immerse themselves in Scripture not merely as an academic exercise, but to know God and see their hearts transformed (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="119" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="105" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 119:105</span>). Practical approaches such as reading plans, study guides, and group discussions provide opportunities to explore doctrine and discuss application in community. By establishing regular study habits, disciples lay a strong foundation for spiritual maturity and fruitful service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16–17</span>).

<!-- p95 -->
### Prayer

<!-- p96 -->
Prayer is the lifeline of discipleship, connecting believers to God’s presence, power, and guidance. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 11:1–4</span> Jesus taught His disciples how to pray, emphasizing both dependence on God and spiritual alignment with His will. Through prayer, disciples learn to seek God’s wisdom, intercede for others, and receive strength for service.

<!-- p97 -->
Teaching disciples how to pray—both personally and corporately—enables them to cultivate intimacy with God and discern His leading (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 4:6–7</span>). Incorporating prayer journals, prayer groups, and the practice of fasting fosters spiritual discipline and empowerment, and ensures that disciples remain Spirit-led and mission-centered.

<!-- p98 -->
### Small Groups and Community

<!-- p99 -->
Discipleship flourishes within community. The early church demonstrated a model of relational discipleship through fellowship, prayer, and breaking bread together (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42–47</span>). Small groups and accountability partnerships provide a safe space for disciples to grow together, share struggles, and celebrate victories.

<!-- p100 -->
The local church serves as the primary hub for discipleship relationships, where opportunities are provided for group Bible study, prayer meetings, and fellowship events. These gatherings enable disciples to practice love, bear one another’s burdens (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:2</span>), and encourage one another toward spiritual maturity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="24-25" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:24-25</span>). Small groups also provide a framework for raising up leaders, as mature disciples train others and are sent to lead groups and mentor new believers.

<!-- p101 -->
### Mentorship

<!-- p102 -->
Mentorship provides a personal and intentional approach to discipleship. Jesus set the example by investing deeply in the twelve disciples, walking with them through teaching, correction, and encouragement. Likewise, one-to-one relationships allow disciple-makers to tailor guidance to individual needs, which helps disciples develop in faith, character, and leadership.

<!-- p103 -->
Mentors serve as accountability partners, providing support, prayer, and wisdom during times of growth and struggle. Practical tools such as spiritual assessments, goal-setting, and regular check-ins help ensure that disciples remain focused and motivated. Peer relationships within a mentorship framework also encourage mutual growth, as both mentor and disciple learn from shared experiences (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PRO" data-chapter="27" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Proverbs 27:17</span>).

## **Personal Reflection Questions**

<!-- p105 -->
1.  What does it mean to you personally to be a disciple of Jesus? How are you actively following Him in your daily life?

<!-- p106 -->
2.  Reflect on Jesus’ model of discipleship. Which aspect of His approach—invitation, teaching, demonstration, or empowerment—stands out to you the most, and why?

<!-- p107 -->
3.  How can you multiply your faith by discipling others? What challenges or fears might hold you back, and how can you overcome them? (Notice, I said multiply your faith, not your disciples!)

<!-- p108 -->
4.  Which qualities of a disciple-maker (e.g., patience, humility, relational focus) do you see in yourself? Which qualities do you feel called to develop further?

<!-- p109 -->
5.  Think about the people in your life. Whom does it seem God is calling you to disciple? How can you take the first step in building that relationship?

## Group Discussion Questions (20-30 minutes)

<!-- p111 -->
1.  **Icebreaker Question:** Who had the greatest influence on your spiritual growth, and what did they do that impacted you?

<!-- p112 -->
2.  **Exploring Jesus’ Model:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span> Read. How does Jesus’ invitation, ‘Follow Me,’ capture the essence of discipleship? What does this look like today?

<!-- p113 -->
3.  **The Call to Make Disciples:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span> Discuss. Why is making disciples central to the church’s mission? How can we participate in this mission as individuals?

<!-- p114 -->
4.  **Overcoming Challenges:** What are some common obstacles to disciple-making (e.g., time, fear, lack of confidence)? How can we help one another overcome these obstacles?

<!-- p115 -->
5.  **Practical Application:** What is one specific step you can take this week to invest in someone’s spiritual growth? How can we, as a group, encourage one another in this process?
