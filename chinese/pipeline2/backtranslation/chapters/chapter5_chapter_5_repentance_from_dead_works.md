### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever been employed knows what work is。Work is a set of tasks you take on to earn a paycheck and make a living（employment）。Employment is an agreement in which you exchange your talent and effort for something of value that helps you sustain your livelihood。You work because you can get a return from it。

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can bring a sense of purpose and often a sense of identity。People often ask ‘What do you do for a living？’，and the answer usually begins with an identity statement: ‘I am’。Although work can be enjoyable and meaningful, it can also be physically or mentally grueling, difficult, challenging, and stressful。如果你的工作完全不涉及这些，你可能会觉得它过于琐碎和无聊。

<!-- p11 -->
If you work 40 hours a week and sleep 8 hours a night, then 36% of your waking time is spent working, and that does not include the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting, caring for your household, and all the other necessary tasks you must do to get your life on track。

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work？The reasons vary, but by definition, it is because we receive a valuable return from it。

<!-- p13 -->
If they stopped paying you, would you keep doing a job？For most of us, the simple and obvious answer is: No！

<!-- p14 -->
If the job were killing you, would you keep doing it？I think that depends on how fast。

<!-- p15 -->
The context of putting ***nekros*** together with ***erga*** refers to labor or work of death, lacking vitality and unable to reproduce！The author of Hebrews is warning us about these dead labors。

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, there are several criteria used to determine whether an organism is alive（this is a partial list for our purposes）：

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living things convert energy through metabolic processes in order to grow, develop, and maintain themselves。

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: Life involves growth and progression over time, with specific life cycles and developmental changes。

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living things have the ability to reproduce, producing offspring or new individuals。

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to Stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound), and can adjust to adapt to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Organisms maintain internal stability, such as temperature regulation or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living organisms can adapt to their environment through genetic changes, enabling them to survive under various conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we take a bit of creative liberty and apply the criteria of ‘life’ to our behaviors, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, and cannot produce positive or desirable outcomes.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, nor respond to the light of God’s presence or the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Cannot maintain internal stability, become dysregulated, and cannot exercise self-control.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (are too rigid) and no longer help us survive.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s will for us. God wants to bring us life!

Although life and death are binary opposites. Death, however, is a gradient. Our dead works may not yet be completely dead in every category, however, they eventually will be! Dead works are like poison, some have a greater impact.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning normally. Some poisons have smaller effects, such as a headache. Others affect the way we think, our vision, and even whether we can reproduce (live fruitfully). Dead works are just like this. They may be merely a headache, or they may lead to death.

Committing adultery is a very potent poison, it will very likely produce complete and negative effects very quickly. ### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
‘Repentance from dead works’ means turning to God and throwing away anything that is harming our walk with Him - beginning with a change of mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Continuing the analogy of poison for dead works, if you discovered that your favorite snack was full of toxic heavy metals, and once consumed would cause you to develop a deadly illness, what would you do? We would not hesitate to keep it far from ourselves, and never enjoy it again. Realizing that consuming it means we will encounter a premature, and possibly painful death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must turn away from the things in this life that are poisoning us、and leading us toward both physical and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to look more deeply at the Greek.

<!-- p42 -->
The term used in 《Hebrews》 is *repentance*. This stands in contrast to the term *repent,* as seen in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach，saying: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
Repent（***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**）is a verb; it is an action or occurrence, meaning to change one’s mind or purpose；to turn away from sin and turn to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance（***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**）is a noun; it is a state of being in repentance, meaning the mind has been changed；a state of having turned away from sin and turned to God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says ‘repentance from dead works’ in 《Hebrews》, He is talking about being in a state of having already repented! We are to continually live in the state of having ‘turned away’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times do we need to repent? That’s a trick question! We need to keep changing our minds until we no longer do dead works. Repentance **is not asking for forgiveness, or apologizing**, though those may be involved. Repentance is turning away from the former way of thinking.

### System of Change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in the process of changing our minds. If we are not aware that we need to change our minds, then why would we open our eyes to look for change? Returning to the poison analogy, if you cannot realize that the poison is killing you, why would you stop ingesting it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that as long as a person hits ‘rock bottom’, they will examine their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us humans are more complex. There are many theories in psychological research on behavior change, and one of the most highly regarded is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** (Transtheoretical Model of Change), proposed by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente in the late 1970s**\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People may cycle through six stages in the process of change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation stage: No intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation stage: Intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation stage: Some steps toward change have already been taken.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action stage: Behavior has changed in the short term.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance stage: Behavior has changed and is being sustained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination stage: No longer desires to return to previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no fixed length for how long each stage may last. For some issues in life, some people never meaningfully progress through these stages. In other cases, we can also move very quickly from stage 1 to stage 6.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotion Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, A father suddenly feels guilty after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a parent who is *strict* but *loving*. He believes raising his voice is necessary. He is not considering change. In fact, he might even justify it- “This is how my dad taught me.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after working all day, he loses control of his emotions and yells harshly at his young daughter, louder and angrier than ever before. She flinches. His daughter’s lower lip quivers. She quietly walks away. In that moment… something **shifts**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. Her hurt expression plays in his mind over and over. His stomach churns. He tells himself, *“This is not the kind of person I want to be.”* He knows he needs to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes into her room, kneels down, sincerely apologizes, and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You didn’t deserve to be treated that way.”* She hugs him. To solidify this change, he prays and talks with a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he starts reading parenting books and sets about developing positive emotional self-regulation skills.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of his behavioral pattern. He is not perfect, but something really has changed. He no longer justifies the old behavior - and he has no desire to go back.

### Helping Others Change

<!-- p79 -->

We are also very likely to encounter those who long to change but don’t know what to do (**Preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
When Jesus was preaching to the crowds, He often encountered people who did not know they needed to change. We cannot forcibly change others’ minds, nor can we repent for them. That is not our duty, nor is it the example Jesus gave us.

### Godly Sorrow and the Goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although the so-called hitting rock bottom is more of a myth than a necessary prerequisite for change, God can use sorrow to influence our minds. God uses sorrow to lead us to repentance. He will convict us of our actions and the state of our lives and hearts, opening our eyes to see our complete, absolute need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (New King James Version)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>the sorrow or painful experiences that lead to *metanoia*</u>, is not the same as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is also the same sorrow or painful experiences, but the world offers no hope, only the promise of relief and of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and grief.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the endurance of pain - physical, emotional, psychological, or spiritual - especially when it lasts a long time or is intense.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is the multifaceted emotional response to a significant personal loss, especially on a relational or existential level.

<!-- p100 -->
Sorrow, suffering, and grief are closely related concepts. As human beings we experience all three, and we can learn and grow through them, but God does not bring death and destruction into our lives in order to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
The Bible says that every good and perfect gift is from above (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow can be uncomfortable, but His discipline is just and loving. Just as a good father would not deliberately break his own child’s arm to 'teach him a lesson,' God does not put sickness or disease on His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### Examples of worldly sorrow and godly sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy and his two-year-old sister are playing in the yard. He is playing with a stick and deliberately hits her, making her cry and leaving a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
Their father hears the commotion and comes to check. After learning what his son has done, he takes the stick, breaks it in two, and throws it away. He sends his son inside to think about what he has done and comforts his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
The father comes inside to deal with his son and finds him crying. When asked why he is crying, the son says, "Because you broke my stick."

<!-- p106 -->
The father replies, "What saddens me is that you care more about your stick than about the sister you hurt," and then sends him back to his room to continue reflecting.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later, his son comes out again to apologize. He says he is still sad because Dad broke his stick, and also because he hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example, the boy experienced worldly sorrow for his lost stick, and eventually godly sorrow for his injured sister.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow is connected to God’s goodness. God is a good Father who will allow us to experience the consequences of our actions, but as we walk through those consequences, He still stands beside us.

<!-- p111 -->
God’s relentless goodness—His willingness to redeem and restore us when we come to Him—is the positive driving force on our path of repentance. His goodness is not permission for us to continue sinning; rather, in spite of our sin, He makes a way for us to be redeemed and to experience His salvation.

People have a great capacity for change; it is written into our very nature. Humans are highly adaptable; we can learn and grow and find better ways of doing things. However, the capacity to change is not the same as the willingness to change or being persuaded to change.

### David’s Example of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no passage in Scripture presents a richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin in 2 Samuel 11-12. The story begins with King David staying in Jerusalem instead of going out to battle, where he saw Bathsheba bathing. Although he knew she was the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David nevertheless had her brought to him and slept with her, resulting in Bathsheba becoming pregnant.

<!-- p125 -->
To cover up his actions, David summoned Uriah back from the battlefield, hoping he would be with Bathsheba so it would appear that he was the child’s father. However, out of loyalty to his comrades, Uriah refused to go home to be with Bathsheba. So David arranged to have Uriah sent to the front lines, ultimately causing him to be killed in battle. After Uriah died, David quickly married Bathsheba to cover up the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was displeased with David’s actions and sent the prophet Nathan to confront him. When the prophet rebuked him, David immediately confessed, “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify his actions or make excuses. This confession was the first step in his repentance, directly acknowledging his transgression before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me,*” show that he takes full responsibility for his actions. He grieves not only over the sin itself but over having offended God, saying: “*Against you, you only, have I sinned.*” David exhibits a “*broken and contrite heart*,” which is an indispensable part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow is not superficial; he is deeply grieved over the sins he has committed and the harm they have caused. This depth of remorse shows that he longs not only for forgiveness but for a renewed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David prays to God: “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He seeks an inner change so that he will not repeat his mistakes. David does not merely apologize; he pleads for God to cleanse him, saying, “*Purge me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He recognizes that only God can cleanse his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s longing for spiritual renewal reflects his sincere commitment to walk in God’s ways going forward. He asks God for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>), indicating his hope to be aligned with God in every aspect of life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God forgave David’s sin, the Lord struck the child so that he became gravely ill. David fasted and prayed, pleading with God to spare the child’s life, but when the child died, David accepted the outcome (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51, we see both his personal prayer of confession and his public acknowledgment of his transgression. David’s decision to write down his plea for mercy resulted in one of the most enduring psalms in Scripture, showing his willingness to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm becomes a testimony to repentance and restoration for others to learn from, memorializing his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not merely feeling sorry; it was a profound change of heart and life. Through confession, acceptance, and the heart-change recorded in Psalm 51, David marked his sin as a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most important examples of genuine repentance in the Bible.
