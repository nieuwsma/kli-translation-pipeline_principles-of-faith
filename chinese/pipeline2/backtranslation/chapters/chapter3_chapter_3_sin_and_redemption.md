# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
The Bible tells us many stories about sin-its causes, consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin is to fall short of God’s standard, or ‘miss the mark,’ a term from archery describing an archer failing to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
The Old and New Testaments use various terms to describe sin, each term emphasizing aspects such as its type, severity, intent, victim, nature, and the punishment required. The general categories Scripture uses to describe sin include the following：

<!-- p3 -->
- Sins against God，sins against others，sins against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Willful sins，unintentional sins

<!-- p5 -->
- Momentary sins，habitual sins

<!-- p6 -->
- Sins requiring severe punishment such as the death penalty，sins requiring monetary restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In our modern criminal justice system there are similar distinctions：degrees of crimes，premeditated or crimes of passion，crimes requiring monetary restitution，or imprisonment, even the death penalty。An act may be both a crime and a sin, but crime is a human classification, whereas sin is God’s。

<!-- p8 -->
The many words in Greek and Hebrew for “sin” have a high degree of overlap。All sins can broadly be grouped under the general term “miss the mark,” Greek ***Hamartia***，Hebrew ***Chata’***。This umbrella term is used for the most serious sins, such as adultery and murder, as well as for lesser sins, for example showing partiality to the rich in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>)。

## Sin Requires Sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God killed an animal and made garments for them from its skin (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first shedding of blood to cover the consequences of sin. There are other similar accounts in Genesis, but it is in Leviticus that God formally instituted the way to offer sacrifices for sin.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, also called ***Olah***, was a general offering for atonement and dedication to God, often symbolizing complete surrender. This offering addressed sin in a broad sense, covering both known and unknown sins. The ritual involved presenting a male animal - usually a bull, sheep, goat, or bird - without blemish. The worshiper laid a hand on the animal’s head to identify with the offering, and then the entire animal was burned on the altar. Burning the whole offering represented a total commitment to the removal of sin and the alignment of one’s life with God. The burnt offering emphasized the seriousness of sin and the necessity of total consecration, expressing reverence and repentance by offering something precious.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, called ***Minchah***, was an offering of thanksgiving and dedication rather than directly for sin. It was often presented together with other offerings, such as the burnt offering and the peace offering, to express gratitude, worship, and consecration to God. Made of fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, the grain offering contained no blood. A portion of the flour was burned on the altar as a gift to God, and the remainder was given to the priests as food. Though not designed specifically for sin, the grain offering was an important component of corporate worship, symbolizing the worshiper’s devotion and gratitude for God’s provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship, symbolizing peace with God. It was often offered after atonement and propitiation, celebrating a restored relationship with God once sin had been dealt with. The worshiper could bring an unblemished bull, sheep, or goat, whether male or female. In the ritual, the fat portions - the best parts of the animal - were burned on the altar as God’s portion; the remaining meat was shared by the priests and the offerer in a communal meal. What made the peace offering distinctive was that it symbolized communion and fellowship, reflecting wholeness and unity with God. After atonement, this offering enabled the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was designated for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When an individual or the community became aware that they had sinned unintentionally, they could offer a sin offering for cleansing and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the status of the offerer: the priest or the whole congregation offered a bull, a leader offered a male goat, and an individual offered a female goat or a female lamb. The priest was to sprinkle the animal’s blood before the LORD and burn the fat on the altar; if it was a communal sin, the remainder of the animal was dealt with outside the camp. This offering indicated that even inadvertent sins are serious, and it provided a way for the people to remain clean and maintain a right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins involving breach of trust, harm to others, or desecration of holy things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins that demanded compensation, such as offenses involving property or violations against sacred things. When presenting this offering, the offender was to bring an unblemished ram as the sacrifice. If the sin involved harm to a person or property, the wrongdoer also had to repay the loss and add 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering emphasized the importance of acknowledging guilt and taking concrete action to make amends. It highlighted the relational and communal dimensions of sin, stressing that true atonement requires not only seeking God’s forgiveness but also setting right what has been wronged for those affected by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Collective Sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific offerings for individual sins, there were daily, weekly, and monthly offerings and contributions for the nation’s collective sin, but the most important among them was the **Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur), recorded in Leviticus 16, was an annual rite intended to cleanse all Israel from the collective sins of the past year. This unique ceremony took place once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, and addressed the sins of the nation rather than individual transgressions. It served to cleanse the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people, symbolizing atonement and cleansing for the entire community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the heart of the Day of Atonement were **two goats**, each representing sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest cast lots to determine each goat’s role. The first goat was designated “for the LORD,” offered as a sin offering, and its blood was brought by the high priest into the Most Holy Place. There, the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat - the cover of the ark - symbolizing atonement for the people’s sins and reconciliation with God. This act of sprinkling blood was profoundly significant, for it represented the cleansing of the sanctuary and ensured that God’s presence remained with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, called the **scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest would lay his hands on the scapegoat, confess the sins of all Israel, and thus transfer those sins onto this animal. Then this goat was sent into the wilderness to die alone, symbolizing that sin was completely removed from the camp. This visible act was a powerful portrayal of atonement and deliverance from sin, showing that God not only forgives but also removes the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God’s forgiveness and cleansing, allowing the nation each year to defer the consequences of sin and postpone judgment. This kind of **atonement was not a permanent pardon** for the nation’s sins; it only delayed God’s judgment.

## We Have All Sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The various books of the Bible place different emphases on the issue of sin. When Moses recorded the Law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he prescribed specific penalties for particular sins/offenses. Here I use “offense” and “sin” interchangeably, because the Law functioned as the legal system of Israel at the time.

<!-- p89 -->
In the Prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in terms of the fate of the entire nation. When the kings and the people rebelled against God, the Israelites were oppressed by neighboring nations, defeated in battle, and at times even taken into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs, the author writes to his son as a loving father, warning him of the personal dangers of sin and of a lifestyle that disobeys God’s commands. In the Psalms, many of David’s compositions deal with the personal and emotional consequences after sinning.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns people away from sin, calls individuals to repentance, and offers the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the apostle Paul profoundly explains how God planned to deal with sin and how we can be set free from its dominion.

<!-- p92 -->
The fact is, we are all guilty and must pay for our transgressions.

This false self-justification ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and offended, and the reality of a holy and perfect God. Scripture says that if we deny we have sin, we are lying, and the truth is not in us!
