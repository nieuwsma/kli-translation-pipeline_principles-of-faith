# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
The Scriptures tell us many stories about sin-its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin is to fall short of God's standard, or ‘miss the mark,’ an archery term describing an archer's failure to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
The Old and New Testaments use various terms for sin, each highlighting aspects like its type, its severity, intent, the victim, its nature, and the required punishment. There are several major categorizations that the Scriptures use to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against man, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Intentional sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- One-time sin, habitually committed sin

<!-- p6 -->
- Sin requiring a severe punishment such as death, sin requiring financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In our modern criminal justice systems, we make similar distinctions: degrees of criminality, premeditated crimes or crimes of passion, crimes requiring financial restitution, or incarceration, or even capital punishment. An act can be both a crime and a sin, but crime is man's categorization, sin is God's.

<!-- p8 -->
The various Greek and Hebrew words for sin show a strong overlap. All sin is generally described under the generic term ‘miss the mark,’ ***Hamartia*** in Greek, and ***Chata’*** in Hebrew. This generic term is used for the most serious sins, adultery, murder, but also for less serious sins, such as showing favoritism toward rich people in our Church assemblies (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## Sin requires a sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the account of Adam and Eve, God kills animals and makes garments for them from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first shedding of blood to cover the consequence of sin. There are a few other similar accounts in the book of Genesis, but God formally instituted a method of sacrifice for sin in the book of Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general offering for atonement and devotion to God, often symbolizing total surrender. This offering addressed sin in a broad sense, covering known and unknown sins. The rite involved the sacrifice of a male-typically a bull, a sheep, a goat, or a bird-without blemish. The one offering laid hands on the head of the animal, symbolizing identification with the sacrifice, then the entire animal was burned on the altar. This act of burning the entire offering represented a total consecration to the removal of sin and to aligning one’s life with God. The burnt offering emphasized the gravity of sin and the need for complete devotion, manifesting reverence and repentance through the giving of something precious.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, called ***Minchah***, was a sacrifice of thanksgiving and consecration rather than a direct offering for sin. This offering accompanied other sacrifices, such as the burnt offering and the peace offering, and was meant to express gratitude, worship, and devotion to God. Prepared from fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, the grain offering involved no blood. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar as a gift to God, while the rest was given to the priests for food. Although not specifically intended for sin, the grain offering was an important part of communal worship, symbolizing the offerer’s consecration and gratitude for God’s provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, emphasized fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This offering often followed sacrifices for sin and atonement, celebrating the restored relationship with God after sin had been dealt with. The offerer could bring a male or female animal without blemish, such as a cow, a sheep, or a goat. In the rite, the fatty portions-the best parts of the animal-were burned on the altar as God’s portion, while the remaining meat was shared by the priests and the offerer at a communal meal. The peace offering was unique because it symbolized fellowship and conviviality, reflecting a sense of wholeness and unity with God. After the atonement of sin, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and to celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was specifically designated for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When an individual or the community realized they had sinned unwittingly, they could offer a sin offering to seek purification and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the status of the person involved: a priest or the congregation offered a bull, a leader offered a male goat, and a private individual offered a female goat or lamb. The priest sprinkled the blood of the sacrifice before the Lord, burned the fat on the altar, and disposed of the rest of the animal outside the camp if it was a community sin. This offering demonstrated the seriousness of even unintentional sins and provided a way for the people to remain purified and in good relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, targeted sins involving a breach of trust, harm done to another, or the desecration of holy things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins requiring reparations, such as offenses against property or violations of sacred objects. To fulfill this offering, the sinner brought an unblemished ram to be sacrificed. If the sin involved harm to another person or to property, the offender also had to make restitution by returning the item plus an additional 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering emphasized the importance of acknowledging one’s guilt and taking concrete steps to correct the wrong. It highlighted the relational and communal nature of sin, stressing that true atonement required not only seeking God’s forgiveness, but also making amends to those who had been affected by the sin.

## Collective sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific offerings for individual sin, there were also sacrifices and offerings for the collective sin of the nation made daily, weekly, monthly, but the most important of them is the **Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16, designed to purify the entire Israelite community of its collective sins from the past year. This unique ceremony took place once a year, on the 10th day of the seventh month, and addressed the sins of the nation rather than individual transgressions. It served to purify the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people, symbolizing both atonement and purification for the whole community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the heart of the Day of Atonement were **two goats** used to represent both the sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat designated “for the Lord,” was sacrificed as a sin offering, and its blood was carried by the high priest into the Most Holy Place. There, the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat-the cover of the Ark of the Covenant-symbolizing the atonement of the people’s sins and their reconciliation with God. This act of sprinkling was deeply significant, for it represented the cleansing of the sacred space and ensured that God’s presence would remain with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, known as the **scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest laid his hands on the scapegoat, confessing the collective sins of Israel, thus transferring them to the animal. The goat was then sent into the wilderness to die alone, symbolizing the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act served as a powerful illustration of both atonement and deliverance from sin, showing that God not only forgave but also removed the sins of his people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God’s forgiveness and cleansing, allowing the nation to delay the consequence of sin and to push back judgment each year. This **atonement did not permanently forgive** the sins of the nation, but delayed God’s judgment.

## We have all sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible address the question of sin in different ways. When Moses recorded the law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he recorded specific penalties for specific sins/crimes. I use the word crime interchangeably here, because the books of the law constituted the legal system of the time for the Israelites.

<!-- p89 -->
In the prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in terms of the fate of the entire nation. When the king and the people disobeyed God, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, defeated in battle, and, in some cases, taken into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs, the author writes as a loving father to his son, warning him about the personal dangers of sin and of a lifestyle contrary to God’s commandments. In the Psalms, many of David’s writings deal with the personal and emotional consequences of having committed sin.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns against sin, calls people to personal repentance, and makes the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the apostle Paul presents a remarkable case for how God planned to deal with sin and how we can be freed from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is that we are all guilty of sin, and we must all pay the price for our transgression.

This false attempt to justify ourselves ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and sinned against, and ignores the reality of a holy and perfect God.
