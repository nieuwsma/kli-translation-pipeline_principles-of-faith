# Chapter 5 - Repentance from dead works

<!-- p1 -->
Very often, in Christianity, we consider repentance as fundamental only at the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The heart of repentance goes far beyond turning from sin for salvation. Repentance from dead works means living with our hearts turned away from the things that harm us or hinder our growth — and turned toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of the Epistle to the Hebrews places « repentance from dead works » as the first item in the list of foundational doctrines after the foundational principles of the Messiah (Chapter 4).

### Dead works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever been employed knows what work is. Work is the set of tasks you undertake to receive wages in order to make a living (employment). Employment is an agreement by which you exchange your talents and efforts for something of value that will help you subsist. You work because you get something out of it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important, it can bring meaning, often a sense of identity. It is very common to be asked ‘What do you do for a living ?’ and the answer begins with an identity statement ‘I am’. Although work can be enjoyable and meaningful, it is also very likely to be physically or mentally taxing, difficult, demanding, and stressful. If your job is none of those things, you might find it too menial and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work 40 hours per week and sleep 8 hours per night, you spend 36% of your waking hours working, and that excludes the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting, taking care of a household, and all the other necessary tasks you have to do to keep your life on track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work ? This varies, but, by definition, because we get something of value from it.

<!-- p13 -->
Would you keep working at a job if they stopped paying you ? For most of us, the easy and painfully obvious answer is a resounding NO!

<!-- p14 -->
Would you keep working at a job if it were killing you ? I suppose that depends on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
In context, ***nekros*** joined to ***erga*** denotes labor or work that is dead, has no vitality, cannot reproduce ! The author of the Epistle to the Hebrews warns us against labor that is dead.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, there are several criteria used to determine whether an organism is alive (this is a partial list for our purposes) :

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living beings convert energy through metabolic processes to grow, develop, and maintain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: Life involves growth and progression over time, with a specific life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living beings have the ability to reproduce, creating offspring or new entities.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adapt to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as temperature regulation or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living beings can adapt to their environments through genetic changes, allowing survival in various conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this standard of ‘life’ to our works with a bit of creative license, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, cannot produce a positive or desirable outcome.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God’s presence or the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Do not maintain internal stability, become deregulated, are not self-controlled.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (are too rigid) and no longer help us to survive.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s desire for us. God wants to bring us life!

<!-- p33 -->
While dead versus alive is binary. Dying is a gradient. Our dead works may not be completely dead in every category, not yet, but they will be! Dead works are like poisons, some have greater effects.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning normally. Some poisons have small effects, like a headache. Others affect the way we think, see, or even our ability to have children (to live fruitfully). Dead works are exactly like that. They may be only a headache, or they may cause death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are all the works that, upon examination and reflection, do not bring the abundant life that Jesus promised. Having an extramarital affair is a very powerful poison, it is likely to have a wholly negative effect fairly quickly. Being a needlessly stubborn and resentful person will slowly poison someone until they have driven everyone away from them.

<!-- p36 -->
To be delivered from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
To ‘repent of dead works’ is to turn to God and leave behind everything that harms our walk with Him—beginning with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Building on the analogy of poison for dead works, if you discovered that your favorite snack was full of toxic heavy metals that would give you a deadly condition if consumed, what would you do? Without hesitation, we would put it far from us, and never enjoy it again. Realizing that consuming it means our premature, and probably painful, death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must turn away from the things of this life that poison us and lead us to both our natural and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To understand this well, we need to look a little more deeply into the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The term used in Hebrews is *repentance*. This is contrasted with the term *to repent,* an example is found in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
To repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or an event; it means to change one’s mind or intention; to turn away from sin and turn to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is the state of having repented; it means having a change of mind; the state of having turned away from sin and turned to God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews ‘*repentance from dead works*,’ He is saying to be in the state of having repented! We must live in a continual state of having ‘turned away’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times must we repent? Trick question! We must continually change our way of thinking until we no longer do dead works. To repent is **not to ask for forgiveness, nor to apologize**, even if that may be involved. To repent is to turn away from the previous way of thinking.

### Systems of change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap when changing our minds. If we do not perceive the need to change our minds, then why would we ever open our eyes to look for change? Once again, to return to the analogy of poison, if you cannot perceive that the poison is killing you, why would you stop consuming it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people think a person just needs to ‘hit rock bottom,’ then they will look at their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that we humans are more complex. There are many theories related to behavior change in the study of psychology, one of the most recognized is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** Developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **[1].**

<!-- p62 -->
People can go through a six-stage cycle to change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: There is no intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: There is an intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken to change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has been changed for a short period.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has been changed and continues to be maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: There is no desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no set duration for the length of each stage. Some people, for certain aspects of their lives, never make meaningful progress through the stages. In other cases, we can move from stage 1 to 6 very quickly.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father experiencing a sudden conviction after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *firm* but *loving* parent. He believes that raising his voice is necessary. He is not considering changing. In fact, he might even justify it- “That’s how my father raised me.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, he snaps and yells at his young daughter very harshly, louder and angrier than ever. She flinches. Her lip quivers. She walks away silently. At that instant… something **shifts**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. The image of her hurt face plays over and over again in his mind. His stomach knots up. He says to himself, *“This is not the man I want to be.”* He knows he must do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He walks into her room, kneels, sincerely apologizes, and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You didn’t deserve that.”* She hugs him. Motivated to anchor the change, he prays and talks to a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day, he begins reading parenting books to work on developing positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He’s not perfect, but something has truly changed. He no longer justifies the old behavior-and he has no desire to go back to it.

### Helping Others Change

<!-- p79 -->

We will likely also encounter those who desperately want to change but do not know what to do (**preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
Jesus, while preaching to the crowds, often encountered people who did not know they needed to change. We cannot forcibly change someone’s mind or repent in their place. That is not our role, nor the example of Jesus.

### Godly sorrow and the goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although hitting bottom is more of a myth than a sure prerequisite for change, God can use sorrow to influence our minds. God will use sorrow to bring us to repentance. He will convict us about our actions and the state of our lives and our hearts to open our eyes to our complete and total need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>the experience of sorrow or distress that leads to a *metanoia*</u>, is not the same as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of sorrow or distress, but the world offers no hope, only relief and the promise of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and grief.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the endurance of pain-physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual-especially when it is prolonged or intense.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is a multifaceted emotional response to a significant personal loss, especially relational or existential.

<!-- p100 -->
Grief, suffering, and mourning are closely related concepts. In the human condition, we will experience all three, we can learn and grow through all three, but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
The Scriptures say that every good and perfect gift comes from above ( <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow will bring discomfort, but His discipline is just and loving. Just as a good father would not intentionally break his child’s arm to ‘teach him a lesson’, God will not inflict sickness or infirmity on His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### Example of worldly sorrow vs godly sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick and he hit her intentionally, which made her cry and left a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
Their father, hearing the commotion, investigated and, when he learned what his son had done, took the stick, broke it in two, and threw it away. He sent his son inside to think about what he had done and comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
The father, going inside to attend to his son, found him in tears. Asking him why he was crying, his son replied: “because you broke my stick”.

<!-- p106 -->
The father replied: “I am sad that you care more about your stick than about your sister whom you hurt,” and he sent him back to his room to think further.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later, his son returned and apologized. He said that he was still sad because dad had broken his stick, and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example, the boy experienced both worldly sorrow, for his lost stick, and finally godly sorrow, for his injured sister.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and the goodness of God are connected. God is a good Father who will let us experience the consequences of our actions, but He will remain by our side as we go through those consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
The unflinching goodness of God, His willingness to redeem and restore us if we come to Him, is a positive agent on our path of repentance. His goodness is not that we continue in sin, but, despite our sin, He opens a way for us to be redeemed and to experience His salvation.

<!-- p112 -->

At the opposite extreme, for some people, the simple example of sin and its consequences in someone else’s life is enough to lead them to change their way of thinking.

<!-- p114 -->
Humankind has a great capacity for change; it is inscribed in our very essence. Humans are highly adaptable, we can learn and grow, and find better ways of doing things. However, a capacity to change is not the same thing as a willingness or a disposition to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some people remain stuck and others are so ready to change, but I can say that the whole process of change truly comes from the grace of God.

### David’s Example of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no account in the Scriptures offers a richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin, reported in 2 Samuel 11-12. It begins when King David stays in Jerusalem instead of going to war, where he sees Bathsheba bathing. Although he knows she is the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David sends for her, and they have an affair, which results in Bathsheba’s pregnancy.

<!-- p125 -->
To cover his actions, David recalls Uriah from the battlefield, hoping he will spend time with Bathsheba and appear to be the father of the child. However, Uriah, out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, refuses to go home to Bethsheba. David then arranges to send Uriah to the front lines where he is eventually killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David hastily marries Bathsheba to conceal the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord is displeased with David’s actions and sends the prophet Nathan to confront him. Confronted by the prophet, David immediately admitted, “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify or excuse his actions. This confession was the first step of his repentance, acknowledging his fault directly before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I acknowledge my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me,*” show his full ownership of his actions. He expresses grief not only for the sin itself but also for how he offended God, saying, “*Against You, You only, have I sinned.*” David showed “*a broken and contrite heart,*” an essential part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he deeply mourned the sin he had committed and the harm he had caused. This depth of remorse was the sign that he wanted not only forgiveness but a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God to “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He sought an inner transformation that would prevent him from repeating the same mistakes. Rather than simply apologizing, David pleaded with God to cleanse him, saying, “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He recognized that only God could purify his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be spiritually renewed reflects a genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways in the future. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) demonstrates his hope to be aligned with God in every part of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God had taken away David’s sin, the Lord struck the child and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God to spare the child, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51, we find a personal prayer of repentance and a public acknowledgment of his transgressions. David’s decision to put his plea for mercy into writing, creating one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible, shows his willingness to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm has become a testimony of repentance and restoration from which others can learn, immortalizing his passage from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not merely a matter of remorse; it was a profound change in his heart and life. Through the confession, acceptance, and heart transformation recorded in Psalm 51, David turned his sin into a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most significant examples of authentic repentance in the Bible.
