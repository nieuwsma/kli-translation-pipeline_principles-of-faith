# Chapter 4 - Jesus-The Messiah

<!-- p1 -->
Building on our study of sin, we concluded that sin requires a serious price, against those against whom the sin is committed and who must be restored, and for those who commit the sin and must make restitution. The price of sin must be paid. In the Old Testament, God established a series of sacrifices carried out by priests, individuals, and communities to deal with, resolve, and judge sin.

<!-- p2 -->
Even with all the sacrifices for sin, they were still not sufficient-not because God failed, but because people kept choosing sin. If the sacrifice of animals could offer limited atonement, no amount of animal sacrifices was able to change the sinful nature of men.

<!-- p3 -->
God had a wonderful plan, the <u>Messiah</u> (in Hebrew ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) also known as <u>Christ</u> (in Greek ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**) meaning “The Anointed"; someone consecrated for a special role (e.g., king, priest, or deliverer).

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it is helpful to examine what the people of his time expected of the Messiah. These expectations shaped the way they saw Jesus and, in many cases, the reason why they struggled to recognize him as the fulfillment of God’s plan.

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical Context

<!-- p6 -->
The Jewish people had long awaited the coming of a Messiah. Jewish beliefs about the Messiah in the period just before Jesus were shaped by Old Testament prophecies, whose interpretations were diverse and not universally accepted. Most Jews expected the Messiah to be a political and military leader who would deliver Israel from foreign oppression (primarily Roman rule) and restore the Davidic kingdom. Here is an overview of common messianic expectations and how they aligned (or not) with biblical prophecy:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish expectations concerning the Messiah before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A political and military deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief :** The Messiah would be a king like David, leading Israel in a victorious revolt against foreign powers and restoring Israel’s independence.

<!-- p10 -->
- **Scriptures cited :** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 9:6-7</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JER" data-chapter="23" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Jeremiah 23:5-6</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ZEC" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Zechariah 9:9-10</span>.

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical context :** The Jews under Roman rule were oppressed and heavily taxed, which fueled the hope of a Messiah who would overthrow Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p12 -->
- **Truth :** Jesus is a King, but his kingdom is not earthly (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="36" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 18:36</span>). He established a spiritual kingdom at his first coming, but he will return as a conquering King to judge and reign with righteousness, as described in the book of Revelation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 19:11-16</span>).

<!-- p13 -->
### **2. A restorer of the Davidic kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief :** The Messiah would rebuild the Davidic dynasty and usher in a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace.

<!-- p15 -->
- **Scriptures cited :** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 7:12-16</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="AMO" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Amos 9:11</span>.

<!-- p16 -->
- **Interpretation :** The focus was often on national restoration rather than spiritual renewal.

<!-- p17 -->
- **Truth :** Jesus, as a descendant of David, fulfills this promise by establishing an eternal kingdom. Although this kingdom is spiritual in nature for now, it will culminate in a physical reign at his second coming (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="32-33" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:4-6</span>).

<!-- p18 -->
### **3. A prophet like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief :** Some Jews believed that the Messiah would be a prophet, like Moses, who would speak directly to God and on his behalf.

<!-- p20 -->
- **Scriptures cited:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="DEU" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Deuteronomy 18:15-19</span>.

<!-- p21 -->
- **Expectation:** Although many anticipated a prophetic figure, they often did not see this role as central for the Messiah but as an additional aspect.

<!-- p22 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus fulfilled this prophecy as the ultimate Prophet, proclaiming the Word of God and directly revealing the Father (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:18</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:10</span>). His prophetic role surpasses that of Moses, since he is the mediator of a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### **4. A ruler who would bring justice**

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would judge the wicked, uphold the righteous, and bring justice to the oppressed.

<!-- p25 -->
- **Scriptures cited:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 11:3-5</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="72" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 72:1-4</span>.

<!-- p26 -->
- **Interpretation:** The expectation was for a primarily political and social justice rather than a spiritual one.

<!-- p27 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus brings ultimate justice. At his first coming, he offered spiritual redemption and righteousness through the cross (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Colossians 2:13-15</span>). At his second coming, he will execute judgment on the wicked and establish perfect justice for all eternity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>).

<!-- p28 -->
### Various messianic beliefs

<!-- p29 -->
Just as Christendom today is divided into sects, denominations, or distinct belief systems, so too were the Jews of Jesus’ day divided. In the time of Jesus, four major Jewish sects shaped the religious and political landscape of Israel, each with distinct beliefs and expectations regarding the Messiah. Understanding some background about these different groups can help provide meaningful context for the study of the New Testament Scriptures.

<!-- p30 -->
The **Pharisees** were the most influential among the people, known for their strict observance of the Law (Torah) and oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection, life after death, and the existence of angels, emphasizing personal piety and obedience to God’s commandments. Their messianic expectation centered on a figure who would uphold the Law, bring justice, and restore Israel both politically and religiously.

<!-- p31 -->
The **Sadducees**, by contrast, were a wealthy and politically powerful group that controlled the Temple and the priesthood. They rejected oral traditions and accepted only the Torah as authoritative Scripture. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, focusing instead on maintaining their power and collaborating with Rome to ensure political stability. Consequently, they placed little emphasis on a Messiah, their chief concern being to preserve the status quo.

<!-- p32 -->
The **Essenes** were a separatist group that withdrew from mainstream Jewish society, living in isolated communities such as Qumran. They were deeply committed to ritual purity, apocalyptic expectations, and a disciplined way of life. The Essenes believed in two messianic figures: a priestly Messiah who would restore the holiness of the Temple and a royal Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness. This concept of a dual Messiah reflected their hope for spiritual and political renewal. The Essenes are not mentioned directly in the Scriptures.

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, the **Zealots** were militant nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent rebellion. They rejected Roman authority and were deeply committed to the restoration of Israel’s sovereignty. Their messianic expectation was of a warrior-king Messiah, in the likeness of David, who would lead them into battle against their oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p34 -->
While these groups focused on earthly expectations, the mission of Jesus revealed a far greater plan—one that addressed not only political oppression but also humanity’s spiritual enslavement to sin.

## The truth about the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### The anointing of Jesus

<!-- p37 -->
The Pharisees’ and Zealots’ vision of a Messiah reflects a recurring pattern in human history—seeking salvation by earthly means while rejecting God’s greater plan. As we have seen throughout the Scriptures, people often turn away from God—even though he is always faithful. Even when God is faithful and accepts us, he is often forgotten and rejected, leading to cycles of disobedience and decline that have plagued earthly kingdoms.

<!-- p38 -->
While many expected a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a far greater purpose for his anointing—a purpose that met spiritual and eternal needs. If Jesus had come as a conquering military leader, as the Jews hoped and expected, it would only have been a matter of time before the kingdom fell into decay because of human disobedience.

<!-- p39 -->
Instead, Jesus established a kingdom that cannot be shaken. His mission was not rooted in military might or political domination but in a divine purpose. As the Anointed One, Jesus explained what he was anointed for and how he would accomplish it:

<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16-21" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

>

>
> He was handed the book of the prophet Isaiah. When He had opened it, He found the place where it was written :
>
> "THE SPIRIT OF THE LORD IS UPON ME, BECAUSE HE HAS ANOINTED ME TO ANNOUNCE THE GOOD NEWS TO THE POOR; HE HAS SENT ME TO HEAL THE BROKENHEARTED, TO PROCLAIM LIBERTY TO THE CAPTIVES AND RECOVERY OF SIGHT TO THE BLIND, TO SET AT LIBERTY THOSE WHO ARE OPPRESSED;

Jesus was anointed-set apart by God-and sent with a divine mission, full of purpose and power.

<!-- p44 -->
Jesus proclaimed:

<!-- p45 -->
- The good news (the gospel) of the Kingdom.

<!-- p46 -->
- Deliverance - pardon, remission of sin, freedom.

<!-- p47 -->
- The year of grace (season) of the Lord.

<!-- p48 -->
The people to whom He came were:

<!-- p49 -->
- Brokenhearted - emotionally and spiritually devastated.

<!-- p50 -->
- Blind - spiritually, and sometimes physically.

<!-- p51 -->
- Poor - destitute, in poverty (spiritual and/or physical).

<!-- p52 -->
- Captives - meaning 'taken with a spear', representing those held in bondage against their own will.

<!-- p53 -->
Jesus transformed these broken people by:

<!-- p54 -->
- By restoring sight

<!-- p55 -->
- By placing them in a state of freedom

<!-- p56 -->
- By binding up, by healing, by restoring

<!-- p57 -->
The purpose of the ministry and service of Jesus was both individual and global, immediate and lasting. His purpose does not have a single focus but is part of God’s divine plan to reconcile humanity with God. After sin entered the world, it caused a division between God and humanity. God had to banish man from the garden and from His presence. Throughout the Old Testament, man could be with God, but only partially. It was very rare for the Spirit of God to be given to men, and for the most part men lived isolated from God. He chose for Himself a people from among the Jews, but He wanted a permanent, sure, and complete way to restore His relationship with humanity.

<!-- p58 -->
The presence of God on the earth at the time of Jesus was for the most part limited to the innermost part of the temple, the Most Holy Place. There, behind the thick veil that separated the Most Holy Place from the Holy Place, the presence of God rested by the ark of the covenant. Man was isolated from God, and God was separated from His creation.

<!-- p59 -->
God sent Jesus to repair, rebuild, restore, and reconcile humanity with Him. (2 Corinthians 5:17-21)

<!-- p60 -->
### The Necessary Work of Jesus

<!-- p61 -->
To fully reconcile humanity with God, the Messiah’s mission required not only proclamation and healing, but also the ultimate act of atonement. Jesus had to become the perfect and complete sacrifice, through his death on the cross.

<!-- p62 -->
The Epistle to the Hebrews explains in detail, over the course of its 13 chapters, how Jesus became the perfect sacrifice, and was both the offering for sin and the high priest who offers it (Hebrews 3 &amp; 4). Jesus’ priesthood is not of the Levitical lineage (from the tribe of Levi), but according to the order of Melchizedek, of whom it is said that he has neither beginning nor end, which means he is not constrained by the patterns and habits of men (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 7:3</span>). The limitation of the Levitical priesthood is that it relies on the blood of bulls and goats (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:12-13</span>), whose sacrifice is insufficient for a permanent and lasting remission of sins. According to our study on sin and sacrifice, we know that the consequence of the people’s sins could be postponed for a year (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:3</span>) on the Day of Atonement, but that this sacrifice had to be constantly renewed.

<!-- p63 -->
Jesus, in dying on the cross (accounts of which are found in Matthew 27, Mark 15, John 19, and Luke 23), shed his blood and paid for the sins, past, present, and future, of all humanity. Isaiah 53 reveals Jesus as the suffering servant, who took upon himself the full weight of punishment and anguish. His once-for-all death became the final and ultimate sacrifice. Jesus is the Lamb of God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="29" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:29</span>), without spot or wrinkle (sin); because he was perfect, complete, and holy, the shedding of his innocent blood made atonement possible. **No further sacrifice is necessary or acceptable. The former ones were only temporary - but that of Jesus was complete and final.**

<!-- p64 -->
Through death and resurrection, we have a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6</span>), sealed by the blood of God’s own Son; Jesus is the mediator of the covenant, he is its intermediary. By the sacrifice of Christ, we receive salvation, and he is the author of the *‘eternal salvation to all who obey him*’ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 5:9</span>).

<!-- p65 -->
The word salvation in Greek is **Sōtēria** (*so-tay-REE-ah - **G4991)**.* It primarily means **salvation, deliverance, or preservation**. It encompasses the act of being saved, both in the spiritual sense (eternal salvation through Christ) and in physical contexts (rescue from danger or harm). The term carries the idea of safety, wholeness, and restoration.

<!-- p66 -->
**Salvation or deliverance:**

<!-- p67 -->
- Refers to the deliverance of humanity from sin and its consequences by God, through Jesus Christ.

<!-- p68 -->
- Used in contexts of eternal salvation (justification, sanctification, and glorification).

<!-- p69 -->
**Preservation:**

<!-- p70 -->
- Implies being kept safe, protected, or preserved from danger.

<!-- p71 -->
**Wholeness or healing:**

<!-- p72 -->
- Includes restoration to a state of integrity or soundness.

<!-- p73 -->
Jesus’ sacrifice paid the price for us to be forgiven and opened a way for us to have a relationship with God, establishing a path to abide with him that depends only on our obedience, not on our perfection nor on our ability to refrain from sin.

<!-- p74 -->
When we accept Jesus, by faith (see later), we receive his salvation. This salvation connects us to God and records our name in the ***Lamb’s Book of Life*** (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>). The Gospel of John says:

<!-- p75 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p76 -->
> **<u>John 3:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life.
>

<!-- p77 -->
</span>

<!-- p78 -->
To perish means to be spoiled — like a fruit that rots before being enjoyed. It is a life that misses what it was made for. Jesus came to give us perpetual, or eternal, life, and to keep us from perishing. Eternal life is not a life that begins after our death, it begins the moment we accept Jesus as our salvation, when we are born again.

### Born Again

<!-- p80 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

When we receive Jesus, we become “born again.” This expression is used by Jesus to describe a transition point, where we are born of the Spirit (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:1-15</span>). This happens by faith — choosing to trust Him fully, like entering into a marriage covenant, with the heart and with words.

<!-- p84 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

When we were born on the earth (born of the flesh and of water), many things happened: we took our first breaths and were declared alive, we were welcomed by our parents, received a name, became heirs, received food and protection, and continued to grow.

<!-- p88 -->
When we put our trust in Jesus and receive Him, we are born again, not of the flesh, but of the Spirit. We become spiritually alive! As with physical birth, many elements accompany spiritual birth:

<!-- p89 -->
1.  When we accepted Jesus, God wiped away our sins and declared us forgiven (**justified**). He gave us a new start. The wall of separation between us and Him is removed. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="TIT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Titus 3:5</span>)

2.  We were **regenerated** spiritually and received the indwelling of the Holy Spirit. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="1CO" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Corinthians 6:19</span>).

<!-- p91 -->
3.  We were **adopted** into the family of God and became heirs. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 1:5</span>)

<!-- p92 -->
4.  We receive a source of spiritual nourishment and eternal protection. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="1PE" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Peter 2:2</span>)
