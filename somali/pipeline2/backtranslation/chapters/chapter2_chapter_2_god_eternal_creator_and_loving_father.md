# Chapter 2 - God-The Everlasting Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is essential that we build a strong foundation of knowledge about who God is. We can understand by learning:

<!-- p2 -->
- His will, his purposes, and his actions,

<!-- p3 -->
- His nature, his character, and his attributes,

<!-- p4 -->
- What is said about him and what he says about himself.

<!-- p5 -->
Scripture specifically reveals the attributes of God, with each part deepening our understanding of the unchanging, eternal God.

<!-- p6 -->
To sum up God in a single attribute is a difficult task, if not impossible, for he is far too vast and multifaceted to be simplified. Although we can, and will, make great efforts to explain, describe, and portray God, ultimately, like art and music, he must be encountered to be understood.

<!-- p7 -->
When a person listens to music, they describe it by expressing how it moves them-how it sounds within, beyond merely the notes and melodies. A melody can carry warmth or strength, with rhythm beating like a heartbeat or crescendos creating feelings of exhilaration or longing. Music can soothe like a gentle wave or rouse like a storm, lifting the listener’s mood, grounding their thoughts, or transporting them somewhere else entirely. This experience is far more than a sequence of sounds; it is the soul of the music that remains, captured in how it shapes the listener’s emotions and personal journey.

<!-- p8 -->
Similarly, a person viewing a painting looks beyond the colors or brushstrokes, exploring what those elements reflect. A landscape can evoke a serene escape, while abstract art can stir curiosity or personal reflection. Certain shades can bring warmth, while composition can create a sense of calm, chaos, or beauty. The painting becomes a living experience, prompting a shift in perspective or a profound emotional response that words alone cannot capture. Thus, music and art become more than their physical form-they become encounters that touch something fundamental, transforming the listener or viewer in deeply personal ways.

## Exploring God through the Old Testament

<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: The God Who Dwells with His Creation

<!-- p11 -->
A central theme in God’s story is his desire to dwell with his creation. This is clearly seen in the narratives in Genesis. Within its fifty chapters and genealogies beginning with Adam (the first man) to Joseph (who is the last major figure in the book of Genesis, the last significant leader of God’s people before Moses), there is a span of approximately ~2,400 years. In these stories we see the constancy of God’s goodness despite human evil.

<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God establishes relationships with chosen individuals.

<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - The first humans, created by God, and placed in the Garden of Eden. God walked with them and spoke to them, giving instructions and building a mutual relationship of love and obedience. However, that relationship was affected by their disobedience when they ate from the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil (Genesis 1-3).

<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - Although Cain ultimately committed the murder of his brother Abel, God spoke to him directly, warning him of the danger of sin and giving him guidance before the killing. Even after Cain’s punishment, God set a mark for him to protect him from harm (Genesis 4).

<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - God formed a special relationship with Noah, a righteous man, at a time when corruption was very widespread. God commanded Noah to build an ark to save his family and the animals from the coming flood. Afterwards, God made a covenant with Noah, promising not to destroy the earth again with a flood, and set the rainbow as the sign of that covenant (Genesis 6-9).

<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - One of the most important relationships in Genesis, God called Abram to leave his homeland and promised to make him the father of a great nation. God made a covenant with Abram, promising land, offspring, and blessings for all nations through him. His faith and obedience become central themes, and his name was changed to Abraham as part of the covenant (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22).

<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - An Egyptian slave who served Sarah, Hagar encountered God in the wilderness after being mistreated. God comforted her, promised a future for her son Ishmael, and instructed her to return to Abraham and Sarah. Hagar gave God the name “El Roi” (“The God who sees me”), recognizing his care (Genesis 16).

<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - The son of Abraham and Sarah, Isaac inherits his father’s covenant promises. God confirms these promises directly to Isaac, including the promise of offspring and blessings. God also guided Isaac during a famine, protecting him and strengthening the covenant relationship (Genesis 26).

<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - Although her relationship with God was indirect, God answered Rebekah’s prayer regarding her difficult pregnancy, revealing that her twins, Jacob and Esau, would become two nations with different destinies (Genesis 25).

<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God forms a personal relationship with Jacob, appearing to him in a dream and confirming the covenant promises of land, offspring, and blessings. Jacob encounters God several times, including the famous wrestling match where he wrestled, where God changed his name to Israel, symbolizing his transformation and the founding of Israel as a people (Genesis 28, 32, 35).

<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Although Joseph’s relationship with God is not based on direct appearances or covenants like those of Abraham or Jacob, God is deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, divine favor, and God’s guidance, God directs Joseph’s journey from being sold into slavery to becoming a powerful leader in Egypt. Joseph’s faith in God’s sovereignty allows him to forgive his brothers, recognizing that God used the suffering he endured for good (Genesis 37, 39-50).

<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this whole story God, desiring to dwell with His creation, was compelled to dwell with a particular chosen group, and ultimately, by making a covenant with Abraham, He established a nation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="17" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 17:7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: The God Who Dwells with His People

<!-- p24 -->
God’s desire to dwell with His people is also clearly seen in the second book of the Bible, Exodus. In the book of Exodus, the people of Israel, who had for a time lived in Egypt in peace and harmony, are now oppressed by a Pharaoh who did not know Joseph and feared the Hebrews (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 1:6-8</span>). God’s people lived in Egypt for about ~400 years (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="40-41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 12:40-41</span>), the latter part of which was spent in slavery (there is no universal consensus precisely defining how long Israel was in Egyptian bondage).

<!-- p25 -->
In the powerful presence of the burning bush that was not consumed, God revealed Himself to a man born a Hebrew, raised in Egypt, a murderer in exile, tending his father-in-law’s flock in the wilderness, Moses.

<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, 'I AM has sent me to you.' "
>

<!-- p28 -->
</span>

<!-- p29 -->
The phrase “I AM WHO I AM” (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is one of the most profound self-revelations in Scripture. When Moses asks for God’s name, God answers with this phrase, which can be translated "I am who I am," "I will be who I will be," or simply "I am." On the surface this statement may seem a bit perplexing, but it carries deep meaning. Can you imagine introducing yourself to someone new as ‘I am’? When God reveals Himself as ‘I AM WHO I AM’ He bears witness to His nature, His eternal existence, and His supremacy. ‘I am’ is limitless in meaning and intent.

<!-- p30 -->
God uses Moses to reveal Himself to Pharaoh:

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Exodus 7:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And you shall say to him, 'The Lord God of the Hebrews has sent me to you, saying, "Let My people go, that they may serve Me in the wilderness"; but indeed, until now you would not hear!
>

<!-- p33 -->
</span>

<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh, God is ‘I AM’ and God demands that the Israelites be released so that they may worship Him in the wilderness.

<!-- p35 -->
Plagues and suffering follow, but eventually Pharaoh relents and allows the Israelites to depart.

<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="31" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p37 -->
> **<u>Exodus 12:31</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Then he called for Moses and Aaron by night, and said, "Rise, go out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel. And go, serve the Lord as you have said.
>

<!-- p38 -->
</span>

<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness God miraculously delivers His people from the vengeful Egyptian army (Exodus 14), He gives them protection from the sun and a pillar of fire to warm them during the cold nights of the wilderness (Exodus 13), food from heaven (Exodus 16), and water to drink (Exodus 17).

<!-- p40 -->
God brings His people to Mount Sinai, where God commands Moses to consecrate the people and gives him the Law (including the Ten Commandments), establishing His covenant with them (the Old Covenant) (Exodus 19, 20, 24). This covenant (the Mosaic Covenant) is built upon the Abrahamic Covenant and continued from Moses to Jesus.

<!-- p41 -->
Although He established a covenant with His people, the relationship is between a loving, jealous, pursuing God who is with a very stiff-necked and ungrateful people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="32" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 32:9</span>). Ignoring God’s miraculous interventions, they often display a rebellious attitude. God even brought them to the Promised Land and told them they could inherit it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="33" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 33:3</span>), but due to fear and disobedience they disregarded God’s supernatural deliverance; instead of taking it, they had to wander in the wilderness for 40 years until nearly that entire generation died (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-34" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).

<!-- p42 -->
### The Rest of the Old Testament

<!-- p43 -->
For nearly 1,400 years between the death of Moses and the birth of the Messiah, the Israelites experienced a recurring cycle: faithfulness leading to God’s favor and blessings, then disobedience, God’s judgment, their repentance, and finally God’s deliverance. This pattern is especially seen in the books of **Judges, Kings, and Chronicles**, which recount cycles of the people turning away from God, often worshiping other gods, resulting in periods of oppression or exile. When they cry out, God repeatedly raises up judges, prophets, or leaders to deliver them.

<!-- p44 -->
Throughout these years, although the Israelites often failed to keep their covenant with Him, God remained faithful, demonstrating both justice and mercy. He continually delivers and forgives His people, revealing His commitment and enduring love.

<!-- p45 -->
As we see in Scripture, God reveals Himself through actions, relationships, and even names that speak to His character. Each name represents a particular aspect of His complete nature. These names are given within the context of a story that shows how God revealed Himself to His people. For your own study, see the Appendix: *Table 10 - The Names of God in the Old Testament.* Read these names and study the Scripture references. Each reference is a story that accurately shows how God saved, provided for, and delivered His people.

<!-- p46 -->
Also in the Appendix is an additional collection of attributes that describe His character, providing explanations of His actions and purposes: *Table 11 - The Attributes of God.* These attributes, though a bit more abstract than the names of God, are no less true!

<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand God—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (**the Trinity**)—working together as one. In our continuing study we will look more deeply at the names and attributes of Jesus and the Holy Spirit.

<!-- p48 -->
The character and actions of God are fully revealed in the unity of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. Scripture teaches that God is essentially one, yet there are three distinct Persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Scripture affirms God’s oneness, saying, “There is no other God besides Me” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="45" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 45:5</span>). Nevertheless, the Godhead is understood as a composite unity—three distinct Persons cooperating in one mind and purpose, united in the truest sense. Each Person of the Trinity has a particular role: **the Father creates**, **the Son redeems**, **the Holy Spirit sanctifies**. The word “Trinity” does not appear in the Bible; it became a theological expression used to describe this concept, which Scripture illuminates.

<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can be illustrated by natural analogies that help clarify the idea of unity in diversity. Like water, which can be liquid, ice, or vapor, yet is still H₂O, each Person of the Trinity is fully God while personally distinct. Or consider a triangle, with three sides and three angles, forming one complete shape; if one side is removed, it is no longer a triangle. Likewise, **God’s nature is not complete without these three Persons**, who work together perfectly in unity.

<!-- p50 -->
- **God the Father**: He is the Creator and sustainer of all things, often understood as the source within the Godhead. His love for humanity is central to the biblical story, as shown in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:16</span>. In our study we will consider God the Father as a loving Father and a righteous Judge, who made the way for our redemption.

<!-- p51 -->
- **The Son of God**: Of the same essence and eternally equal with the Father, Jesus took on human nature, being conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the virgin Mary. He lived a sinless life, died to redeem humanity, and rose again, conquering sin and death. He now sits at the Father’s right hand, interceding for us (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In our study we will consider Jesus as the perfect sacrifice for sin, the risen Lord, and the head of the church.

<!-- p52 -->
- **God the Holy Spirit**: The Holy Spirit, co-existent and equal with the Father and the Son, was sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world of sin and to prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. He empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In our study we will consider the Holy Spirit as Counselor and Helper, whom God has given to equip us, strengthen us, sanctify us, and be with us.

<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct Persons, the triune nature of God is revealed in perfect unity of purpose, will, and love, and each Person plays an indispensable role in the divine mission of creation, redemption, and sanctification. The Trinity invites believers to experience God fully—as a loving Father, a redeeming Savior, and a transforming Spirit.

<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion

<!-- p55 -->
Our names can set us apart: spouse, father, son, brother, friend. Each is a statement of what we are in relation to someone else. We can also be known by attributes: faithful, patient, compassionate, intelligent, humorous. Likewise, we can know God by His attributes and His names—He is our Healer, our Provider, our Shepherd, and our Redeemer. Yet, just as labels and words capture only a small part of us, to be truly known we must be experienced. Even more so with God. To know Him truly and fully, He must be experienced.

<!-- p56 -->
The names of God reveal His character, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of what these names mean. For example, “my God the Healer” takes on deeper meaning when we grasp the depths of illness, despair, or weakness from which God has delivered us. Knowing Him as Provider is transformed when we have felt real need and have found Him faithful.

<!-- p57 -->
Scripture provides the context for these encounters, showing us God’s faithfulness to His people in every time and circumstance, and reinforcing that His names and attributes are not merely titles but realities He brings into life, to be experienced in the lives of individuals. It is through the interplay of personal experience and the scriptural story that we come to know God—not as a distant description, but as a continual, dynamic relationship in every season of our lives.
