# Chapter 5 - Repentance from dead works

<!-- p1 -->
We often see repentance in Christianity as foundational only to the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The heart of repentance goes much deeper than turning away from sin with regard to salvation. Repentance from dead works means a life in which our hearts turn away from the things that harm us or hinder our growth—and turn toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews places ‘repentance from dead works’ as the first item in the list of foundational doctrines after the elementary principles of Christ (Chapter 4).

### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever been employed knows what work is. Work is the activities you do to receive wages in order to earn a living (employment). Employment is an agreement in which you exchange your gifts and efforts for something of value that supports you. You work because you get something out of it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can bring a sense of purpose, and often a sense of identity. It’s very common to be asked, ‘What do you do in life?’ and the answer begins with an identity statement, ‘I am’. Although work can be enjoyable and purposeful, it is also very likely to be physically or mentally demanding, heavy, challenging, and stressful. If your job is none of these things, you might see it as too easy and quite boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work a 40-hour job per week, and you sleep 8 hours a night, you spend 36% of your waking hours working, which does not include the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting to work, taking care of a home, and all the other activities necessary to keep your life on the right track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? It varies, but by definition the reason is that we obtain something of value from it.

<!-- p13 -->
Would you continue working at a job if they stopped paying you? For most of us, the simple and painfully obvious answer is, NO!

<!-- p14 -->
Would you continue a job if it were killing you? I suppose it depends on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
The contextual meaning of ***nekros*** when coupled with ***erga*** is work or deeds that are dead, inactive, unproductive! The author of Hebrews is warning us about dead work.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, there are several criteria for identifying that an organism is alive (this is a partial list used for our purposes):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living things convert energy through metabolic processes in order to grow, develop, and maintain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and Development**: Life involves growth and development over time, with a distinct life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living things are capable of reproducing, generating offspring or new individuals.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound), and can adapt to environmental changes.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: A living organism maintains internal stability, such as regulating temperature or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living things can adapt to their environments by making genetic changes, which allows them to continue living in various conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this criterion of ‘life’ to our deeds using a little creativity, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow growth or personal development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, and cannot produce a positive or desired outcome.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God’s presence or His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Do not maintain internal stability; they just deteriorate and are not self-regulating.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (they are very rigid) and no longer help us to live.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s will for us. God wants to give us life!

Although dead and alive are a binary. Death has different degrees. Our dead works may not yet be fully dead in every part, but they will be! Dead works are like poison; some have a strong effect.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning normally. Some poisons have a minor effect, like a headache. Others affect how we think, see, or even whether we bear children (that we live fruitfully). Dead works are the same; they may be only a headache, or they may cause death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are anything which, when reviewed and considered, do not bring about the abundant life that Jesus promised. Entering into sexual immorality outside of marriage is a very strong poison; it is likely to quickly have a fully negative effect. Being an unnecessarily rigid and bitter person slowly poisons one until everyone withdraws from them.

<!-- p36 -->
To escape dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
‘Repenting from dead works’ is to turn to God and leave behind anything that harms our walk with Him - beginning with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Building further on the analogy of poison for dead works, if you found out that your favorite snack was full of toxic heavy metals and, if eaten, would lead to a lethal condition, what would you do? We, without any doubt, would distance ourselves from it, and never again enjoy it. Knowing that eating it would mean a death we hasten toward, and in the middle of it may be very painful.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must turn away from the things in this life that are poisoning us and leading us to both physical and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to look a little more deeply into the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The word used in Hebrews is *repentance*. This is compared with the word *repent,* an example of which is found in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
Repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or event; it means to change one’s mind or intention; to turn away from sin and turn to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is the state of having repented; it means having undergone a change of mind; the state of having turned from sin and turned to God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews ‘*repentance from dead works*’ He is saying to be in a state of having repented! We must live in a continual state of being ‘turned away’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times do we need to repent? Trick question! We need to continually change our thinking until we stop doing dead works. Repentance is not **asking for forgiveness, or apologizing**, although that may be involved. Repentance is turning away from the previous way of thinking.

### Processes of Change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in changing our thinking. If we do not feel a need to change our thinking, then why would we open our eyes to seek change? Again, let us return to the example of poison: if you do not know that the poison kills you, why would you stop consuming it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that a person has to reach ‘rock-bottom’, then he will look at his life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that human beings are more resilient than that. There are many theories related to behavior change in the study of psychology; one that is widely respected is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change**. It was developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People can go through a cycle of six stages of change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: No intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: There is an intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken toward change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has been changed for a short period.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has been changed and is being sustained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: There is no desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no fixed time indicating how long each stage might take. Some, when it comes to certain aspects of their lives, never meaningfully move through the stages. At other times, we can move quickly from stage 1 to 6.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father feeling sudden remorse after he yelled at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a firm but loving parent. He believes that raising his voice is necessary. He is not considering change. He can even excuse it— “That’s how my father raised me.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day of work, he boils over and shouts at his little daughter, louder and angrier than before. She is startled. Her lips tremble. She moves quietly and leaves. In that moment… something changes.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, astonished. The image of her hurt face returns to his mind again and again. His stomach is in turmoil. He says to himself, *“That is not the person I want to be.”* He knows he needs to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes into her room, kneels, offers a sincere apology, and says, *“Dad was wrong. You did not deserve that.”* She hugs him. Encouraged to strengthen the change, he prays and speaks with a friend to get support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he begins to read parenting books to work on improving his own positive emotional regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He is not perfect, but something has truly changed. He no longer excuses the old behavior—and he has no desire to return to it.

### Godly Sorrow and the Goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although reaching the lowest point is more than a riddle or a story that is told and is not a guaranteed condition for change, God can use sorrow to affect our minds. God will use sorrow to bring us to repentance. He will rebuke us for our actions and the state of our lives and hearts in order to open our eyes so that we may see our complete need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or an experience of grief or desolation that leads to a *metanoia*, is not the same as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of grief or desolation, but the world offers no hope; it has only relief and a promise of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and mourning.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the endurance of pain—physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual—especially when it is prolonged or very severe.

<!-- p99 -->
Mourning is a multifaceted emotional response arising from a significant personal loss, especially one related to a relationship or existence.

<!-- p100 -->
Grief, suffering, and mourning are concepts very close to each other. In the human condition we will encounter all three, we can learn from and grow through all three, but God does not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
Scripture says every good and perfect gift comes from the God above (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow brings discomfort, but His discipline is justice and love. Just as a good father would not deliberately break his child's arm to 'teach a lesson,' likewise God does not put pain or disease on His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### Example: Worldly Sorrow vs Godly Sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick, and he deliberately hit her with it, which caused her to cry and left a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
When their father heard the commotion, he investigated; when he discovered what his son had done, he took the stick, broke it, and threw it away. He sent his son inside to think about what he had done, and he comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came inside to deal with his son, he found him crying. When he asked why he was crying, his son said, “Because you broke my stick.”

<!-- p106 -->
The father's reply was, “I’m sad that you’re more concerned about the stick than your sister whom you hurt,” and then he sent him back to his room to think further.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later his son came out and asked for forgiveness. He said, “I’m still sad because Dad broke my stick, and also because I hit my sister.”

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example the boy experienced both worldly sorrow, over the stick he lost, and eventually godly sorrow, over the harm he caused his sister.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and His goodness are connected. God is a good Father who allows us to face the consequences of our actions, and yet He still stands with us as we walk through those consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
The tireless goodness of God, His readiness to forgive us and restore us if we come to Him, is a positive spur along our path of repentance. His goodness does not mean that we continue in sin, but even though our sins exist, He makes a way for us to be forgiven and to taste His salvation.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a great capacity for change; it is woven into our very nature. People are highly adaptable; they can learn and grow, and they can find better ways to do things. However, the capacity for change is not the same as readiness or willingness to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I don’t know why some people get stuck while others are very ready for change, but I can say that the process of change truly comes from the grace of God.

### David’s Example of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no story in Scripture offers a richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin in 2 Samuel 11–12. It begins with King David staying in Jerusalem instead of going to war, where he saw Bathsheba bathing. Although he knew she was the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David sent for her, and they were together, which resulted in Bathsheba’s pregnancy.

<!-- p125 -->
To cover his actions, David brought Uriah back from the battle, hoping he would spend time with Bathsheba and appear to be the child’s father. However, Uriah, out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, refused to go to his house and be with Bathsheba. Then David arranged for Uriah to be sent to the front lines, where he was ultimately killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David quickly married Bathsheba to conceal the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was displeased with David’s actions, and He sent the prophet Nathan to speak to him. When the prophet confronted him, David immediately confessed, “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to argue or excuse his actions. This confession was the first step of his repentance, directly acknowledging his wrong to God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I acknowledge my transgressions, and my sin is always before me,*” show full responsibility for his actions. He expressed sorrow, not only for the sin itself but also for how it offended God, saying, “*Against You, You only, have I sinned.*” David displayed a “*broken and contrite heart,*” an essential part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he deeply grieved the sin he committed and the harm it caused. The depth of this contrition signaled that he sought not only forgiveness but also a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God, “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He sought an inner transformation that would prevent him from returning to the same mistakes. Rather than mere appeasement, David pleaded with God to cleanse him, saying, “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He recognized that only God could cleanse his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire for spiritual renewal reflects a genuine commitment to continue walking in God’s ways. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) shows his hope to align with God in every part of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God took away David’s sin, the Lord struck the child, and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God for the child to be spared, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find a personal prayer of repentance and a public confession of his transgressions. David’s decision to write his plea for mercy, creating one of the most enduring Psalms in the Bible, shows his willingness to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony of repentance and restoration from which others can learn, reminding them of his journey from contrition to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance did not stop at sorrow; it was a profound transformation of his heart and life. Through confession, acceptance, and the heart change inscribed in Psalm 51, David turned his sin into a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most important examples of true repentance in the Bible.
