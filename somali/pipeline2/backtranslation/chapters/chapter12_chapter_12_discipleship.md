# Chapter 12 - Discipleship

<!-- p1 -->
Discipleship means following Jesus, learning from Him, and becoming more like Him in how we live and love. It involves a lifelong journey of obedience, transformation, and service, guided by the Holy Spirit and grounded in the Word of God.

<!-- p2 -->
Discipleship is the central command of the Great Commission. Jesus defined this task:

<!-- p3 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p4 -->
> **<u>Matthew 28:18-20</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And Jesus came and spoke to them, saying, "All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth.
>
> Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit,
>
> teaching them to observe all things that I have commanded you; and behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the age." Amen.
>

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

<!-- p6 -->
We, His followers, are commanded to “go and make disciples of all nations.” Discipleship is about personal growth and multiplying that growth by teaching others to follow Christ. Discipleship is the vehicle by which we build the Kingdom of God; the teaching of Jesus is the message.

<!-- p7 -->
The call to be a disciple is more than attending church or studying Bible verses—it is an invitation to live like Jesus and to unite with Him in advancing the Kingdom of God. True discipleship helps believers become spiritually mature and effective for ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:13</span>). It shapes the character and skills needed to serve people and to share the Gospel. As Paul instructed Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, discipleship follows a pattern of spiritual multiplication, where trained disciples raise up new followers to lead others to Christ.

<!-- p8 -->
Discipleship is both a personal calling and a global mission. At a personal level, it involves growing in faith, cultivating a vibrant spiritual life, and being in relationship with other people &amp; God. At the same time, it fuels the Church’s mission of reaching every nation with the Gospel, ensuring that the message of Christ continues to spread and transform lives from generation to generation.

<!-- p9 -->
## Why Discipleship Matters

<!-- p10 -->
The impact of discipleship extends beyond individual transformation—it has Kingdom impact. Paul reminded Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> to entrust what he had learned to faithful men who would then teach others also. This generational model shows how discipleship is the engine driving the growth and resilience of the Church. Discipleship is vital because it was the method Jesus chose to spread the Gospel and build His Church.

<!-- p11 -->
Rather than focusing on large crowds, Jesus intentionally invested in a small group of followers, teaching them, modeling faithfulness, and preparing them to carry on His mission. This personal, relational approach ensured that His message multiplied, as each disciple was equipped to make many more disciples.

<!-- p12 -->
At its core, discipleship is central to fulfilling the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). Jesus did not merely call us to make converts, but to make disciples—followers grounded in the Word of God, empowered by the Holy Spirit, and equipped for service. Through discipleship, believers grow in knowledge of Scripture, develop spiritual disciplines, and learn to walk in obedience to Christ. It prepares the Church to impact the world, advance the Gospel, and reflect the glory of God among all nations.

<!-- p13 -->
### Nature vs. Nurture

<!-- p14 -->
In biological, psychological, and anthropological research, we often consider the impact of nature versus nurture. The question often asked is, is a person more the result of their natural, inherent genetics? Or are they more the result of their environment and upbringing? The truth is that every person is a combination of nature and nurture. These two strongly interacting factors guide the development of the human soul.

<!-- p15 -->
When you accepted Jesus, the miracle of salvation caused you to be born again of incorruptible seed. You received a divine nature! Your spiritual DNA has been rewritten! This divine nature births in our hearts a stirring to draw near to the heavenly Father, to know Him, and to encounter Him.

<!-- p16 -->
Like a newborn child, whose wonderful, beautiful nature of purity must be shaped by loving parents so that they grow and mature, we too need to be formed and nurtured in order to develop.

<!-- p17 -->
Discipleship is the process of spiritual development that builds on our divine nature and makes us spiritually mature. By investing in discipleship, we ensure that the faith is inherited, leaders are raised up, and the Church remains healthy and vibrant—both locally and globally.

<!-- p18 -->
### Didactic Model vs. Apprenticeship Model

<!-- p19 -->
In modern Western education, the primary relationship a student has is with the material. The student’s learning focuses on content, resources, books, videos, activities, etc. The change in learning occurs in the syllabus. This approach to teaching is known as the didactic model.

<!-- p20 -->
The didactic approach has not always been the norm. The model and method used in the time of Jesus was the *apprenticeship method.* This educational model emphasizes that knowledge is mostly ‘caught’ rather than ‘taught,’ and it stresses the relationship between the student and the teacher rather than between the student and the curriculum.

<!-- p21 -->
The didactic method gradually became the standard over the course of nearly a thousand years, beginning with the earliest universities of medieval Europe in the 11<sup>th</sup> - 13<sup>th</sup> centuries, the invention of the printing press in the 15<sup>th</sup> century, the Enlightenment of the 17<sup>th</sup> &amp; 18<sup>th</sup> centuries, and the impact of the Industrial Revolution on information and education in the 19<sup>th</sup> &amp; 20<sup>th</sup> centuries.

<!-- p22 -->
The advantage of the didactic method is that it can scale efficiently beyond the apprenticeship method. Massive open online courses (MOOCs) can have thousands of students at the same time, whereas even a highly skilled master can only have a dozen or so apprentices at the same time.

<!-- p23 -->
The outcome of the didactic method is that the student gains a high degree of command over the content; however, the student does not become like the teacher. The teacher is the facilitator of the curriculum. The teacher transmits knowledge.

<!-- p24 -->
In the apprenticeship method, the master shares more than knowledge; he imparts wisdom. The result of this method is that the student becomes fully like the master and is able to reproduce the master's work and take on apprentices of his own.

<!-- p25 -->
The didactic method has its use and place in the Kingdom of God; a good example is this book or any other Christian instructional material. Jesus, as a rabbi, skilled and trained in the rabbinic way, used the apprenticeship method as the primary model for his disciples.

<!-- p26 -->
Jesus explains the apprenticeship method in:

<!-- p27 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="40" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p28 -->
> **<u>Luke 6:40</u>** (NKJV)
>
> A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone who is perfectly trained will be like his teacher.
>

<!-- p29 -->
</span>

<!-- p30 -->
In the didactic method the student becomes a carrier of knowledge; in the apprenticeship method, the student becomes like the teacher. Let us learn the key words in that passage in Luke.

<!-- p32 -->
In short, the purpose and goal of the apprenticeship method as used for discipling are twofold:

<!-- p33 -->
**First, the student becomes fully trained**, like the teacher. This does not mean that the student imitates everything his teacher does. We are not commanded to create identical copies, but to build mature men and women who can carry the message of Jesus to the ends of the earth.

<!-- p34 -->
**Next, the student now becomes a teacher to others**. The student must pass on what he has learned to someone else; otherwise the message of the kingdom stops with him/her. This does not mean they must have the exact same gifts as their teacher, or enter full-time ministry, but it does mean they are to produce others so that their training becomes complete.

<!-- p35 -->
Every organism that does not reproduce is barren, and its chain is broken. Animals that cannot reproduce are not healthy; likewise a church that cannot reproduce is not healthy.

## The Pattern and Process of Jesus' Discipling

<!-- p37 -->
Jesus showed the mature, complete example of discipling in the Gospels with himself and the disciples he chose. We are to apply these steps broadly to the foundation of our walk as we become and make disciples.

<!-- p38 -->
The apprenticeship method has two roles: master and disciple. Every master was once a disciple, and we hope that every disciple becomes a master. We will use the term *disciple* to refer to the learner, and *disciple-maker* to refer to the master/teacher.

<!-- p39 -->
Every discipling relationship goes through a progression consisting of eight phases. The phases, or steps, are not completely separate from one another; often the relationship moves among the phases. In each phase there is a particular emphasis for the disciple, and another for the disciple-maker. We will study the traits that describe disciple-makers more deeply in a later section of this chapter.

<!-- p40 -->
The eight facets of discipleship are:

<!-- p41 -->
1.  Invitation/Call

<!-- p42 -->
2.  Teaching

<!-- p43 -->
3.  Modeling

<!-- p44 -->
4.  Participation/Experimentation

<!-- p45 -->
5.  Correction

<!-- p46 -->
6.  Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p47 -->
7.  Multiplication

<!-- p48 -->
8.  Stay Connected

<!-- p49 -->
### Invitation/Call

<!-- p50 -->
Discipleship begins with a personal invitation from Jesus to follow him. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span>, Jesus called his first disciples, saying, *“Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.”* This invitation was both relational and transformative—it required leaving a former way of life and entering a new purpose grounded in God’s mission. Jesus’ call was not to spectatorship but to participation in his ministry and the work of his Kingdom.

<!-- p51 -->
Modern disciple-makers should invite people into genuine relationships that encourage spiritual growth and obedience. The invitation to discipleship is highly personal. Jesus meets each person where they are, addressing their specific needs and circumstances, yet he calls them to go deeper—to grow, serve, and become like him. This approach reflects the relational nature of discipleship, where the focus is not only on knowledge but on a life-transforming relationship with Christ.

<!-- p52 -->
### Teaching

<!-- p53 -->
Teaching is central to discipleship. Jesus devoted much of his ministry time to teaching the principles of the Kingdom of God, especially in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7). His teaching provided a foundation for living righteously, showing love to others, and walking in obedience to God. The teaching of the Word of God remains central to discipleship today, since Scripture is the primary instrument for spiritual growth, correction, and training in righteousness (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p54 -->
Disciple-makers should be committed to faithfully teaching the Holy Scriptures, helping others understand and apply them to their lives. Teaching is more than the transmission of knowledge—it requires the imitation of a Christlike life so disciples can see faith in action. Jesus embodied the truth he taught, showing that effective teaching is both spoken and visible. Likewise, disciple-makers should strive to live what they teach, offering practical examples of obedience and integrity.

<!-- p55 -->
### Modeling

<!-- p56 -->
Jesus did not only teach his disciples- He modeled how to live the truth he proclaimed. Jesus demonstrated what it means to love, serve, and practice ministry. Some Gospel examples include Jesus washing his disciples’ feet as an act of humility and service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="13" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 13:12-15</span>), healing the sick, and feeding the hungry. In his actions he exemplified compassion, faith, and courage, leaving a clear example for his followers to imitate.

<!-- p57 -->
Discipleship needs to be shown, not merely stated. Modern disciple-makers should be transparent and speak about their faith journey, including their struggles and victories, so others can see how faith works in real life. Whether praying with someone in need, sharing testimony, or serving the community, discipleship grows when it is tied to practical demonstration. Actions often speak louder than words, making modeling a powerful tool for training others in the faith.

<!-- p58 -->
### Participation/Experimentation

<!-- p59 -->
Jesus did not sideline his disciples- He actively involved them in ministry experiences. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 9:1-6</span> and <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 10:5-15</span>, Jesus sent the disciples to preach the Gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons. These hands-on opportunities enabled the disciples to learn by doing, gaining confidence and skills under his guidance.

<!-- p60 -->
Modern discipleship should also include practical participation. Disciple-makers should invite disciples to take part in ministry, whether by leading prayer groups, sharing testimonies, or serving in outreach efforts. Providing safe spaces for practice allows disciples to grow in courage while being supported and encouraged. Learning happens best when applied, and participation is the key to turning vision into action.

<!-- p61 -->
### Correction

<!-- p62 -->
Growth often requires correction, and Jesus lovingly corrected his disciples when they misunderstood his teaching or failed in their tasks. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-37" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Mark 9:33-37</span>, he addressed their pride by teaching servant leadership. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="22" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="31-32" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 22:31-32</span>, he prayed for Peter, preparing him to recover from failure and strengthen others.

<!-- p63 -->
Correction in discipleship should be gracious and edifying—anchored in Scripture and aimed at restoration (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 4:2</span>). It is not about condemnation but about helping disciples grow and avoid pitfalls. Disciple-makers should provide accountability, using the Word of God to guide and encourage growth, while modeling humility when they themselves are corrected.

<!-- p64 -->
### Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p65 -->
Jesus not only taught and corrected—He empowered his disciples to carry out his mission. He gave them authority and commission to preach the Gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:18-20</span>). After his resurrection, he promised the Holy Spirit to be the source of power for their ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>).

<!-- p66 -->
Modern discipleship should include empowering others to operate in their spiritual gifts and serve effectively. This involves recognizing gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:11-13</span>), encouraging their use, and releasing disciples to serve. Empowerment also emphasizes dependence on the Holy Spirit for strength, courage, and wisdom, ensuring that disciples are spiritually equipped for the work God has called them to do.

<!-- p67 -->
### Multiplication

<!-- p68 -->
The goal of discipleship is not merely personal growth—it is multiplication. Jesus modeled this by training his disciples to make many other disciples (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). After his resurrection, the apostles continued this multiplying work, spreading the Gospel and planting churches throughout the known world. Paul’s instruction in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> reinforces this principle, encouraging Timothy to teach others who will, in turn, teach others.

<!-- p69 -->
Discipleship involves equipping disciples to disciple others, creating a chain reaction of faith that expands the Kingdom of God. This process requires intentionality—teaching disciples how to lead, teach, and train others, sending them with confidence and purpose (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 10:15</span>).

<!-- p70 -->
### Stay Connected

<!-- p71 -->
Discipleship does not end when someone is sent. Jesus modeled ongoing relationship with his disciples, continuing to encourage and teach even after his resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="21" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 21:15-19</span>). The early church maintained this pattern, meeting regularly for teaching, fellowship, and prayer (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>).

<!-- p72 -->
Discipleship today should likewise foster ongoing relationships, providing accountability, encouragement, and support as disciples continue to grow and lead. Maintaining the unity of the Body of Christ ensures that disciples are never alone, but remain strengthened and encouraged in their faith journey.

<!-- p73 -->
## Who Does Discipleship?

<!-- p74 -->
Everyone in the Body of Christ has the responsibility first to be a disciple, and then to participate in the process of disciple-making. There are different gifts and roles, so our contributions may vary, but we are all responsible. Those ultimately most responsible for the state and health of discipleship in the local church are the leaders (**elders and deacons - <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 14:23</span>**), but everyone has a part to play.

<!-- p75 -->
Discipleship is a living, growing process—like raising a child to maturity. Healthy discipleship brings people strength and life. Just as a parent strengthens and nourishes a child, leading to a healthy, mature adult, disciple-makers likewise help disciples with nourishment, strengthening, and maturation. The ultimate goal of discipleship is not a ‘transfer of knowledge,’ but transformation.

<!-- p76 -->
We should all become complete (mature) and make disciples. Though we will have different gifts, there are common traits shared by all disciple-makers.

<!-- p77 -->
### Characteristics of a Disciple-Maker

<!-- p78 -->
A disciple-maker is someone who not only follows Christ but is also committed to helping others grow in their faith. This role requires specific qualities that reflect both spiritual maturity and practical dedication. Disciple-makers must continually rely on God’s strength, wisdom, and grace to overcome the challenges that arise from leading others in their spiritual journey.

<!-- p79 -->
#### Rooted in Christ

<!-- p80 -->
The foundation of a disciple-maker’s life must be deeply rooted in Christ. Jesus said, *“I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from me you can do nothing”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="15" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 15:5</span>). Effective disciple-makers know that the power to grow and to help others comes from daily closeness to Jesus. However, resistance to personal growth and spiritual transformation can hinder this dependence. Disciple-makers must continually cultivate their personal relationship with Jesus through prayer, study of Scripture, and obedience, modeling to those they shepherd what it means to abide in Christ.

<!-- p81 -->
#### Committed to the Mission

<!-- p82 -->
A disciple-maker must remain committed to the work of making disciples as commanded by Jesus in the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). This work requires intentional focus and prioritization, recognizing that making disciples is not an optional task but the central purpose of the church. However, challenges such as time constraints, busyness, and personal distractions can make it difficult to stay focused. Disciple-makers should intentionally schedule time for mentoring, teaching, and relationship-building, viewing disciple-making as a way of life rather than an occasional activity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 19:10</span>).

<!-- p83 -->
#### Led by the Holy Spirit

<!-- p84 -->
Disciple-making cannot be accomplished by human strength alone; it requires the guidance and empowerment of the Holy Spirit. Jesus promised his disciples that they would receive power when the Holy Spirit came upon them (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>), equipping them to be witnesses and ministers of the Gospel. Disciple-makers should be responsive to the Spirit’s leading, relying on his wisdom to navigate difficult situations, cultural challenges, and hard conversations. This dependence on the Holy Spirit enables disciple-makers to operate in love, courage, and spiritual discernment, ensuring that their efforts are Spirit-led rather than self-driven.

<!-- p85 -->
#### Humble and Teachable

<!-- p86 -->
Humility is an essential characteristic of an effective disciple-maker. As Paul instructed, *“Do nothing out of selfish ambition or empty conceit. Rather, in humility, value others above yourselves”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 2:3</span>). Disciple-makers recognize that they too are lifelong learners who must remain teachable and accountable. They welcome correction and feedback, modeling humility to those they lead. Pride can be a major obstacle to growth, both for disciple-makers and for their disciples. Embracing accountability and maintaining a servant’s heart fosters trust and creates an environment of growth and integrity.

<!-- p87 -->
#### Patient and Persevering

<!-- p88 -->
Disciple-making rarely happens instantly—it is a long-term process that requires patience and perseverance. Paul reminds believers, *“let us not grow weary of doing good, for in due season we will reap if we do not give up”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:9</span>). Faith growth often comes with setbacks, questions, and failures; yet disciple-makers are called to walk with others, offering encouragement and support at every step. It is easy to become discouraged when progress seems slow, but disciple-makers trust that God is at work, even when results are not immediately visible.

<!-- p89 -->
Becoming a disciple-maker requires a combination of spiritual dependence, resolve, and humility. It is a calling that demands patience and perseverance while being Spirit-led and teachable. Disciple-makers are not expected to be perfect, but they must be faithful—abiding in Christ, investing in others, and trusting God to bring growth and transformation in His timing. These qualities reflect the love of Christ and help others become mature disciples who, in turn, can make other disciples.

<!-- p90 -->
## Practical Tools for Disciple-Making

<!-- p91 -->
Effective disciple-making requires tools and practices used intentionally that promote spiritual growth, accountability, and fellowship. These tools help disciples build a solid foundation of faith, grow in closeness to God, and be equipped to make other disciples. Practical methods such as Bible study, prayer, small groups, and mentoring provide both structure and support needed for sustained growth.

<!-- p92 -->
### Studying Scripture

<!-- p93 -->
Deep engagement with Scripture is foundational to disciple-making. Jesus emphasized the importance of God’s Word for spiritual growth, saying, *“Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:4</span>). Consistent Bible study and memorization enable disciples to internalize truth, combat falsehood, and apply God’s wisdom to daily life.

<!-- p94 -->
Disciples should be encouraged to immerse themselves in Scripture, not merely as an intellectual exercise but to know God and be transformed in heart (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="119" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="105" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 119:105</span>). Practical approaches, such as reading plans, study guides, and group discussions, provide opportunities to explore doctrine and examine application within community. By establishing consistent study habits, disciples lay a solid foundation for spiritual maturity and fruitful service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p95 -->
### Prayer

<!-- p96 -->
Prayer is the lifeline of disciple-making, connecting believers to God’s presence, power, and guidance. Jesus taught his disciples how to pray in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 11:1-4</span>, emphasizing both dependence on God and the spiritual alignment with His will. Through prayer, disciples learn to seek God’s wisdom, intercede for others, and receive strength for service.

<!-- p97 -->
Teaching disciples how to pray—both privately and corporately—equips them to cultivate closeness to God and to discern His leading (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 4:6-7</span>). Incorporating prayer journals, prayer groups, and fasting fosters spiritual discipline and empowerment, ensuring that disciples remain Spirit-led and mission-focused.

<!-- p98 -->
### Small Groups and Community

<!-- p99 -->
Disciple-making thrives within community. The early church modeled relational disciple-making through fellowship, prayer, and the breaking of bread together (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>). Small groups and accountability partnerships provide a safe space for disciples to grow together, share their struggles, and celebrate victories.

<!-- p100 -->
The local church serves as the central hub for disciple-making relationships, providing opportunities for group Bible study, prayer gatherings, and fellowship events. These gatherings allow disciples to practice love, to bear one another’s burdens (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:2</span>), and to encourage one another toward spiritual maturity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="24-25" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:24-25</span>). Small groups also provide a structure to multiply leaders, as mature disciples train and send others to lead groups and guide new believers.

<!-- p101 -->
### Mentoring

<!-- p102 -->
Mentoring provides a personal and intentional approach to disciple-making. Jesus modeled this by deeply investing in the twelve disciples, accompanied by teaching, correction, and encouragement. Likewise, face-to-face relationships allow disciple-makers to tailor guidance to individual needs, helping disciples grow in faith, character, and leadership.

<!-- p103 -->
Mentors function as accountability partners, providing support, prayer, and wisdom during seasons of growth and struggle. Practical tools such as spiritual assessments, goal-setting, and regular check-ins ensure that disciples stay focused and remain motivated. Peer relationships within the mentoring framework also foster mutual growth, as the mentor and the disciple learn from shared experiences (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PRO" data-chapter="27" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Proverbs 27:17</span>).

## **Personal Reflection Questions**

<!-- p105 -->
1.  What does it personally mean to you to be a disciple of Jesus? How do you actively follow Him in your daily life?

<!-- p106 -->
2.  Reflect on Jesus’ model of disciple-making. Which part of His approach—invitation, teaching, demonstration, or empowerment—stands out to you the most, and why?

<!-- p107 -->
3.  How can you multiply your faith by making disciples of others? What challenges or fears might hold you back, and how can you overcome them? (Note I said multiply your faith, not multiply your disciples!)

<!-- p108 -->
4.  Which traits of a disciple-maker (e.g., patience, humility, relational focus) do you see in yourself? Which do you sense being led to further develop?

<!-- p109 -->
5.  Consider the people in your life. Who might God be calling you to disciple? How can you take the first step in building that relationship?
