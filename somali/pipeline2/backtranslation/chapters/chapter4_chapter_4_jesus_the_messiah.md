# Chapter 4 - Jesus Christ

<!-- p1 -->
Continuing our study of sin, we have concluded that sin carries a heavy cost, that what was sinned against must be restored, and those who sinned must make restitution. The price of sin must be paid. In the Old Covenant God established a series of sacrifices carried out by priests, individuals, and communities to deal with sin, to resolve it, and to judge it.

<!-- p2 -->
Even with all the sin offerings, they were still not sufficient—not because God failed, but because people kept choosing sin. Although animal sacrifices could bring limited atonement, no amount of animal offerings can change the sinful nature of humankind.

<!-- p3 -->
God had an amazing plan, <u>Messiah</u> (Hebrew ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) also known as <u>Christ</u> (Greek ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**) meaning “the Anointed One"; a person consecrated for a special role (e.g., a king, priest, or savior).

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it helps to look at what the people of His time expected of the Messiah. Those hopes shaped how they saw Jesus, and often why they struggled to recognize Him as the fulfillment of God’s plan.

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical Context

<!-- p6 -->
The Jewish people had long awaited a coming Messiah. Jewish beliefs about the Messiah in the period before Jesus were shaped by Old Testament prophecy, whose interpretations often varied and were not universally agreed upon. Most Jews expected the Messiah to be a political and military leader who would free Israel from foreign oppression (especially Roman rule) and restore the kingdom of David. Here is an overview of common messianic hopes and how they aligned (or did not align) with the prophecies of Scripture:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish Expectations of the Messiah before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A Political and Military Deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would be a king like David, who would lead Israel in a successful revolt against foreign powers and restore Israel’s independence.

<!-- p10 -->
- **Verses cited:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 9:6-7</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JER" data-chapter="23" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Jeremiah 23:5-6</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ZEC" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Zechariah 9:9-10</span>.

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical Situation:** Under Roman rule the Jews were oppressed and heavily taxed, which led to the hope that the Messiah would overthrow Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p12 -->
- **The Truth:** Jesus is King, but His kingdom is not of this world (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="36" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 18:36</span>). He established a spiritual kingdom at His first coming, but He will return as a victorious King to rule and judge in righteousness, as described in the Book of Revelation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 19:11-16</span>).

<!-- p13 -->
### **2. Restorer of the Davidic Kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would rebuild the kingdom of David and bring about a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace.

<!-- p15 -->
- **Verses cited:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 7:12-16</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="AMO" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Amos 9:11</span>.

<!-- p16 -->
- **Interpretation:** The focus was often on national restoration rather than spiritual renewal.

<!-- p17 -->
- **The Truth:** Jesus, a descendant of David, fulfills this promise by establishing an everlasting kingdom. Although this kingdom is now spiritual, it will culminate in a literal reign at His second coming (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="32-33" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:4-6</span>).

<!-- p18 -->
### **3. A Prophet Like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief:** Some Jews believed that the Messiah would be a prophet like Moses, who would speak directly with God and speak in His name.

<!-- p20 -->
- **Scriptures referenced:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="DEU" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Deuteronomy 18:15-19</span>.

<!-- p21 -->
- **Expectation:** Although most expected a prophetic figure, they often did not see this role as central to the Messiah but merely as an additional aspect.

<!-- p22 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus fulfilled this prophecy as the ultimate Prophet, speaking the Word of God and directly revealing the Father (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:18</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:10</span>). His prophetic role surpasses Moses, as He mediates a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### **4. A Leader Who Would Bring Justice**

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would judge the wicked, support the righteous, and bring justice to the oppressed.

<!-- p25 -->
- **Scriptures referenced:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 11:3-5</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="72" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 72:1-4</span>.

<!-- p26 -->
- **Interpretation:** Justice was expected to be primarily political and social rather than spiritual.

<!-- p27 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus brings ultimate justice. At His first coming, He provided redemption and spiritual justice through the cross (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Colossians 2:13-15</span>). At His second coming, He will execute judgment on the wicked and establish perfect, everlasting justice (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>).

<!-- p28 -->
### Different beliefs about the Messiah

<!-- p29 -->
Just as Christians today are divided into groups, denominations, or belief structures, so too were the Jews of Jesus’ time divided. During the time of Jesus, four major Jewish groups shaped the religious and political landscape of Israel, each with its own beliefs and expectations regarding the Messiah. A basic understanding of these different groups can help provide deeper insight when studying the New Testament Scriptures.

<!-- p30 -->
The **Pharisees** were the most influential among the common people, known for their strict adherence to the Law (Torah) and oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, emphasizing personal piety and obedience to God's commandments. Their messianic expectation was centered on a figure who would uphold the Law, bring justice, and restore Israel both politically and religiously.

<!-- p31 -->
The **Sadducees**, in contrast, were a wealthy and politically powerful group that controlled the Temple and priesthood. They rejected oral traditions and accepted only the Torah as authoritative scripture. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, afterlife, and the existence of angels, focusing instead on maintaining their power and collaborating with Rome to ensure political stability. Consequently, they had little emphasis on a Messiah, as their primary concern was preserving the status quo.

<!-- p32 -->
The **Essenes** were a separatist group that withdrew from mainstream Jewish society, living in isolated communities such as Qumran. They were deeply focused on ritual purity, apocalyptic expectations, and a disciplined lifestyle. The Essenes believed in two Messianic figures: a priestly Messiah who would restore the Temple's sanctity and a kingly Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness. This dual-Messiah concept reflected their hope for a spiritual and political renewal. The Essenes are not directly referenced in the scriptures.

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, the **Zealots** were militant nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent rebellion. They rejected Roman authority and were deeply committed to the restoration of Israel's sovereignty. Their messianic expectation was for a warrior-king Messiah, akin to David, who would lead them in battle against their oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p34 -->
While these groups focused on earthly expectations, Jesus’ mission revealed a far greater plan-one that addressed not just political oppression but humanity’s spiritual bondage to sin.

## The truth about the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### The Anointing of Jesus

<!-- p37 -->
The **Pharisees’** and **Zealots’** vision of a Messiah reflects a recurring pattern in human history-seeking salvation through earthly means while rejecting God’s greater plan. As we’ve seen throughout Scripture, people often turn away from God-even though He’s always faithful. Even when God is faithful and accepts us, He is often forgotten and rejected, leading to the cycles of disobedience and decay that plagued earthly kingdoms.

<!-- p38 -->
While many expected a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a far greater purpose for His anointing-one that fulfilled spiritual and eternal needs. If Jesus had come as a conquering military leader, as the Jews hoped and expected, it would have only been a matter of time before the kingdom fell into disrepair because of human disobedience.

<!-- p39 -->
Instead, Jesus established a kingdom that cannot be shaken. His mission was not rooted in military might or political dominance, but in divine purpose. As the anointed one, Jesus explained what He was anointed to do and how He would accomplish it:

Jesus was anointed - set apart by God - and sent for a divine mission, full of purpose and power.

<!-- p44 -->

<!-- p46 -->
- Salvation - forgiveness, remission of sin, freedom.

<!-- p47 -->
- The acceptable year (season) of the Lord.

The people to whom he came were:

<!-- p49 -->

<!-- p50 -->

<!-- p54 -->
- Restoring sight

<!-- p55 -->
- Bringing into a state of freedom

<!-- p56 -->
- Binding up, healing, restoring

<!-- p57 -->
The purpose of Jesus’ ministry and work was both particular and universal, immediate, and enduring. His purpose is not a single thing but is part of God’s divine plan to reconcile humanity to God. When sin entered the world, it caused a separation between God and people. God forced humanity out of the garden and from His presence. During the Old Covenant, humanity could be with God, but only in a limited way. It was very rare for the Spirit of God to be given to people; most people lived isolated from God. He found a people, the Jews, yet He desired a permanent, secure, and complete way to restore His relationship with humanity.

<!-- p58 -->
The presence of God on earth in the time of Jesus was mostly confined to the innermost part of the temple, the Holy of Holies. There, behind a thick veil that separated the innermost part and the Holy Place, the presence of God rested on the ark of the covenant. Humanity was cut off from God, and God was set apart from His creation.

<!-- p59 -->
God sent Jesus to repair, rebuild, restore, and reconcile humanity to Himself. (2 Corinthians 5:17-21)

<!-- p60 -->
### The Essential Work of Jesus

<!-- p61 -->
In order to fully reconcile humanity to God, the work of the Messiah was not only preaching and healing, but it also required the final act of atonement. Jesus had to become the perfect and complete sacrifice by undergoing his death on the cross.

<!-- p62 -->
The Book of Hebrews explains at great length across its 13 chapters how Jesus became the perfect offering, and that he was both the sin offering and the High Priest who presents it (Hebrews 3 & 4). Jesus’ priesthood was not that of the Levites (after the tribe of Levi), but it was like that of Melchizedek, who is said to have neither beginning nor end, which means it is not bound by human forms and customs (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 7:3</span>). The limitation of the Levitical priesthood is that it relies on the blood of bulls and goats (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:12-13</span>) whose sacrifices were not sufficient for a lasting and permanent forgiveness of sins. From our study about sin and sacrifice, we know that the consequences of people’s sins could be postponed for one year (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:3</span>) during the Day of Atonement, but that sacrifice had to be continually renewed.

<!-- p63 -->
Jesus, by dying on the cross (whose accounts are found in Matthew 27, Mark 15, John 19, and Luke 23), shed his blood and paid the ransom for sins—the past, present, and future—of all humanity. Isaiah 53 shows Jesus as the suffering servant, who took upon himself the full weight of punishment and pain. His once-for-all offering became the final, the supreme sacrifice. Jesus is the Lamb of God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="29" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:29</span>), without spot or wrinkle (sin); because he was perfect, complete, and holy, the shedding of his innocent blood made atonement possible. **No more sacrifices are needed nor acceptable. The former were provisional—but that of Jesus is complete and final.**

<!-- p64 -->
Through death and resurrection, we have received a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6</span>), one secured by the blood of the very Son of God; Jesus is the mediator of the covenant, the go-between. Through Christ’s sacrifice, we receive salvation, and he is the author of *‘eternal salvation to those who obey*’ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 5:9</span>).

<!-- p65 -->
The Greek word for salvation is **Sōtēria** (*so-tay-REE-ah - **G4991)**.* It fundamentally means **salvation, deliverance, or protection**. It encompasses the act of being saved, both in a spiritual sense (eternal salvation in Christ) and in physical circumstances (deliverance from danger or harm). The word conveys the idea of safety, completeness, and restoration.

<!-- p66 -->
**Salvation or Deliverance:**

<!-- p67 -->
- Refers to God’s deliverance of humanity from sin and its consequences through Jesus Christ.

<!-- p68 -->
- Used in contexts of eternal salvation (justification, sanctification, and glorification).

<!-- p69 -->
**Protection:**

<!-- p70 -->
- Denotes being kept, defended, or preserved from danger.

<!-- p71 -->
**Wholeness or Healing:**

<!-- p72 -->
- Involves restoration to a state of completeness or soundness.

<!-- p73 -->
Jesus’ sacrifice paid the price for our forgiveness, made a way for us to have a relationship with God, and set a path by which we remain with him based solely on our obedience, not on our perfection or our ability to stay away from sin.

<!-- p74 -->
When we receive Jesus by faith (discussed later), we receive his salvation. This salvation binds us to God and places our names in the ***Lamb’s Book of Life*** (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>). The Gospel of John says:

<!-- p75 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p76 -->
> **<u>John 3:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For God so loved the world that He gave His only Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish, but have eternal life.
>

<!-- p77 -->
</span>

<!-- p78 -->
To perish means to be ruined—like fruit that spoils before it can be enjoyed. It is a life that misses what it was created for. Jesus came to give us enduring, or eternal, life, and to keep us from perishing. Eternal life is not a life that begins after our death; it is a life that begins the very moment we accept Jesus as our salvation, when we are born again.

It comes by faith—as we choose to trust Him fully, like entering a marriage covenant, with both heart and words.

<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

When we were born on the earth (born of the flesh and of water) many things happened: we took our first breath and were declared alive, our parents welcomed us, a name was given to us, we became heirs, we received nourishment and protection, and we continued to grow.

When we put our trust in Jesus and accept Him, we become born again, not of the flesh, but of the Spirit. We become spiritually alive! As with physical birth, spiritual birth also comes with many things:

<!-- p89 -->
1.  When we accepted Jesus, God wiped away our sins and declared us forgiven (**justified**). He gave us a new beginning. The wall that separated us from Him was removed. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="TIT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Titus 3:5</span>)

3.  We have been **adopted** into the family of God and have become heirs. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 1:5</span>)

<!-- p92 -->
4.  We receive the source of spiritual nourishment, and everlasting protection. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="1PE" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Peter 2:2</span>)
