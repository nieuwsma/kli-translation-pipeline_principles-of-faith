# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
Scripture tells us many stories about sin—its causes, its consequences, and how God responds. To commit sin is to fall short of God’s standard, or ‘missing the mark,’ a term from archery that describes the archer’s failure to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both Testaments, Old and New, use various words for sin, each highlighting aspects such as its kind, its severity, its deliberateness, its victim, its character, and the punishment required. There are broad categories Scripture uses to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against humanity, sin against yourself

<!-- p4 -->
- Deliberate sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- One-time sin, habitual sin

<!-- p6 -->
- Sin requiring severe punishment such as death, sin requiring financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In our modern criminal justice systems, similar distinctions exist: degrees of crime, premeditated or crimes of passion, crimes requiring financial restitution, or imprisonment, even capital punishment. An act can be both a legal crime and a sin, but legal crime is a human classification, whereas sin is God’s classification.

<!-- p8 -->
The Greek and Hebrew words for sin are very similar. Every sin is generally defined under the term ‘missing the mark,’ ***Hamartia*** in Greek, and ***Chata’*** in Hebrew. This brief term is used for the most serious sins—adultery, murder—but also for sins not as serious, such as the favoritism shown to the rich in our church meetings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

As we examine the sacrifices of the Old Covenant, we begin to see how God prepared His people to understand the cost of sin and the hope of restoration.

## Sin requires sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God slaughtered animals and made garments of their skins for them (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first shedding of blood to cover the consequences of sin. There are several similar incidents in the book of Genesis, but God formally established a sacrificial system for atonement for sin in the book of Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general offering of atonement and devotion to God, often representing total surrender. This offering addressed sin in general, covering both known and unknown sins. The procedure involved offering a male animal—typically a bull, sheep, goat, or bird—without blemish. The offerer laid his hands on the animal’s head, symbolizing identification with the offering, and then the entire animal was burned on the altar. The act of burning the whole offering represented complete devotion, putting away sin and aligning one’s life with God. The burnt offering highlighted the gravity of sin and the need for full consecration, expressing reverence and repentance through the giving of something costly.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, known as ***Minchah***, was an offering of thanksgiving and devotion rather than a direct sin offering. This offering accompanied other offerings, such as the burnt and peace offerings, and was intended to express gratitude, worship, and dedication to God. It was made from fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, and the offering contained no blood. A portion of the flour was burned on the altar as a gift to God, and the remainder was given to the priests to eat. Although not specifically for sin, the grain offering was an important part of corporate worship, symbolizing the offerer’s devotion and gratitude for God’s care and mercy.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This offering often followed sin and guilt offerings, serving as a celebration of restored relationship with God after sin had been addressed. The offerer could bring a male or female animal without blemish, such as cattle, sheep, or goats. In the procedure, the fat portions—the best parts of the animal—were burned on the altar as God’s portion, while the remaining meat was shared by the priests and the offerer in a communal meal. This peace offering was unique in that it symbolized communion and sharing, reflecting wholeness and unity with God. After sin was atoned for, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was specifically designated for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When a person or the community realized they had unintentionally sinned, they could bring a sin offering to seek purification and forgiveness. The type of animal offered depended on the status of the one involved: a priest or the assembly would bring a bull, a leader would bring a male goat, and an individual would bring a female goat or a lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the offering before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and the rest would be taken outside the camp if it was a communal sin. This offering underscored the seriousness of even unintentional sins and provided a way for people to remain clean and maintain a right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins involving breaches of trust, harm to another person, or misuse of sacred things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with offenses needing compensation, such as property sins or violations against sacred things. To carry out this offering, the offender would bring an unblemished ram to be sacrificed. If the sin involved harm to a person or property, the offender had to make restitution by returning the property and adding an additional 20 percent as a penalty. This guilt offering emphasized the importance of confessing one’s sin and taking tangible steps to correct the wrong. It reinforced the relational and communal nature of sin, stressing that true atonement involves not only seeking God’s forgiveness but also making things right with those affected.

<!-- p76 -->
## Corporate Sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these individual offerings for personal sin, there were also offerings and gifts made for national, corporate sin, performed daily, weekly, monthly, but the most important of these was the Day of Atonement.

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16, instituted to cleanse the entire community of Israel from their collective sins of the past year. This special observance occurred once a year, on the 10th day of the seventh month, and addressed national sins rather than individual ones. It served to purify the tent of meeting, the priesthood, and the people, symbolizing atonement and cleansing for the community as a whole.

<!-- p79 -->
Central to the Day of Atonement were **two goats** used to represent both the offering and the removal of sin. The high priest cast lots to determine the role of each. The first goat, designated “for the Lord,” was offered as a sin offering, and its blood was carried by the high priest into the Most Holy Place. There, the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat—the cover of the Ark of the Covenant—as a sign of atonement for the people’s sins and their reconciliation with God. This act of sprinkling was highly significant, as it represented the cleansing of the holy place and affirmed that God’s presence would continue to dwell with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, known as the scapegoat (Azazel), symbolically carried the community’s sins. The high priest would place his hands on the scapegoat, confessing the collective sins of Israel, thereby transferring the sins onto the animal. The goat was then sent into the wilderness to die alone, which symbolized the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act became a powerful example of both atonement and the release of sin, showing that God not only forgave but also took away the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound display of God’s forgiveness and cleansing, which allowed the nation to delay the consequences of sin and to postpone judgment each year. This atonement did not permanently forgive the nation’s sins, but it postponed God’s judgment.

## We Have All Sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible focus on the issue of sin in different ways. When Moses was recording the law of the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he wrote specific penalties for specific sins. Here I use the term offense interchangeably, because the books of the law were the legal system that the people of Israel had at that time.

<!-- p89 -->
In the prophetic and historical books, sin and its consequences are often described by speaking about the fate of the whole nation. When the king and the people rebelled against God, Israel was oppressed by their neighbors, defeated in wars, and sometimes taken into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In the book of Proverbs, the writer speaks like a loving father to his son, warning him of the personal dangers of sin and of a life that goes against God’s commands. And in the book of Psalms, many of David’s writings speak about the personal and emotional consequences of sin committed.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns about sin and calls people to personal repentance, and he made the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In the letter to the Romans, the apostle Paul presents a great argument about how God planned to deal with sin, and how we can be freed from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
Indeed, we all have sinned, and we must all pay the penalty for our transgression.

<!-- p93 -->

It can be easy for us to try to excuse our actions and say we have not committed very serious sins like murder or adultery, and that we are generally moral, good people. This false attempt at self-justification ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and sinned against, and it ignores the reality of a holy and perfect God. The Scriptures say that if we deny that we have sinned, we are liars, and the truth is not in us!

From the very first sacrifice in Genesis to the cross of Christ, God’s plan has always been to redeem us and restore our relationship with Him. There is no sin too great, and no distance too far that the grace of God cannot reach us.
