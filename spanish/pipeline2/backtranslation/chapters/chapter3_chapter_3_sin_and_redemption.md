# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
The Scriptures tell us many stories about sin-its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin is to fall short of God’s standard, or to ‘miss the mark,’ an archery term that describes an archer’s failure to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old and New Testaments use various terms for sin, each highlighting aspects such as its type, severity, intent, victim, nature, and the punishment required. There are several broad categorizations that the Scriptures use to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against man, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Intentional sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- Sin of a single moment, sin committed habitually

<!-- p6 -->
- Sin that requires severe punishment such as death, sin that requires monetary restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In our modern criminal justice systems, we have similar distinctions: degrees of offense, premeditated or crimes of passion, offenses that require monetary restitution, or imprisonment, or even capital punishment. An act can be both a crime and a sin, but crime is man’s categorization, sin is God’s.

<!-- p8 -->
The various Greek and Hebrew words for sin have a high degree of overlap. All sin is broadly described under the general term ‘miss the mark,’ ***Hamartia*** in Greek, and ***Chata’*** in Hebrew. This umbrella term is used for the most serious sins, adultery, murder, but also for less serious sins, such as showing partiality toward wealthy people in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## Sin requires sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God kills animals and makes clothes for them with their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first shedding of blood to cover the consequence of sin. There are a few other similar accounts in the book of Genesis, but God formally institutes a method of sacrifice for sin in the book of Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general offering for atonement and devotion to God, often symbolizing total surrender. This offering addressed sin in a broad sense, covering both known and unknown sins. The ritual involved sacrificing a male animal-typically a bull, a sheep, a goat, or a bird-without any defect. The offerer laid their hands on the animal's head, symbolizing identification with the sacrifice, and then the animal was burned completely on the altar. This act of burning the whole offering represented total dedication to removing sin and aligning life with God. The burnt offering underscored the seriousness of sin and the need for complete devotion, showing reverence and repentance by giving up something valuable.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, known as ***Minchah***, was a sacrifice of thanksgiving and dedication rather than a direct offering for sin. This offering accompanied other sacrifices, such as the burnt offering and the peace offering, and was intended to express gratitude, worship, and devotion to God. Made of fine flour mixed with oil and incense, the grain offering included no blood. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar as a gift to God, while the rest was given to the priests as food. Although it was not specifically for sin, the grain offering was an important part of communal worship, symbolizing the offerer's dedication and gratitude toward God's provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This offering often followed the sacrifices for sin and atonement, celebrating the restored relationship with God once sin had been dealt with. The offerer could bring a male or female animal without defect, such as a cow, a sheep, or a goat. In the ritual, the fat portions-the best parts of the animal-were burned on the altar as God's portion, while the remaining meat was shared by the priests and the offerer in a communal meal. The peace offering was unique because it symbolized communion and fellowship, reflecting a sense of wholeness and unity with God. After sin had been atoned for, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God's acceptance and celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was designated specifically for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When a person or the community became aware that they had sinned unintentionally, they could offer a sin offering to seek purification and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the status of the person involved: a priest or the congregation offered a young bull, a leader offered a male goat, and an individual offered a female goat or lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the sacrifice before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and dispose of the rest of the animal outside the camp if it was a communal sin. This offering demonstrated the seriousness even of unintentional sins and provided a means for the people to remain purified and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins that involved a breach of trust, harm to another, or the profaning of sacred things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins in which reparations needed to be made, such as property offenses or violations against sacred objects. To make this offering, the sinner brought an unblemished ram to be sacrificed. If the sin involved harm to another person or to property, the offender also had to make restitution by returning the property plus an additional 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering highlighted the importance of acknowledging one's guilt and taking tangible steps to make wrongs right. It underscored the relational and communal nature of sin, emphasizing that true atonement required not only seeking God's forgiveness, but also making things right with those who had been affected by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Collective sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific offerings for individual sin, there were also sacrifices and offerings for the nation's collective sin made daily, weekly, monthly, but the most important of these is the **Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual ritual described in Leviticus 16, designed to purify the entire Israelite community from its collective sins of the previous year. This unique ceremony took place once a year, on the 10th day of the seventh month, and dealt with the sins of the nation rather than individual transgressions. It served to cleanse the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people, symbolizing both the atonement and the purification of the entire community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the center of the Day of Atonement were **two male goats** used to represent both the sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat designated “for the Lord,” was sacrificed as a sin offering, and its blood was taken by the high priest into the Most Holy Place. There, the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat-the cover of the Ark of the Covenant-symbolizing atonement for the people's sins and reconciling them to God. This act of sprinkling was deeply significant, as it represented the purification of the sacred space and ensured that God's presence remained with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second male goat, known as the **scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically carried the sins of the community. The high priest laid his hands upon the scapegoat, confessing the collective sins of Israel and thus transferring them to the animal. Then the male goat was sent into the wilderness to die alone, symbolizing the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act served as a powerful illustration of both atonement and deliverance from sin, showing that God not only forgave but also took away the sins of his people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God's forgiveness and purification, allowing the nation to delay the consequence of sin and postpone judgment each year. This **atonement did not permanently forgive** the sins of the nation, but rather delayed God's judgment.

## We Have All Sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible address the subject of sin in different ways. When Moses recorded the law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament) he prescribed specific punishments for specific sins/offenses. I use the word offense here interchangeably, because the books of the law were the legal system of the time for the Israelites.

<!-- p89 -->
In the prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in terms of the fate of the entire nation. When the king and the people disobeyed God, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, defeated in battle, and in some cases taken into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs, the author writes as a loving father to his son, warning him of the personal dangers of sin and of a lifestyle contrary to God's commandments. In the Psalms, many of David's writings deal with the personal and emotional consequences of having committed sin.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns about sin, calls people to personal repentance, and makes the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the apostle Paul presents a compelling case for how God has planned to deal with sin and how we can be free from its dominion.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is that we are all guilty of sin and we all must pay the price for our transgression.

This false attempt to justify ourselves ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and against whom we have sinned, and it ignores the reality of a holy and perfect God. The Scriptures say that if we deny that we sin, we are liars and the truth is not in us!
