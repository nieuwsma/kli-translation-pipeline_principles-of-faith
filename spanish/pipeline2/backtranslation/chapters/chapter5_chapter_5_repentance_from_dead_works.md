# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
Often in Christianity we think of repentance as something fundamental only for the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The heart of repentance goes far beyond turning from sin for salvation. Repentance from dead works means living with our hearts turned away from the things that harm us or hold back our growth-and turned toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews sets 'repentance from dead works' as the first item in the list of the foundational doctrines after the elementary principles of the Messiah (Chapter 4).

### Dead works

<!-- p9 -->
Everyone who has ever been employed knows what work is. Work is the set of tasks you undertake to receive a wage with which you can make a living (employment). Employment is an agreement to exchange your talents and efforts for something of value that helps you sustain yourself. You work because you get something out of it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can provide a sense of purpose, often a sense of identity. It is very common to be asked 'What do you do for a living?' and for the answer to begin with an identity statement 'I am'. Although work can be enjoyable and purposeful, it is also very likely to be physically or mentally hard, difficult, demanding, and stressful. If your job is none of these things, you might find it too routine and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work a 40-hour-a-week job, and sleep 8 hours per night, you spend 36% of your waking hours working, and this excludes the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting to work, caring for a household, and all the other necessary tasks you need to do to keep your life in order.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? It varies, but, by definition, because we get something of value from it.

<!-- p13 -->
Would you keep working at a job if they stopped paying you? For most of us the easy and painfully obvious answer is a resounding, NO!

<!-- p14 -->
Would you keep working at a job if it were killing you? I suppose it depends on how fast.

<!-- p15 -->
The context of ***nekros*** joined with ***erga*** is labor or work that is dead, has no vitality, cannot reproduce itself! The author of Hebrews warns us about a labor that is dead.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, there are several criteria used to determine whether an organism is alive (this is a partial list used for our purpose):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living beings convert energy through metabolic processes to grow, develop, and sustain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: Life involves growth and progression over time, with a specific life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living beings have the ability to reproduce, creating offspring or new entities.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adapt to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as the regulation of temperature or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living beings can adapt to their environments through genetic changes, which allows survival in diverse conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this standard of ‘life’ to our works with a bit of creative license, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  They do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  They do not reproduce, they cannot produce a positive or desirable outcome.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  They do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God’s presence, nor to the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  They do not maintain internal stability, they become dysregulated, they have no self-control.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  They do not adapt (they are too rigid) and no longer help us to survive.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s desire for us. God wants to bring us life!

<!-- p33 -->
Although dead versus alive is binary. Dying is a gradient. Our dead works may not be completely dead in each of the categories yet, but they will be! Dead works are like poisons; some have greater effects.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning normally. Some poisons have minor effects, like a headache. Others affect how we think, see, or even whether we can have children (live fruitfully). Dead works are like that. They may be just a headache, or they may cause death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are any works that, when reviewed and considered, are not bringing the abundant life that Jesus promised. Having an extramarital affair is a very strong poison; it is likely to have an entirely negative effect fairly soon. Being an unnecessarily stubborn and resentful person will slowly poison someone until they have driven everyone away from their side.

<!-- p36 -->
To be freed from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
‘Repenting of dead works’ is turning to God and leaving behind anything that is harming our walk with Him-starting with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Building on the poison analogy for dead works, if you discovered that your favorite snack was full of toxic heavy metals that would give you a fatal condition if consumed, what would you do? Without hesitation we would put it away from us, and never enjoy it again. Realizing that consuming it means our premature and, probably, painful death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must turn away from the things of this life that are poisoning us and leading us to both our natural and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to dig a little deeper into the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The term used in Hebrews is *repentance*. This is contrasted with the term *to repent*; an example is found in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
To repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or event; it means to change the mind or purpose; to turn away from sin and turn to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is a state of having repented; it means having had a change of mind; the state of having turned away from sin and turned to God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews 'repentance from dead works' He is saying to be in the state of having repented! We must live in a continuous state of having 'turned our back' on dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times do we need to repent? Trick question! We need to change our mind continually until we no longer do dead works. Repenting is **not asking for forgiveness nor apologizing**, though that may be involved. Repenting is turning away from the former way of thinking.

### Systems of change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap when changing one’s mind. If we do not perceive the need to change our mind, then ¿why would we open our eyes to look for change? Again, returning to the poison analogy, if you cannot perceive that the poison is killing you, ¿why would you stop consuming it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that one simply has to hit ‘rock bottom’, and then will look at their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that human beings are more complex. There are many theories related to behavior change in the study of psychology; a widely recognized theory is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People can go through a six-stage cycle for change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: There is no intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: There is an intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken to change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has changed for a short time.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has changed and continues to be maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: There is no desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no set time for how long each stage may last. Some people, with respect to certain matters in their lives, never progress through the stages in a meaningful way. In other cases, we can move from stage 1 to 6 very quickly.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father who experiences a sudden conviction after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *firm* but *loving* father. He believes that raising his voice is necessary. He is not considering changing. In fact, he might even justify it- “That’s how my dad raised me.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long workday, he loses control and yells at his little daughter harshly, louder and angrier than ever before. She is startled. Her lip quivers. She walks away in silence. In that moment… something **changes**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. The image of her hurt face plays over and over in his mind. His stomach turns. He says to himself, *“That’s not who I want to be.”* He knows he needs to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes into her room, kneels, apologizes sincerely and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You didn’t deserve that.”* She hugs him. Motivated to consolidate the change, he prays and talks with a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he begins reading parenting books to work on developing positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He is not perfect, but something has truly changed. He no longer justifies the previous behavior-and has no desire to go back.

<!-- p79 -->

We are also likely to encounter those who are desperate to change, but do not know what to do (**preparation**).

### Godly sorrow and the goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although hitting rock bottom is more a myth than a sure prerequisite for change, God can use sorrow to influence our minds. God will use sorrow to lead us to repentance. He will convict us of our actions and the state of our lives and hearts to open our eyes to see our complete and total need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>the experience of sorrow or distress that leads to a *metanoia*</u>, is not the same as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of sorrow or distress, but the world offers no hope, only relief and the promise of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and from grief.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the resistance to pain - physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual - especially when it is prolonged or intense.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is a multifaceted emotional response to a significant personal loss, especially relational or existential.

<!-- p100 -->
Sadness, suffering, and grief are closely related ideas. In the human condition we will experience all three; we can learn and grow through all three, but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
The Scriptures say that every good and perfect gift comes from God above (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow will cause discomfort, but His discipline is just and loving. Just as a good father would not intentionally break his child’s arm to ‘teach him a lesson’, God will not inflict sickness or disease on His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### Example of worldly sorrow vs godly sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old little sister. He was playing with a stick and, intentionally, hit her, making her cry and leaving a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
His father, hearing the commotion, investigated and, when he learned what his son had done, took the stick, broke it in half, and threw it away. He sent his son inside to think about what he had done and comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
The father, coming in to deal with his son, found him crying. When he asked him why he was crying, his son said, “because you broke my stick”.

<!-- p106 -->
The father’s response was: “It saddens me that you are more concerned about your stick than about your sister, whom you hurt,” and he sent him back to his room for further reflection.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later his son came out again and asked for forgiveness. He said he was still sad because Dad had broken his stick and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example, the boy experienced both worldly sorrow, for his lost stick, and, finally, godly sorrow, for his hurt sister.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and the goodness of God are connected. God is a good Father who will allow us to experience the consequences of our actions, but He will still stay by our side as we go through those consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
The unceasing goodness of God, His willingness to redeem us and restore us if we come to Him, is a positive agent in our path of repentance. His goodness is not that we continue in sin, but that, in spite of our sin, He opens a way for us to be redeemed and to experience His salvation.

<!-- p112 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

> Or do you despise the riches of His goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?

</span>

<!-- p113 -->
Despite the goodness, mercy, and grace of God, some people do not repent or change; they get stuck in a cycle of frustration and pain. At the opposite extreme, for some people the mere example of sin and consequence in someone else’s life is enough to make them change their way of thinking.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a great capacity for change; it is built into our very essence. We humans are highly adaptable, we can learn and grow, and find better ways to do things. However, the ability to change is not the same as the willingness or the conviction to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I don’t know why some people get stuck and others are so ready to change, but I can say that the whole process of change really comes from the grace of God.

Repentance can be a purely natural process, but repentance is also the work of the Spirit who calls us, opens our eyes to the truth, changes our heart, and invites us to be transformed. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:1</span> reminds us that some will be ‘caught’ in a transgression (sin) and will need help to get unstuck. ¡That help comes in the form of mature men and women who help restore us with a spirit of gentleness.

The Holy Spirit is the greatest agent of change with whom humanity can relate, and this is supported both by the Scriptures and by research. The research of “Lyons et al. (2010) found that up to 82% of clients who experienced a spiritual awakening during treatment and recovery from substance abuse were completely abstinent at a one-year follow-up, compared to 55% of clients without a spiritual awakening.” \[2\] Although man can initiate and mediate change, we can accomplish much more when we walk with the Spirit of God.

### David’s Example of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no account in the Scriptures has a richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin found in 2 Samuel 11-12. It begins with King David remaining in Jerusalem instead of going to battle, where he sees Bathsheba bathing. Despite knowing that she is the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David sends for her and they have an affair, which results in Bathsheba’s pregnancy.

<!-- p125 -->
To cover up his actions, David has Uriah return from the battlefield, hoping that he will spend time with Bathsheba and appear to be the father of the child. However, Uriah, out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, refuses to go to his house with Bathsheba. Then David arranges for Uriah to be sent to the front lines, where he ultimately dies in battle. After Uriah’s death, David hastily marries Bathsheba to cover up the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was not pleased with David’s actions and sent the prophet Nathan to confront him. When the prophet confronted him, David immediately admitted: “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify or excuse his actions. This confession was the first step in his repentance, acknowledging his wickedness directly before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I acknowledge my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me,*” show his complete ownership of his deeds. He expresses sorrow not only for the sin itself, but also for how he offended God, saying: “*Against You, You only, have I sinned.*” David showed “*a broken and contrite heart,*” an essential part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he wept deeply for the sin he committed and the harm he caused. This depth of remorse was a sign that he desired not just forgiveness, but a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God: “*Create in me, O God, a clean heart, and renew a right spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 51:10</span>). He sought an inner transformation that would keep him from repeating the same mistakes. Instead of only apologizing, David pleaded with God to cleanse him, saying: “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 51:7</span>). He recognized that only God could purify his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be spiritually renewed reflects a genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways going forward. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 51:12</span>) demonstrates his hope to align with God in every part of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God removed David’s sin, the Lord struck the child and he became gravely ill. David fasted, prayed, and begged God to save the child, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find a personal prayer of repentance and a public acknowledgment of his transgressions. David’s decision to write his plea for mercy, creating one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible, shows his willingness to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony of repentance and restoration for others to learn from, memorializing his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s path of repentance was not simply about feeling sorry; it was a profound change in his heart and his life. Through the confession, acceptance, and heart transformation captured in Psalm 51, David memorialized his sin as a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most significant examples of genuine repentance in the Bible.
