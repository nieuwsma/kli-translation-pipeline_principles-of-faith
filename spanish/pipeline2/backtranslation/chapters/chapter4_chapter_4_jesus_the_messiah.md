# Chapter 4 - Jesus-The Messiah

<!-- p1 -->
Based on our study of sin, we conclude that sin demands a serious price, both for those against whom sin is committed and must be restored, and for those who commit sin and must make restitution. The price of sin must be paid. In the Old Testament God established a series of sacrifices performed by priests, individuals, and communities to deal with, resolve, and adjudicate sin.

<!-- p2 -->
Even with all the sacrifices for sin, even so they were not enough-not because God failed, but because people kept choosing sin. While animal sacrifice could provide limited atonement, no amount of animal sacrifices could change man’s sinful nature.

<!-- p3 -->
God had a wonderful plan, the <u>Messiah</u> (in Hebrew ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) also known as the <u>Christ</u> (in Greek ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**) which means “Anointed"; someone consecrated for a special role (e.g., king, priest, or deliverer).

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it helps to look at what people in His time expected the Messiah to be. These expectations shaped how they viewed Jesus and, in many cases, why they struggled to recognize Him as the fulfillment of God’s plan.

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical Context

<!-- p6 -->
The Jewish people had long awaited the coming of a Messiah. Jewish beliefs about the Messiah during the time just before Jesus were shaped by Old Testament prophecies, whose interpretations were diverse and not universally accepted. Most Jews expected the Messiah to be a political and military leader who would free Israel from foreign oppression (primarily Roman rule) and restore the Davidic kingdom. Here is an overview of common messianic expectations and how they aligned (or did not align) with biblical prophecy:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish Expectations of the Messiah before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A political and military deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would be a king like David, who would lead Israel in a victorious revolt against foreign powers and restore Israel’s independence.

<!-- p10 -->
- **Scriptures cited:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 9:6-7</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JER" data-chapter="23" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Jeremiah 23:5-6</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ZEC" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Zechariah 9:9-10</span>.

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical context:** The Jews under Roman rule were oppressed and heavily taxed, which led to hopes for a Messiah who would overthrow Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p12 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus is a King, but His kingdom is not earthly (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="36" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 18:36</span>). He established a spiritual kingdom through His first coming, but He will return as a conquering King to judge and rule with righteousness, as described in the book of Revelation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 19:11-16</span>).

<!-- p13 -->
### **2. A restorer of the Davidic kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would rebuild the Davidic dynasty and bring a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace.

<!-- p15 -->
- **Scriptures cited:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 7:12-16</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="AMO" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Amos 9:11</span>.

<!-- p16 -->
- **Interpretation:** The focus was often on national restoration rather than spiritual renewal.

<!-- p17 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus, as a descendant of David, fulfills this promise by establishing an eternal kingdom. While this kingdom is spiritual in nature now, it will culminate in a physical reign during His second coming (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="32-33" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:4-6</span>).

<!-- p18 -->
### **3. A prophet like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief:** Some Jews believed that the Messiah would be a prophet, like Moses, who would speak directly with God and in His name.

<!-- p20 -->
- **Cited Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="DEU" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Deuteronomy 18:15-19</span>.

<!-- p21 -->
- **Expectation:** Although many anticipated a prophetic figure, they often did not see this role as central to the Messiah, but as an additional aspect.

<!-- p22 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus fulfilled this prophecy as the supreme Prophet, speaking the Word of God and revealing the Father directly (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:18</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:10</span>). His prophetic role goes beyond Moses, since He mediates a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### **4. A leader who would bring justice**

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would judge the wicked, uphold the righteous, and bring justice to the oppressed.

<!-- p25 -->
- **Cited Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 11:3-5</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="72" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 72:1-4</span>.

<!-- p26 -->
- **Interpretation:** Justice was expected to be primarily political and social rather than spiritual.

<!-- p27 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus brings ultimate justice. In His first coming, He offered spiritual redemption and righteousness through the cross (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Colossians 2:13-15</span>). In His second coming, He will execute judgment upon the wicked and establish perfect justice for all eternity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>).

<!-- p28 -->
### Diverse messianic beliefs

<!-- p29 -->
Just as Christianity today is divided into sects, denominations, or separate belief structures, so too were the Jews in the time of Jesus divided. During the time of Jesus, four major Jewish sects shaped the religious and political landscape of Israel, each with distinct beliefs and expectations about the Messiah. Understanding some background about these different groups can help provide meaningful context for the study of the New Testament Scriptures.

<!-- p30 -->
The **Pharisees** were the most influential among the common people, known for their strict adherence to the Law (Torah) and to the oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, emphasizing personal piety and obedience to God’s commandments. Their messianic expectation centered on a figure who would uphold the Law, bring justice, and restore Israel both politically and religiously.

<!-- p31 -->
The **Sadducees**, by contrast, were a wealthy and politically powerful group that controlled the Temple and the priesthood. They rejected the oral traditions and accepted only the Torah as authoritative Scripture. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, focusing instead on maintaining their power and collaborating with Rome to ensure political stability. Consequently, they placed little emphasis on a Messiah, since their primary concern was preserving the status quo.

<!-- p32 -->
The **Essenes** were a separatist group that withdrew from mainstream Jewish society, living in isolated communities such as Qumran. They were deeply focused on ritual purity, apocalyptic expectations, and a disciplined lifestyle. The Essenes believed in two messianic figures: a priestly Messiah who would restore the holiness of the Temple and a royal Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness. This concept of a dual Messiah reflected their hope for both spiritual and political renewal. The Essenes are not mentioned directly in the Scriptures.

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, the **Zealots** were militant nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent rebellion. They rejected Roman authority and were deeply committed to the restoration of Israel’s sovereignty. Their messianic expectation was that of a warrior-king Messiah, like David, who would lead them into battle against their oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p34 -->
Although these groups focused on earthly expectations, the mission of Jesus revealed a much greater plan-one that addressed not only political oppression but also humanity’s spiritual slavery to sin.

## The truth of the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### The anointing of Jesus

<!-- p37 -->
The vision of the Pharisees and the Zealots of a Messiah reflects a recurring pattern in human history-seeking salvation by earthly means while rejecting God’s greater plan. As we have seen throughout the Scriptures, people often turn away from God-though He is always faithful. Even when God is faithful and accepts us, He is often forgotten and rejected, leading to cycles of disobedience and decay that plagued earthly kingdoms.

<!-- p38 -->
Although many expected a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a much greater purpose for His anointing-one that met spiritual and eternal needs. If Jesus had come as a conquering military leader, as the Jews expected and anticipated, it would only have been a matter of time before the kingdom fell into decay due to human disobedience.

<!-- p39 -->
Instead, Jesus established a kingdom that cannot be shaken. His mission was not based on military power or political dominion, but on divine purpose. As the Anointed One, Jesus explained what He was anointed to do and how He would carry it out:

The purpose of Jesus’ ministry and service was both individual and global, immediate and enduring. His purpose does not have a singular focus, but is part of God’s divine plan to reconcile humanity with God. After sin entered the world, it caused a division between God and humanity. God had to expel man from the garden and from his presence. Throughout the Old Testament, man could be with God, but only partially. It was very rare for the Spirit of God to be given to men, and for the most part men lived isolated from God. He found a people in the Jews, but he wanted a permanent, secure, and complete way to restore his relationship with humanity.

<!-- p58 -->
The presence of God on earth in the time of Jesus was, for the most part, restricted to the innermost part of the temple, the Most Holy Place. There, behind the thick veil that separated the innermost part of the Holy Place, the presence of God rested next to the ark of the covenant. Man was isolated from God, and God was separated from his creation.

<!-- p59 -->
God sent Jesus to repair, rebuild, restore, and reconcile humanity to himself. (2 Cor 5:17-21)

<!-- p60 -->
### The Necessary Work of Jesus

<!-- p61 -->
To fully reconcile humanity to God, the Messiah’s mission required not only proclamation and healing, but also the supreme act of atonement. Jesus had to become the perfect and complete sacrifice through His death on the cross.

<!-- p62 -->
The book of Hebrews explains in great detail throughout its 13 chapters how Jesus became the perfect sacrifice, and was both the sin offering and the High Priest who offered it (Hebrews 3 and 4). Jesus’ priesthood is not from the Levitical line (from the tribe of Levi), but was in the manner of Melchizedek, of whom it is said that he has no beginning or end, which means he is not limited to the patterns and habits of men (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 7:3</span>). The limitation of the Levitical priesthood is that it depends on the blood of bulls and goats (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:12-13</span>), whose sacrifice is insufficient for the permanent and lasting remission of sins. From our study on sin and sacrifice, we know that the consequence for the people’s sins could be postponed for a year (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:3</span>) during the Day of Atonement, but that this sacrifice had to be continually renewed.

<!-- p63 -->
Jesus, by dying on the cross (accounts of which are in Matthew 27, Mark 15, John 19, and Luke 23), shed His blood and made payment for the sins—past, present, and future—of all humanity. Isaiah 53 reveals Jesus as the suffering servant, who took upon Himself the full weight of punishment and anguish. His once-for-all death became the final and supreme sacrifice. Jesus is the Lamb of God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="29" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:29</span>), without spot or wrinkle (sin); because He was perfect and complete and holy, the shedding of His innocent blood made atonement possible. **No further sacrifices are needed or acceptable. The former were only temporary—but Jesus’ was complete and final.**

<!-- p64 -->
Through death and resurrection, we have a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6</span>), one secured with the blood of God’s own Son; Jesus is the mediator of the covenant—He is the intermediary. Through Christ’s sacrifice, we receive salvation, and He is the author of *‘eternal salvation to all who obey Him*’ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 5:9</span>).

<!-- p65 -->
The word salvation in Greek is **Sōtēria** (*so-tay-REE-ah - **G4991)**.* It primarily means **salvation, deliverance, or preservation**. It encompasses the act of being saved, both in a spiritual sense (eternal salvation through Christ) and in physical contexts (rescue from danger or harm). The term conveys the idea of safety, wholeness, and restoration.

<!-- p66 -->
**Salvation or Deliverance:**

<!-- p67 -->
- Refers to God’s deliverance of humanity from sin and its consequences through Jesus Christ.

<!-- p68 -->
- Used in contexts of eternal salvation (justification, sanctification, and glorification).

<!-- p69 -->
**Preservation:**

<!-- p70 -->
- Implies being kept safe, protected, or preserved from danger.

<!-- p71 -->
**Wholeness or Healing:**

<!-- p72 -->
- Includes restoration to a state of wholeness or health.

<!-- p73 -->
Jesus’ sacrifice paid the price for us to be forgiven and opened a way for us to have a relationship with God, and established a path for us to remain with Him that depended only on our obedience—not on our perfection or our ability to abstain from sin.

<!-- p74 -->
When we accept Jesus by faith (to be addressed later), we receive His salvation. This salvation connects us with God and puts our name in the ***Lamb's Book of Life*** (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>). The Gospel of John says:

<!-- p75 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p76 -->
> **<u>John 3:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life.
>

<!-- p77 -->
</span>

<!-- p78 -->
Perish means to be ruined—like fruit that spoils before it can be enjoyed. It is a life that does not reach what it was made for. Jesus came to give us perpetual life, or eternal life, and to prevent us from perishing. Eternal life is not the life that begins after we die; it is the life that begins the moment we accept Jesus as our salvation, when we are born again.
