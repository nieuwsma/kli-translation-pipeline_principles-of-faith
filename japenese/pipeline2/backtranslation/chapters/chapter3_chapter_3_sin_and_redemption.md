# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
The Bible tells many stories about sin - its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin means failing to meet God's standard, or 'missing the mark'. This is an archery term that indicates an archer failed to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old Testament and the New Testament use various words for sin, each highlighting aspects such as its kind, seriousness, intent, victim, nature, and the punishment required. There are several broad categories the Bible uses when describing sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sins against God, sins against others, sins against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Intentional sins, sins committed unintentionally

<!-- p5 -->
- Momentary sins, sins committed habitually

<!-- p6 -->
- Sins requiring severe punishment including death, sins requiring monetary restitution

<!-- p7 -->
Modern criminal justice systems make similar distinctions: the degree of the offense, whether it was premeditated or a crime of passion, and whether the crime requires monetary restitution, imprisonment, or even the death penalty. An act may be both a crime and a sin, but crime is a human classification, whereas sin is God's.

<!-- p8 -->
There is significant overlap among the various Greek and Hebrew words that denote sin. All sin is broadly described under the umbrella of 'missing the mark', in Greek, ***Hamartia***; in Hebrew, ***Chata’***. This umbrella term applies to the gravest sins, such as adultery and murder, as well as to lighter sins, such as showing favoritism to the rich in a church gathering (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## Sin Requires Sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God slew animals and made garments for them from the skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first instance where blood was shed to cover the consequences of sin. While Genesis contains several similar accounts, God formally instituted the means of sin offerings in Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general offering for atonement and dedication to God, often symbolizing complete surrender. This offering broadly covered both known and unknown sins. The ritual involved presenting an unblemished male animal - usually a bull, a sheep, a goat, or a bird. The offerer laid a hand on the head of the offering, symbolizing identification with it, and then the entire animal was burned on the altar. This act of wholly burning the offering represented the removal of sin and a complete devotion to aligning one’s life with God. The burnt offering emphasized the seriousness of sin and the necessity of total dedication, demonstrating reverence for God and repentance by offering something of value.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, known as ***Minchah***, was less a direct sacrifice for sin and more an offering of gratitude and devotion. It often accompanied other offerings, such as the burnt offering or peace offering, to express thanksgiving, worship, and dedication to God. Made of fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, it contained no blood. Part of the grain was burned on the altar as a gift to God, and the rest was given to the priests for food. Though not for sin, the grain offering was an important part of communal worship, symbolizing the offerer’s devotion and gratitude for God’s provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, called ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. It was often presented after the sin and atonement offerings, celebrating the restoration of relationship with God once sin had been addressed. The offerer could present an unblemished male or female animal (such as a bull, sheep, or goat). In the ritual, the fat portions - the best parts of the animal - were burned on the altar as God’s portion, and the remaining meat was shared in a meal by the priests and the offerer together. The peace offering was unique in that it symbolized fellowship and intimacy, reflecting wholeness and unity with God. After atonement for sin, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, called ***Chatat***, was specifically designated for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When individuals or the community realized they had sinned unintentionally, they could present a sin offering to seek cleansing and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the person’s status: the priest or the congregation offered a bull, a leader offered a male goat, and an individual offered a female goat or a female lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the sacrifice before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and, if it was for the community’s sin, carry the remainder outside the camp for disposal. This offering highlighted the gravity of sin even when unintentional and provided a way for the people to be cleansed and remain in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, called ***Asham***, addressed sins involving breaches of trust, harm to others, or contamination of what was holy. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often connected to sins that necessitated repayment, such as violations involving property or infringements against holy things. To perform this offering, the one who sinned presented an unblemished ram. If the sin had harmed another person or property, the offender had to return what was taken and add an additional 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering emphasized the importance of acknowledging one’s culpability and taking concrete steps to make things right. It showed the relational and communal nature of sin and underscored that true atonement involves not only seeking God’s forgiveness but also making things right with those affected by the wrongdoing.

## Communal Sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific offerings for individual sins, sacrifices and offerings for the communal sins of the nation were performed daily, weekly, and monthly, the most important of which is **the Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite detailed in Leviticus 16, instituted to cleanse the entire congregation of Israel from the sins of the previous year as a community. This special ritual took place once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, and addressed national sins rather than individual transgressions. It purified the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people, symbolizing both atonement and cleansing for the whole community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the center of the Day of Atonement were **two goats**, used to represent both sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat, designated for the Lord, was slaughtered as a sin offering, and its blood was carried by the high priest into the Most Holy Place. There the blood was applied to the mercy seat - the cover of the ark of the covenant - symbolizing atonement for the people’s sins and their reconciliation with God. This act of sprinkling represented the purification of the holy place and carried profound meaning in assuring that God’s presence would remain with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat was known as the **scapegoat (Azazel)** and symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest laid his hands on that scapegoat, confessed the communal sins of Israel, and transferred those sins to the animal. The goat was sent into the wilderness, left to die alone, symbolizing that sin was completely removed from the camp. This visible act powerfully demonstrated both atonement and deliverance from sin, showing that God not only forgives the people's sins but also removes them.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God's forgiveness and cleansing, enabling the nation to defer the consequences of sin and postpone judgment each year. This **atonement did not grant permanent forgiveness**, but delayed God's judgment.

## We Have All Sinned

<!-- p88 -->
Each book of the Bible places a different focus on the problem of sin. When Moses recorded the law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), specific penalties for particular sins/crimes were recorded. The term "crime" is used synonymously here because the books of the Law were the legal code for the Israelites of that time.

<!-- p89 -->
In the Prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often discussed in terms of the fate of the nation as a whole. When the kings and the people turned against God, the Israelites were oppressed by surrounding peoples, defeated in battle, and in some cases carried away into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs, the author speaks to his son as a loving father, warning about the personal dangers of sin and a life that defies God's commands. In the Psalms, many of David's poems deal with the personal and emotional consequences of having sinned.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns about sin, calls people to personal repentance, and becomes the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the apostle Paul argues powerfully for how God has planned to deal with sin and how we can be set free from its dominion.

<!-- p92 -->
In reality, we are all guilty of sin, and each of us must pay the price for our rebellion.
