# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
Often in Christianity, we tend to think of repentance as essential only at the beginning of faith in Jesus. The core of repentance is, much deeper than turning from sin for salvation. Repentance from dead works, means turning our hearts away from what harms us or hinders our growth, and living with our hearts turned toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews, positions ‘repentance from dead works’ as the first item in the list of foundational doctrines that follows the basic principles of the Messiah (Chapter 4).

### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Everyone who has ever been employed, knows what work is. Work, is a series of tasks (employment) undertaken for the purpose of receiving wages to earn a living. Employment, is an agreement to exchange your talents and labor for something of value that helps support your life. People, work because they can gain something of value by it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important, and brings a sense of purpose, and often a sense of identity. It is quite normal to be asked, ‘What is your occupation?’ and the response begins with the self-identifying turn of phrase, ‘I am ~’. While work can be enjoyable and purposeful, it can also be physically and mentally demanding, difficult, challenging, and very likely to be high in stress. If your job fits none of these, you may feel it is trivial and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work 40 hours a week, and sleep 8 hours every night, you are spending 36% of your waking time on work, and this is, excluding the time spent on getting ready for work, commuting, housework, and every other task necessary to keep your life on track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do this much work? It varies, but by definition, it is because we can obtain something of value through it.

<!-- p13 -->
If the pay stopped, would you continue that job? For most of us, the simple and painfully obvious answer is a resounding, NO!

<!-- p14 -->
If that job were killing you, would you keep working? It would depend on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
The context of ***nekros*** coupled with ***erga***, means labor or work that is dead, lacking vitality, and non-reproducing! The author of Hebrews, is warning us about dead labor.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, there are several criteria used to determine whether an organism is alive (here is a partial list for our purposes):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living things convert energy through metabolic processes, grow, develop, and maintain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: Life, includes growth and progression over time, and has specific life cycles and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Organisms have the ability to reproduce, producing offspring or new individuals.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Living organisms respond to external stimuli (light, heat, sound, etc.) and can adapt to changes in their environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as temperature regulation and pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living organisms adapt to their environment through genetic changes and become able to survive under various conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply these ‘life’ criteria to our actions with a bit of creative interpretation, then dead works are as follows:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce; cannot produce positive or desirable outcomes.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God’s presence or the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Cannot maintain internal stability, fall into dysregulation, and cannot self-regulate.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (are too rigid), and no longer serve our survival.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not what God desires for us. God wants to give us life!

Whether something is dead or alive is a dichotomy. There are stages to dying. Our dead works may not yet be completely dead in every respect, but they will be! Dead works are like poison, and their strength of effect varies.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning properly. Some poisons have small effects, like a headache. Some affect thinking and vision, or even whether one can have children (whether one can live fruitfully). Dead works are exactly the same. Sometimes they amount to just a headache; sometimes they lead to death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works, upon examination and reflection, are any actions that are not producing the abundant life Jesus promised. An extramarital affair is a very potent poison and is likely to soon exert pervasive, negative effects. Being needlessly stubborn and resentful slowly poisons a person and eventually drives everyone around them away.

<!-- p36 -->
To be freed from dead works, we must repent!

### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
To ‘repent from dead works’ means to turn back to God and let go of whatever is impairing our walk with Him - it begins with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
If we liken dead works to poison, then if you found out that your favorite snack was full of toxic heavy metals and that eating it would bring about a fatal condition, what would you do? We would unhesitatingly remove it from ourselves and never enjoy it again, understanding that consuming it would mean an untimely and likely painful death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent！ We must turn away from the things of this world, that are poisoning us, and leading us to death both physically and spiritually。

<!-- p41 -->
To understand this fully、we need to look a little deeper into the Greek。

<!-- p42 -->

To repent（***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**）is a verbで, an act or occurrenceで, to change the mind or purpose; to turn from sin toward God。

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance（***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**）is a nounで, the state of having repentedで, a changed mind; the state of having turned from sin toward God。

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews『*repentance from dead works*』, He means being in a repented state！ We should constantly live in a state of being 『turned away』 from dead works。

<!-- p49 -->
How many times do we need to repent？ Trick question！ We need to keep changing our minds continually until we no longer do dead works。 Repenting is not **seeking forgiveness, or apologizing**, though that may be involved。 Repentance is turning away from our previous way of thinking。

### A system of change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap to changing our minds. If we do not perceive the need to change our thinking, why would we open our eyes to look for change? Returning again to the poison analogy, if we cannot perceive that the poison is killing us, why would we stop ingesting it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some believe that if a person just reaches “rock bottom,” they will take a hard look at their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology teach us that we humans are more complicated. Psychological research includes many theories of behavior change, and among the most highly regarded is the **Transtheoretical Model of Behavior Change**, developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **[1].**

<!-- p62 -->
People may go through a six-stage cycle toward change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: No intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: Intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Taking some steps toward change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: Behavior has changed for a short period.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: Behavior has changed and is being maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: No desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no set length for how long each stage takes. For certain life challenges, some people do not progress meaningfully through the stages. In other instances, they may move very quickly from stage 1 to 6.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotion Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, A father who feels a sudden pang of guilt after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *strict* but *loving* parent. He believes raising his voice is necessary. He is not thinking about changing. In fact, he might justify it - “That's how my dad raised me.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, he snaps and yells at his young daughter louder than ever, with more anger, more harshly. She flinches. Her lips tremble. She walks away quietly. In that moment… something **changes**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. Her hurt face plays over and over in his mind. His stomach turns. He says to himself, *“I don't want to be this kind of person.”* He has to change something -*right now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes into her room, kneels, apologizes from the heart, and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You didn't deserve to be treated that way.”* She hugs him. Motivated to make the change stick, he prays and reaches out to friends for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day, he begins reading parenting books to cultivate positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. It's not perfect, but something has truly changed. He no longer justifies his previous behavior - and he has no desire to go back.

### Godly Sorrow and God’s Goodness

<!-- p92 -->
Though the idea that hitting rock bottom is a sure prerequisite for change is close to a myth, God can use sorrow to work on our hearts。 God uses sorrow to lead us to repentance。 He gives us a conviction of sin regarding our deeds, our lives, and the state of our hearts, opening our eyes to show us how completely and utterly we need God。

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or the <u>experience of sorrow or distress that leads to *metanoia*</u>, is not the same as the sorrow of this world。 In worldly sorrow, the experience of sorrow or distress itself is the same, but the world gives no hope; what remains is only the promise of alleviation and of death。

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is different from suffering and grief as well。

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is to endure pain—physical, emotional, mental, and also spiritual—especially when it is prolonged or intense.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is a multifaceted emotional response to significant personal loss, especially losses involving relationships or existence。

<!-- p100 -->
Sorrow, suffering, and lament are concepts that are closely related to one another. Given the human condition, we experience all three, and through all three we can learn and grow; however, God does not bring death and destruction into our lives in order to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
The Bible says that every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, from God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). God is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow brings discomfort, but God’s discipline is just and full of love. Just as a good father would not deliberately break his child’s arm “to teach a lesson,” God does not afflict His children with sickness or disease in order to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### Examples of Worldly Sorrow and Godly Sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick and intentionally hit her; she cried, and it left a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
Hearing the commotion, their father came to check, and when he learned what his son had done, he took the stick, snapped it in two, and threw it away. He sent his son inside to think about what he had done and comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came inside to deal with his son, he found him crying. When he asked why he was crying, the boy said, “Because you broke my stick.”

<!-- p106 -->
The father replied, “It makes me sad that you’re more concerned about your stick than about your sister whom you hurt,” and sent him back to his room to think further.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later, the boy came back and apologized. He said he was still sad that Dad had broken his stick, and he was also sad that he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example, the boy experienced worldly sorrow over the stick he lost, and eventually he also experienced godly sorrow over the sister he had hurt.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow is tied to God’s goodness. God is a good Father who allows us to experience the consequences of our actions, yet even as we walk through those consequences, He stands with us.

<!-- p111 -->
God’s unceasing goodness—His will to redeem and restore us if we come to Him—is a positive driving force on our path of repentance. God’s goodness does not mean that we remain in sin; rather, despite our sin, He provides a way for us to be redeemed and to experience His salvation.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a great capacity for change. It is built into our very nature. Humans are highly adaptable; we can learn, grow, and find better ways. However, having the capacity to change is not the same as having the willingness or the prompting to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some people get stuck while others are so ready to change. But I can say that the entire process of change truly comes by God’s grace.

### David’s Model of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no story in the Bible shows a model of repentance so richly as the account of King David’s sin recorded in 2 Samuel 11–12。The narrative begins when King David, who stayed in Jerusalem instead of going out to battle, saw Bathsheba bathing。Even though he knew she was the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David summoned her and slept with her, and as a result Bathsheba conceived。

<!-- p125 -->
To conceal his actions, David recalled Uriah from the battlefield, hoping he would spend time with Bathsheba and appear to be the child’s father。しかしウリヤは、戦友への忠誠から、自宅に帰って妻のもとへ行くことを拒みます。So David arranged for Uriah to be sent to the front lines, where he ultimately died in battle。After Uriah’s death, David quickly took Bathsheba as his wife to hide the pregnancy。

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was displeased with David’s actions and sent the prophet Nathan to rebuke him。When confronted by the prophet, David immediately admitted, 「*I have sinned against the Lord*」 （<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>）。David did not justify his actions or make excuses。この告白は、神に直接自分の過ちを認めるという、彼の悔い改めの第一歩でした。

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David says, 「*I know my transgressions; my sin is always before me，*」 fully owning his actions。He laments not only the sin itself but also how it has offended God, saying, 「*Against you, you only, have I sinned.*」 David showed a 「*broken and contrite heart*」 essential to true repentance（<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>）。This sorrow was not superficial。He deeply grieved the sin he had committed and the pain it caused。This deep contrition was a sign that he sought not just forgiveness but a transformed heart。

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God, 「*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*」 （<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>）。He sought an inner change that would keep him from repeating the same failures。Rather than merely apologizing, David pleaded with God to cleanse him, 「*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*」 （<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>）。He acknowledged that only God could purify his heart and restore his joy。

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be spiritually renewed reflects a genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways going forward。His request for a 「*willing spirit*」 （<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>） shows his desire to be aligned with God in every area of life。

<!-- p130 -->
Even though God put away David’s sin, the Lord struck the child, and the child became gravely ill。David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God for the child to live, but when the child died, David accepted it（<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>）。

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we see both a personal prayer of repentance and a public acknowledgment of his transgression。David’s recording of his plea for mercy, which produced one of the most enduring psalms in Scripture, shows his willingness to be candid about his sin and God’s forgiveness。This psalm stands as a testimony of repentance and restoration from which others can learn, commemorating his journey from guilt to grace。

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not merely feeling sorry; it was a profound change of heart and way of life。Through the confession, acceptance, and transformation of heart inscribed in Psalm 51, David recorded his sin as a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most important biblical examples of genuine repentance。
