# Chapter 2 - God - the Eternal Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is vitally important to build a solid foundation in the knowledge of who God is. We can arrive at understanding by learning the following:

<!-- p2 -->
- God's desires, intentions, and actions,

<!-- p3 -->
- God's essence, character, and attributes,

<!-- p4 -->
- What is said about God, and what God Himself says about Himself.

<!-- p5 -->
Scripture uniquely reveals God's qualities, and each facet deepens our understanding of the unchanging and eternal God.

<!-- p6 -->
To summarize God in a single expression is difficult, if not impossible. God is too vast and multifaceted to lend Himself to simplification. We can, and will, strive greatly to explain, describe, and depict God, but ultimately, as with art and music, God is understood through being experienced.

<!-- p7 -->
When people listen to music, they speak of it by expressing how it moves them - beyond mere notes and rhythms, how it resonates within - . A melody may carry warmth or intensity, accompanied by rhythms that throb like a heartbeat and crescendos that produce sensations of excitement or longing. Music can comfort like gentle waves, electrify like a storm, lift the listener's heart, ground their thoughts, or carry them to an entirely different place. This experience goes far beyond a sequence of sounds; it is captured in how the soul of the music lingers and shapes the listener's emotions and personal journey.

<!-- p8 -->
Similarly, a viewer of a painting looks beyond color and brushwork to explore what those elements embody. A landscape may evoke a tranquil escape, while an abstract work can stir curiosity or introspection. Certain hues bring warmth, and certain compositions may convey tranquility, chaos, or beauty. A painting becomes a living experience, provoking shifts in perspective and deep emotional responses that words alone cannot capture. Thus both music and art are transformed into something beyond their physical form - it becomes an encounter that touches something essential and changes the listener or viewer in a deep, personal way.

## Exploring God through the Old Testament

<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: God Who Dwells with His Creation

<!-- p11 -->
A central theme in God's story is His desire to dwell with His creation—the desire to dwell together. This is evident throughout the narrative of Genesis. Across its fifty chapters, the genealogy from Adam (the first man) to Joseph (the last major figure in Genesis, the last significant leader of God's people before Moses) spans approximately ~2,400 years. Through these stories we find God's unchanging goodness despite human wickedness.

<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God enters into relationship with chosen individuals.

<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - The first humans, created by God and placed in the Garden of Eden. God walked with them, spoke to them, gave them instructions, and established a relationship of mutual love and obedience. However, this relationship was affected by their disobedience when they ate from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 1-3).

<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - Though Cain ultimately rebels by killing his brother Abel, God speaks to him directly, warning him of the danger of sin and giving guidance before the murder. Even after Cain’s punishment, God put a mark on him and protected him from harm (Genesis 4).

<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - In a time when corruption was widespread, God formed a unique relationship with the righteous man Noah. God commanded Noah to build an ark, saving his family and the animals from the flood. Afterwards, God made a covenant with Noah, promising never again to destroy the earth with a flood, and He set the rainbow as the sign of this covenant (Genesis 6-9).

<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - As one of the most important relationships in Genesis, God calls Abram to leave his homeland and promises to make him the father of a great nation. God makes a covenant with Abram, promising land and descendants, and blessing for all nations through him. Abram’s faith and obedience become central themes, and as part of the covenant his name is changed to Abraham (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22).

<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - Hagar, an Egyptian slave woman serving Sarah, after being mistreated, encounters God in the wilderness. God comforts her, promises a future for her son Ishmael, and commands her to return to Abraham and Sarah. Hagar calls God “El Roi” (“the God who sees me”), acknowledging that God is the One who sees her (Genesis 16).

<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - Isaac, the son of Abraham and Sarah, inherits the covenant promises given to his father. God reaffirms these promises directly to Isaac, including the promises of offspring and blessing. God also guided Isaac during famine, protected him, and established the covenant relationship (Genesis 26).

<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - Though her relationship with God is indirect, God answers Rebekah’s prayer regarding her difficult pregnancy and reveals that her twins, Jacob and Esau, will become two nations with distinct destinies (Genesis 25).

<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God builds a personal relationship with Jacob, appears to him in dreams, and confirms the promises of the covenant concerning land, descendants, and blessing. Jacob encounters God multiple times, including the famous wrestling episode in which God changed his name to Israel, symbolizing his transformation and the establishment of Israel as one people（Genesis 28, 32, 35）.

<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Joseph’s relationship with God is not based on direct manifestations or a covenant like Abraham’s or Jacob’s, yet God is deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, divine favor, and providence, God guides Joseph from being sold as a slave to becoming a powerful leader in Egypt. Joseph’s faith in God’s sovereignty enabled him to acknowledge that God used his suffering for good and to forgive his brothers（Genesis 37, 39-50）.

<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this entire narrative, God, who desires to dwell with His creatures, is content to dwell with chosen individuals, and ultimately establishes a single people through the covenant with Abraham (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="17" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 17:7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: God Who Dwells with His Own People

<!-- p24 -->
God’s desire to dwell with His people is clearly seen in Exodus, the second book of the Bible. In Exodus, the people of Israel, who had once lived for a time in Egypt in relative peace and harmony, are now oppressed by a Pharaoh who did not know Joseph and feared the Hebrews（<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 1:6-8</span>）. God’s people lived in Egypt for approximately ~400 years（<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="40-41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 12:40-41</span>）, and in the latter part of that period they lived as slaves (there is no universal agreement on the exact length of time Israel was under Egypt’s bondage).

<!-- p25 -->
In the powerful presence of the burning bush that was not consumed, God revealed Himself to Moses, a murderer who had been born a Hebrew, raised in Egypt, and who, while in exile, was tending his father-in-law’s flock in the wilderness.

<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, 'I AM has sent me to you.'"
>

<!-- p28 -->
</span>

<!-- p29 -->
**“I AM THAT I AM”** (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is one of the most profound self-revelations in Scripture. When Moses asked God for His name, God replied with this phrase, which can be translated "I Am Who I Am," "I Will Be Who I Will Be," or simply "I Am." This declaration may seem somewhat puzzling at first, but it carries deep meaning. Can you imagine introducing yourself to someone you’ve just met as ‘I am’? When God reveals Himself as ‘I AM THAT I AM,’ He is testifying to His nature, eternal presence, and sovereignty. ‘I am’ is infinite in meaning and purpose.

<!-- p30 -->
God uses Moses to make Himself known to Pharaoh:

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Exodus 7:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And you shall say to him, 'The LORD, the God of the Hebrews, "Let My people go, that they may serve Me in the wilderness"; but indeed, until now you have not listened!'
>

<!-- p33 -->
</span>

<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh that God is ‘I AM,’ and that God demands the Israelites be released to worship Him in the wilderness.

<!-- p35 -->
Plagues and hardships follow, but eventually Pharaoh yields and allows the Israelites to leave.

<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="31" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p37 -->
> **<u>Exodus 12:31</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Then he called for Moses and Aaron by night, and said, "Rise, go out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel. Go, serve the LORD as you have said.
>

<!-- p38 -->
</span>

<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness God miraculously delivers His people from the vengeful Egyptian army（Exodus 14）, provides protection from the sun and a pillar of fire to warm them in the desert’s chilling nights（Exodus 13）, food from heaven（Exodus 16）, and water to drink（Exodus 17）.

<!-- p40 -->
God led His people to Mount Sinai, where He commanded Moses to consecrate the people, gave the Law (including the Ten Commandments), and made a covenant (the Old Covenant) with them (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 19</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">20</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="24" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">24</span>). This covenant (the Mosaic Covenant) was built upon the Abrahamic Covenant and continued from Moses to Jesus.

<!-- p41 -->
Despite God making a covenant with His people, the relationship is one in which a loving and jealous God pursues and dwells with a people who are very stubborn and ungrateful (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="32" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 32:9</span>). Ignoring God’s miraculous interventions, they remain rebellious for much of the story. God leads them to the Promised Land and tells them they may inherit it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="33" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 33:3</span>). Yet because of fear and disobedience, they disregard the supernatural salvation God has given and, instead, wander in the wilderness for 40 years until most of that generation dies out (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-34" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).

<!-- p42 -->
### The Rest of the Old Testament

<!-- p43 -->
During the ~1,400 years from Moses’ death to the birth of Christ, Israel experienced a recurring cycle: receiving God’s grace and blessing through devotion, followed by rebellion, God’s judgment, repentance, and finally God’s salvation. This pattern is especially evident in **Judges, Kings, and Chronicles**, which record cycles in which the people turn away from God, often worshiping other gods, thereby bringing about periods of oppression and exile. In response to their cries, God repeatedly raised up judges, prophets, and leaders to deliver them.

<!-- p44 -->
Throughout these years, despite Israel’s frequent failure to keep the covenant with God, He remained faithful and showed both justice and mercy. He continually rescued and forgave His people, revealing unwavering faithfulness and love.

<!-- p45 -->
As we see in Scripture, God reveals Himself through actions, relationships, and names that speak to His nature. Each name represents a particular aspect of His overall character. These names were given as part of the narrative of how God revealed Himself to His people. For personal study, a reference table is included in the appendix: *Table 10 - The Names of God in the Old Testament.* Read these names and study the Scripture references. Each reference is a story that precisely portrays how God saved, delivered, and rescued His people.

<!-- p46 -->
The appendix also includes a list of derivative attributes that describe God’s nature, works, and will: *Table 11 - The Attributes of God.* These attributes are somewhat more abstract than God’s names, but in truthfulness they are in no way inferior!

<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand that God - the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (**Trinity**) - works together as One. In the studies that follow, we will probe further into the names and attributes of Jesus and the Holy Spirit.

<!-- p48 -->
God’s nature and works are perfectly expressed in the unity of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. Scripture teaches that God is one in essence, yet exists as three persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Scripture affirms God’s uniqueness, stating, "There is no God besides Me" (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="45" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 45:5</span>). However, the Godhead is understood as a compound unity—three distinct persons who cooperate with a single mind and purpose. Each of the three has a unique role: **the Father creates**, **the Son redeems**, and **the Holy Spirit sanctifies**. The term "Trinity" does not appear in the Bible. It is a theological expression developed to describe this concept and is revealed throughout Scripture.

<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can be illustrated by examples from nature, which help clarify the idea of unity in diversity. Just as water can be liquid, ice, or steam yet is essentially H₂O, each of the three is fully God while remaining distinct as persons. Or consider a triangle: its three sides and three angles form a single complete shape, but remove one side and it is no longer a triangle. Likewise, **without all three persons, God’s nature would not be complete**. The three work together in perfect unity.

<!-- p50 -->
- **God the Father**: the Creator and Sustainer of all things, often understood as the initiator within the Godhead. His love for humanity is central to the biblical narrative, exemplified by <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:16</span>. In this study, we consider God the Father as the loving Father and righteous Judge who has provided the way of our redemption.

<!-- p51 -->
- **God the Son**: Jesus, coexistent and eternally equal with the Father, took on humanity, was conceived by the Holy Spirit, and was born of the virgin Mary. He lived a sinless life, died to redeem humanity, and rose again, triumphing over sin and death. He now sits at the right hand of the Father, interceding for us (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In this study, we consider Jesus as the perfect atoning sacrifice, the risen Lord, and the head of the Church.

<!-- p52 -->
- **God the Holy Spirit**: The Holy Spirit, coexistent and equal with the Father and the Son, is sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world of sin and to prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. The Holy Spirit empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In this study, we consider the Holy Spirit as the Comforter who equips, empowers, sanctifies, and dwells with us.

<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct persons, God’s triune nature is revealed as perfect unity in purpose, will, and love, with each person playing a vital role in God’s mission of creation, redemption, and sanctification. The Trinity invites believers to experience God in His fullness—as the loving Father, the redeeming Savior, and the transforming Spirit.

<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion

<!-- p55 -->
We can be uniquely known by names such as husband, father, son, brother, and friend. Each of these is a declaration of who we are in relation to someone. We are also known by attributes such as faithful, patient, kind, intelligent, and funny. Similarly, we can know God by His attributes and names - He is our healer, provider, shepherd, and redeemer. Yet labels and words are only a small part of us; to be truly known by others, we must be experienced. How much more so with God. To know God truly and fully, we must experience Him.

<!-- p56 -->
God’s names reveal His nature, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of what these names mean. For example, "God my Healer" takes on richer, deeper significance when we grasp the depths of the sickness, despair, or helplessness from which He delivers. Knowing God as Provider is likewise transformed when we feel real need and come to know that God is faithful.

<!-- p57 -->
Scripture provides context for such encounters, showing God’s faithfulness to His people across times and circumstances, and reinforcing that God’s names and attributes are not mere titles but living realities experienced within individual lives. We come to know God through this interplay of personal experience and the biblical narrative—not as a distant description, but as a present, active relationship in every season of our lives.
