# Chapter 2 - God—The Eternal Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is crucial that we lay a strong foundation for knowing who God is. We can understand by studying:

<!-- p2 -->
- His desires, purposes, and actions,

<!-- p3 -->
- His nature, character, and attributes,

<!-- p4 -->
- what is said about Him and what He says about Himself.

<!-- p5 -->
The Scriptures uniquely reveal the attributes of God, and each facet deepens our understanding of our unchanging, eternal God.

<!-- p6 -->
To encapsulate God in a single description is challenging, if not impossible, because He is too vast and multifaceted to be simplified. While we can and will make great efforts to explain, describe, and depict God, ultimately—like art and music—He must be experienced to be understood.

<!-- p7 -->
When someone listens to music, they describe it by how it moves them—how it resonates within, beyond mere notes and rhythms. A melody may carry gentleness or intensity, a rhythm may throb like a heartbeat, or swelling moments may evoke feelings of excitement or longing. Music can soothe like a gentle wave or electrify like a storm, lift the listener’s spirit, steady their thoughts, or transport them entirely elsewhere. This experience is far more than a sequence of sounds; it is the spirit of the music that lingers—captured in how it shapes the listener’s emotions and personal journey.

<!-- p8 -->
Similarly, a viewer of a painting looks beyond colors or brushstrokes, exploring what these elements embody. A landscape may evoke a sense of quiet escape, while an abstract composition may spark curiosity or introspection. Certain hues may bring warmth, while a composition may evoke a sense of peace, chaos, or beauty. The painting becomes a living experience, prompting a shift in perspective or a deep emotional response that words alone cannot capture. Thus, both music and art become more than their physical forms—they become encounters that touch something fundamental, transforming the listener or viewer in a profound and personal way.

## Discovering God through the Old Testament

<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: God Who Dwells with His Creation

<!-- p11 -->
A central theme of God’s story is His desire to dwell with His creation. This is evident throughout the narratives of Genesis. Within its fifty chapters and genealogies, from Adam (the first human) to Joseph (the last major figure of the book of Genesis, the last major leader of God’s people before Moses), approximately ~2,400 years are encompassed. Through these stories we can see the consistency of God’s goodness despite human wickedness.

<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God establishes relationships with chosen individuals.

<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - The first humans, created by God and placed in the Garden of Eden. God walked and spoke with them, giving them instructions and forming a relationship of mutual love and obedience. But this relationship is affected by their disobedience in eating the fruit of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 1-3).

<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - Even though Cain ultimately rebels by murdering his brother Abel, God speaks directly to him, warning him about the danger of sin and offering guidance before the murder. Even after Cain is punished, God marks him to protect him from harm (Genesis 4).

<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - In a time of widespread corruption, God forms a unique relationship with Noah, a righteous man. God commands Noah to build an ark to save his family and the animals from the flood. God later makes a covenant with Noah that He will never again destroy the earth with a flood, and establishes the rainbow as a sign of this covenant (Genesis 6-9).

<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - One of the most significant relationships in Genesis. God calls Abram to leave his country and promises to make him the father of a great nation. God establishes a covenant with Abram—of land, offspring, and blessings for all nations through him. Abram’s faith and obedience become central themes, and as part of the covenant his name is changed to Abraham (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22).

<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - An Egyptian servant of Sarah, Hagar encounters God in the wilderness after being mistreated. God comforts her, promises a future for her son Ishmael, and instructs her to return to Abraham and Sarah. Hagar calls God “El Roi” (“The God Who Sees Me”), acknowledging His care for her (Genesis 16).

<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - The son of Abraham and Sarah, Isaac inherits the promises of the covenant made with his father. God directly reaffirms these promises to Isaac, including the promise of offspring and blessings. During a famine, God guides Isaac, protects him, and establishes a covenant-based relationship (Genesis 26).

<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - Though her relationship with God is indirect, God answers Rebekah’s prayer about her difficult pregnancy, revealing that her twin sons, Jacob and Esau, will become two nations with distinct destinies (Genesis 25).

<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God establishes a personal relationship with Jacob, appears to him in a dream, and confirms the covenantal promises of land, offspring, and blessings. Jacob encounters God multiple times, including the famous wrestling match in which God changes his name to Israel, symbolizing his transformation and the establishment of Israel as a people (Genesis 28, 32, 35).

<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Although Joseph’s relationship with God was not based on direct manifestations or covenants like Abraham’s or Jacob’s, God is deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, divine favor, and providence, God guides Joseph’s journey from being sold into slavery to becoming a powerful leader in Egypt. Joseph’s faith in God’s sovereignty enables him to forgive his brothers, recognizing that God used his suffering for good (Genesis 37, 39-50).

<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this narrative, God, who desires to dwell with His creation, is content to dwell only with a few chosen ones, and ultimately establishes a people through a covenant with Abraham (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="17" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 17:7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: The God who dwells with His people

<!-- p24 -->
God’s desire to dwell with His people is also clearly seen in the second book of the Bible, Exodus. In the book of Exodus, the people of Israel, who had lived for a time in relative peace and accord in Egypt, are now under the oppression of a Pharaoh who did not know Joseph and who feared the Hebrews (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 1:6-8</span>). The people of God lived in Egypt for about ~400 years (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="40-41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 12:40-41</span}), the latter portion of which was spent as slaves (there is no universal consensus on how long Israel was in Egyptian bondage).

<!-- p25 -->
In the powerful presence of the burning bush that was not consumed, God reveals Himself to Moses, a Hebrew-born, Egyptian-raised, exiled killer—who was tending his father-in-law’s sheep in the wilderness.

<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And God said to Moses, "I am who I am." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, 'I AM has sent me to you.' "
>

<!-- p28 -->
</span>

<!-- p29 -->
The phrase **“I AM THAT I AM”** (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is one of the deepest self-revelations in Scripture. When Moses asks for God’s name, God replies with this phrase, which can be translated as "I Am Who I Am," "I Will Be Who I Will Be," or simply "I Am." This declaration may seem somewhat puzzling on the surface, but it carries profound meaning. Can you imagine introducing yourself to someone new by saying, ‘I am’? When God reveals Himself as ‘I AM THAT I AM,’ He is affirming His nature, His eternal presence, His sovereignty. ‘I am’ is infinite in meaning and purpose.

<!-- p30 -->
God uses Moses to make Himself known to Pharaoh:

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Exodus 7:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And you shall say to him, 'The LORD God of the Hebrews has sent me to you, saying, "Let My people go, that they may serve Me in the wilderness"; but indeed, until now you would not listen!
>

<!-- p33 -->
</span>

<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh that God is ‘I AM,’ and God demands that the Israelites be released so that they may worship Him in the wilderness.

<!-- p35 -->
Plagues and afflictions come, but eventually Pharaoh relents and lets the Israelites go.

<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="31" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p37 -->
> **<u>Exodus 12:31</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Then he called for Moses and Aaron by night, and said, "Rise, go out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel. And go, serve the LORD as you have said.
>

<!-- p38 -->
</span>

<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness God miraculously delivers His people from the vengeful Egyptian army (Exodus 14), provides protection from the sun and a pillar of fire to keep them warm in the cold desert night (Exodus 13), food from heaven (Exodus 16), and water to drink (Exodus 17).

<!-- p40 -->
God brings His people to Mount Sinai where God commands Moses to consecrate the people and gives them the Law (including the Ten Commandments) and establishes His covenant with them (the Old Covenant) (Exodus 19, 20, 24). This covenant (the Mosaic covenant) is built upon the Abrahamic covenant and remains in effect from Moses to Jesus.

<!-- p41 -->
Despite making a covenant with His people, this relationship is that of a loving and jealous God who pursues and dwells with a very stubborn and ungrateful people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="32" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 32:9</span>). Ignoring God’s astonishing intervention, they remain in a rebellious attitude for long stretches. God even brings them to the Promised Land and tells them that they can receive it as an inheritance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="33" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 33:3</span>), but in fear and disobedience they ignore the supernatural deliverance that God had given them, and instead they have to wander in the wilderness for 40 years until almost every one of that generation has died (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-34" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).

<!-- p42 -->
### The rest of the Old Testament

<!-- p43 -->
Over roughly ~1,400 years between Moses’ death and the birth of Christ, the Israelites experienced a recurring cycle of devotion, which brings God’s favor and blessing, and then rebellion, God’s judgment, followed by their repentance, and finally deliverance by God. This pattern is especially seen in the books of **Judges, Kings, and Chronicles**, which describe those cycles where the people turn away from God, often by worshiping other gods, leading to periods of oppression or exile. In response to their cries, God repeatedly raised up judges, prophets, or leaders to deliver them.

<!-- p44 -->
During these years, despite the Israelites often failing to keep their covenant with Him, God remains faithful, showing both justice and mercy. He continually saves and forgives His people, demonstrating His steadfast commitment and love.

<!-- p45 -->
As we see in the Scriptures, God reveals Himself through His actions, relationships, and even through His names, which speak about His character. Each name represents a particular aspect of His whole character. These names were given as part of the story of how God revealed Himself to His people. For your personal study a reference table is included in the appendix: *Table 10 - Names of God in the Old Testament.* Read these names and study the Scriptural references. Each reference is a story that shows exactly how God saved, redeemed, and brought relief to His people.

<!-- p46 -->
The appendix also includes a set of more derived attributes that describe His nature, including details of His actions and intentions: *Table 11 - Attributes of God.* These attributes, though a bit more abstract than the names of God, are by no means any less real!

<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand God—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (**Trinity**)—working together as one. As we continue our study, we will look more deeply at the names and attributes of Jesus and the Holy Spirit.

<!-- p48 -->
God’s character and works are fully revealed in the unity of Father, Son, and Spirit. Scripture teaches that in essence God is one, yet exists as three distinct persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Scripture affirms the unity of God, saying, “There is no other God besides Me” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="45" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 45:5</span>). Even so, the Godhead is understood as a **compound unity**—three distinct persons who work together with one mind and purpose, united in the truest sense. Each person of the Trinity has a unique role: **the Father creates**, **the Son redeems**, and **the Holy Spirit sanctifies**. The word “Trinity” is not found in the Bible; it developed as a theological expression to explain this concept revealed through the Scriptures.

<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can be explained with examples in nature, which clarify this idea of unity in diversity. Like water, which can be liquid, ice, or steam yet remains H₂O, each person of the Trinity is fully God but distinct in personhood. Or consider a triangle, whose three sides and three angles together form a complete shape; if one side is removed, it is no longer a triangle. Similarly, **God’s nature is incomplete without all three persons**, who work together in perfect unity.

<!-- p50 -->
- **God the Father**: He is the Creator and Sustainer of all creation, and is often understood as the initiator within the Godhead. His love for humanity is central to the biblical narrative, as shown in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:16</span>. In our study we will see God the Father as a loving Father and a righteous Judge, who has made a way for our salvation.

<!-- p51 -->
- **God the Son**: Co-existent and co-eternal with the Father, Jesus assumed human nature, was conceived by the Holy Spirit, and born of the virgin Mary. He lived a sinless life, accepted death to redeem humanity, and then rose again, conquering sin and death. He now sits at the right hand of the Father, interceding for us (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In our study we will see Jesus as the ultimate atoning sacrifice, the risen Lord, and the Head of the Church.

<!-- p52 -->
- **God the Holy Spirit**: The Holy Spirit, co-existent and equal with the Father and the Son, is sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world of sin and to prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. He empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In our study we will see the Holy Spirit as the Comforter, given by God to prepare us, empower us, sanctify us, and dwell with us.

<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct persons, God’s triune nature is revealed as perfect unity in purpose, will, and love, each person playing a vital role in the divine mission of creation, redemption, and sanctification. The Trinity invites believers to experience God fully—as a loving Father, a redeeming Savior, and a transformative Spirit.

<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion

<!-- p55 -->
We can be known in unique ways by our names: husband, father, son, brother, friend. Each of these is a declaration of who we are in relation to someone else. We can also be known by our attributes: faithful, patient, kind, wise, humorous. Similarly, we can know God by His attributes and names—He is our Healer, Provider, Shepherd, and Redeemer. However, just as labels and words are only small parts of us, in order for others to truly know us, we must be experienced. This is even more true of God. To truly and fully know Him we must experience Him.

<!-- p56 -->
God’s names reveal His character, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of the meaning of these names. For example, “God my Healer” takes on a richer and deeper significance when we understand the depths of sickness, despair, or helplessness from which God rescues. Knowing Him as our Provider is transformed when we feel real need and find Him faithful.

<!-- p57 -->
Scripture provides the context for these encounters, showing us God’s faithfulness to His people across times and circumstances, reinforcing that His names and attributes are not merely titles but realities that He brings to life, experienced in the lives of individuals. Through this interweaving of personal experience and Scriptural narrative we come to know God—not as a distant description, but as a present, active relationship in every season of our lives.
