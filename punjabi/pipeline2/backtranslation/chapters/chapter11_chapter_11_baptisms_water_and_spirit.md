# Chapter 11 - Baptisms-Water and Spirit

<!-- p1 -->
In today’s modern age, which has become detached from Jewish roots, baptism is generally understood only in the context of water baptism and the baptism of the Holy Spirit (Pentecost).

<!-- p2 -->
The book of Hebrews was written for a Jewish audience. Many Christians in our modern age do not have Jewish roots or heritage. Without Jewish roots and culture, many of us lack the cultural context that first-century Messianic Jews (those Jewish believers who confessed Jesus) would have shared. Because we were not raised in Jewish tradition, we generally do not know much about the feasts, fasts, prayers, washings, rites, and customs that Jewish believers knew.

<!-- p3 -->
The Greek word for baptisms or washings is: **Baptismos** (bap-tis-mos’ - **G909**) - the act of immersing or washing. It refers to ceremonial washings or ritual purification. It can refer to baths by immersion, purification rites, or washings used in Jewish and Christian contexts.

<!-- p4 -->
Baptisms are one of the foundational teachings mentioned in Hebrews:

## Old Testament ceremonial washings - historical context

<!-- p9 -->
In the Old Testament, ceremonial washings played a central role in maintaining ritual purity and symbolizing holiness before God. These washings were prescribed in the Law of Moses and applied to both the priests who served in the tabernacle and the people of Israel. For example, the priests were required to wash their hands and feet at the bronze basin before entering the tabernacle or approaching the altar, symbolizing their readiness to minister in the presence of a holy God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="30" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18-21" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 30:18-21</span>). Similarly, rites for cleansing from impurity—such as contact with a dead body or skin diseases—involved washing with water, signifying restoration to holiness (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LEV" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Leviticus 14:8</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 19:17-19</span>). These acts were outward expressions of an inward desire for holiness, emphasizing the importance of being set apart for God.

<!-- p10 -->
In the Old Testament, ceremonial washings had to be repeated regularly to maintain ritual purity. Unlike permanent solutions, these rites provided only temporary outward cleanliness and left the serious issue of sin unresolved. The author of Hebrews highlights this limitation, explaining that these practices were “symbolic for the present time” and could not cleanse the worshiper’s conscience (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:9-10</span>). These washings served as continual reminders of human imperfection and the need for something greater—an ultimate purification that could sanctify not only the body but also the heart and spirit.

<!-- p11 -->
The fulfillment of the Old Covenant’s ceremonial washings is found in Christ, who through His sacrifice provides complete and eternal cleansing. Unlike the temporary rites of the Law, the blood of Christ fully sanctifies—able to wash not only the body but also the conscience (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:13-14</span>). Through His death and resurrection, Jesus accomplished what the Old Testament rites only foreshadowed—true reconciliation with God. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="22" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:22</span> calls believers to “draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, having our hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water.” This language connects Christ’s work to the imagery of the Old Testament washings while also demonstrating its far greater efficacy. His sacrifice does not merely cover sin, but completely removes it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="1JN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 John 1:7</span>). In this way, the ceremonial washings served as a shadow, pointing to the deeper cleansing that only Christ could give, fulfilling humanity’s need for holiness through divine intervention.

<!-- p12 -->
To reiterate, there is no mandatory requirement upon Christians to perform any ceremonial washings. Washings did not grant salvation under the Old Covenant and do not grant salvation in the New Covenant either. Ceremonial washings will not elevate our standing with God, nor will they earn us more favor. Paul sharply rebuked the Judaizers in Galatia (read Galatians) who sought to make Jewish-context requirements (e.g., circumcision) necessary for salvation. Salvation is only through faith in Jesus!

<!-- p13 -->
## John’s baptism

<!-- p14 -->
Within the New Testament canon, several baptisms are mentioned. We have already discussed the baptisms and washings of Jewish tradition.

<!-- p15 -->
The focus of John the Baptist’s ministry was to prepare the way for the coming of Jesus Christ. His baptism, called a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Mark 1:4</span>), called people to confess their sins, turn their hearts toward God, and be ready for the coming of the Messiah. It was an outward sign of inward repentance, symbolizing spiritual cleansing and readiness for the kingdom of God. But John’s baptism was temporary and preparatory—it pointed to the greater work Christ would accomplish. While John’s baptism focused on repentance, it did not provide full forgiveness or spiritual renewal, as it lacked the indwelling and empowering of the Holy Spirit (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 3:11-12</span>). Instead, John openly declared that One mightier than he was coming, who would baptize not only with water, but with the Holy Spirit and fire (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 3:16</span>).

<!-- p16 -->
John’s baptism was fulfilled and surpassed through the death, burial, and resurrection of Jesus Christ. Christian baptism, instituted by Jesus and practiced by the apostles, signifies a deeper reality—union with Christ, death to sin, and newness of life (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 6:3-4</span>). Unlike John’s baptism, Christian baptism is directly connected to faith in Jesus’ atoning sacrifice and the indwelling of the Holy Spirit (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="38" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:38</span>). The coming of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost inaugurated the New Covenant and the beginning of Spirit-empowered living, which John’s baptism could not provide. Therefore, John’s baptism is no longer practiced, having been replaced by the baptism commanded by Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19</span>), which includes repentance, faith, and the sealing of believers by the Holy Spirit (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 1:13-14</span>).

<!-- p17 -->
## Christ’s baptism

<!-- p18 -->
Christ’s baptism served as a powerful example for His disciples and laid the foundation for Christian water baptism. Although Jesus was without sin, He humbly submitted to baptism by John “to fulfill all righteousness” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 3:15</span>). In doing so, He identified Himself with humanity’s need for repentance and foreshadowed His own death, burial, and resurrection. At His baptism, the Holy Spirit descended like a dove, and the Father declared, “This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 3:16-17</span>). This moment marked the beginning of Jesus’ public ministry and revealed the unity of the Father, Son, and Spirit, foreshadowing the spiritual transformation believers would experience through faith in Christ.

<!-- p19 -->
Later, Jesus commanded His disciples to baptize, instructing them to “go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19</span>). Water baptism, as instituted by Christ, became an **act of obedience** and a **public declaration of faith** for believers. It signifies their identification with Jesus’ death, burial, and resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 6:3-4</span>). Going under the water symbolizes dying to sin, and coming up out of the water represents being raised to new life in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Colossians 2:12</span>). Still, it is essential to clarify that this outward act is not the source of salvation, but a **visible sign** of the **internal work of grace** that has already been accomplished by the blood of Jesus.

<!-- p20 -->
Water baptism is a **sign** of the cleansing power of Christ’s blood and the renewal of the Holy Spirit (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 5:26</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="TIT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Titus 3:5</span>). It signifies repentance, faith, and the commitment to follow Jesus. It is the outward testimony of an inward change-a way by which believers publicly declare their allegiance to Christ and their participation in the body of Christ, the church (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:41</span>). Although it was commanded by Jesus and modeled in the early church, it does not **save** anyone. Salvation is by grace alone, through faith (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8-9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 2:8-9</span>), and true cleansing happens spiritually through the blood of Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 1:5</span>). Water baptism is a response to that salvation—an act of obedience, not a condition for it.

<!-- p21 -->
In the New Testament, water baptism is consistently associated with **repentance and faith** (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="38" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:38</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="16" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Mark 16:16</span>), both of which require a personal decision to follow Christ—something infants are not able to do. Baptism symbolizes the believer’s intentional identification with Christ’s death, burial, and resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 6:3-4</span>), as well as their commitment to live in His commands. Some denominations (e.g., Catholic, Lutheran) practice infant baptism as a covenantal sign similar to circumcision, but since infants cannot express personal faith or repentance, the practice of **infant baptism** does not align with the biblical model of **believers’ baptism**. Instead, many churches hold **dedication ceremonies** for infants, following the example of Hannah dedicating Samuel to the Lord (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="1SA" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="27-28" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Samuel 1:27-28</span>) and Mary and Joseph presenting Jesus in the temple (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="22" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 2:22</span>). Dedication is not a substitute for baptism, but a pledge by parents that they will raise their child in the faith, teach them God’s Word, and model a righteous life, until the child is old enough to decide to follow Christ and be baptized for themselves.

<!-- p22 -->
Unlike the **ceremonial washings** of the Old Covenant, which required repeated rituals for purification, Christian baptism is a one-time event that signifies the finished work of grace. The Old Testament washings symbolized outward cleansing and pointed to the deeper spiritual cleansing that comes through Christ. By contrast, New Testament baptism symbolizes the inner renewal and new birth effected by the Holy Spirit (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="TIT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Titus 3:5</span>). It points to the believer’s participation in the New Covenant, where cleansing from sin is permanent and based on Christ’s finished work.

<!-- p23 -->
Water baptism is closely connected to the **promise** of the Holy Spirit. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="38" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:38</span>, Peter calls people to repent, be baptized, and receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. While water baptism publicly signifies the believer’s repentance and faith, the true baptism happens within when the Holy Spirit washes, regenerates, and seals the believer (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="1CO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Corinthians 12:13</span>). This spiritual washing makes us members of the body of Christ and empowers us to live holy lives. The **role** of the Holy Spirit in baptism reminds us that salvation is not through rituals, but through God’s transforming power.

<!-- p24 -->
It is important to distinguish between the **symbolic act** of water baptism and the spiritual reality of salvation through the blood of Christ. The Bible clearly teaches that salvation is based on faith in Jesus (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 10:9-10</span>) and not on works, baptism included (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8-9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 2:8-9</span>). Yet, water baptism is an **act of obedience** that publicly joins the believer to Christ and to the church. It expresses the outward commitment to live as a disciple of Jesus, but it does not add to or complete the work of salvation.

<!-- p25 -->
Christ’s water baptism served as an example for believers to follow, displaying humility, obedience, and readiness for service. For Christians today, water baptism functions as a public declaration of faith, symbolizing death to sin and resurrection to new life. Although it is commanded by Jesus and is an important step of obedience, it is not necessary for salvation. True cleansing comes through faith in the blood of Jesus, and the enabling for the Christian life comes through the work of the Holy Spirit in the believer. Baptism unites us with Christ and His church, reminding us of the inner transformation and renewal that God has already accomplished.

<!-- p26 -->
While water baptism has been established as the outward sign of faith, we now turn to the baptism of the Holy Spirit, which empowers believers for ministry and service.

<!-- p27 -->
## Baptism of the Holy Spirit

<!-- p28 -->
Baptism in the Holy Spirit is a profound and transformative experience that God has promised to empower believers for ministry, service, and holy living. Every believer receives the Holy Spirit at new birth-this is how we are made spiritually alive. But later, we should also receive baptism in the Holy Spirit, which empowers us with boldness, spiritual gifts, and effective ministry.

<!-- p29 -->
While water baptism is the public declaration of faith and signifies the cleansing of sin through Christ’s blood, baptism in the Spirit represents God’s empowering presence in the believer’s life. Together, these two modes of baptism form complementary aspects of the Christian life—one as the outward sign of obedience and the other as the inward source of power and renewal. The baptism of the Holy Spirit is not within, but the Spirit coming upon us. At salvation all believers receive the Spirit within them, but when God’s Spirit comes upon them, they receive power and boldness for ministry.

<!-- p30 -->
The baptism in the Holy Spirit was prophesied by John the Baptist, who declared,

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="fragment" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Matthew 3:11</u>** (NKJV)
>
> I indeed baptize you with water unto repentance, but He who is coming after me is mightier than I, whose sandals I am not worthy to carry. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.
>

<!-- p33 -->
</span>

<!-- p34 -->
This promise pointed to the greater work that Jesus would accomplish after His resurrection—sending the Holy Spirit to indwell and empower His disciples. Jesus confirmed this when He told His disciples to wait for the gift of the Spirit, explaining that they would receive power to be His witnesses (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>). The fulfillment of this promise occurred on the Day of Pentecost, when the disciples were filled with the Holy Spirit, evidenced by speaking in tongues, prophesying, and boldly preaching the gospel (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:1-4</span>).

<!-- p35 -->
This outpouring signaled the beginning of the **Church Age** and showed that the power of the Spirit is now available to all believers. This promise was not limited to the apostles, but extends to all who believe, as Peter declared, “This promise is for you and your children and for all who are far off-everyone whom the Lord our God will call” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="39" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:39</span>).

<!-- p36 -->
### Spirit Within vs. Spirit Upon

<!-- p37 -->
When someone puts their faith in Jesus, the Holy Spirit gives them new life from within. This is what it means to be born again (**regeneration**). At that moment of faith, the Holy Spirit **WITHIN** indwells the believer, making them spiritually alive and adopting them into God’s family (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:5-6</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="TIT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Titus 3:5</span>). This is the new birth, or being born again, as Jesus described in John 3.
