# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
Scripture tells us many stories about sin—its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin is to fall short of God’s standard, or ‘miss the mark,’ an archery term that refers to the archer’s failing to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old and New Testaments use various words for sin, highlighting aspects such as its type, severity, intent, victim, nature, and required penalty. Scripture uses some broad classifications to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sins against God, sins against man, sins against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Deliberate sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- Momentary sin, habitual sin

<!-- p6 -->
- Sins requiring severe penalties such as death, sins requiring financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
Our modern criminal justice systems also have such distinctions: degrees of crime, crimes committed with premeditation or in passion, crimes that require financial restitution, or imprisonment, or even the death penalty. An act can be both a crime and a sin, but crime is a human classification, sin is God’s.

<!-- p8 -->
The Greek and Hebrew words for sin overlap considerably. All sin falls under the broad term ‘missing the mark,’ ***Hamartia*** in Greek and ***Chata’*** in Hebrew. This umbrella term is used for the most serious sins—adultery, murder—but also for less serious sins, such as showing favoritism to the rich in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## A Sacrifice Is Required for Sin

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God kills animals and makes garments for them from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first instance of blood being shed to cover the consequence of sin. The book of Genesis includes a few more such accounts, but God formally established a system of sacrifice for sin in the book of Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a common offering for atonement and devotion to God, often symbolizing total self-surrender. This offering addressed sin in a broad sense, covering both known and unknown sins. The ritual involved the sacrifice of an unblemished male animal—typically a bull, sheep, goat, or bird. The offerer placed a hand on the animal's head, symbolizing identification with the sacrifice, and then the entire animal was burned on the altar. This act of burning the whole offering signified complete dedication to removing sin and aligning one's life with God. The burnt offering emphasized the seriousness of sin and the need for total consecration, expressing reverence and repentance by giving up something valuable.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, called ***Minchah***, was an offering of thanksgiving and dedication, not directly an offering for sin. This offering was given alongside other sacrifices, such as burnt and peace offerings, and was meant to express gratitude, worship, and devotion to God. Made from fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, the grain offering contained no blood. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar for the Lord, while the remainder was given to the priests as food. Though not specifically for sin, the grain offering was an important part of corporate worship, symbolizing the offerer's dedication and gratitude for God's provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This offering often followed sacrifices made for sin and atonement, celebrating the renewed relationship with God after sin had been addressed. The offerer could bring an unblemished male or female animal, such as a cow, sheep, or goat. In the ritual, the fatty portions—the best parts of the animal—were burned on the altar as the Lord's portion, while the remaining meat was shared in a communal meal by the priests and the offerer. The peace offering was unique in that it symbolized community and fellowship, expressing a sense of wholeness and unity with God. After atonement for sin, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God's acceptance and celebrate restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was designated specifically for sins committed unintentionally or in ignorance. When an individual or group realized they had sinned unintentionally, they could bring a sin offering to seek cleansing and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the person's status: a priest or the congregation would bring a bull, a leader would bring a male goat, and an individual would bring a female goat or lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the offering before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and if it was a communal sin, the rest of the animal would be taken outside the camp. This offering demonstrated the seriousness even of unintentional sins and provided a means for the people to remain pure and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, dealt with sins involving a breach of trust, causing harm to another, or desecrating holy things. Unlike the standard burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins where compensation had to be made, such as property-related violations or offenses against sacred objects. To make this offering, the offender would bring an unblemished ram to be sacrificed. If the sin involved harm to another person or property, the guilty party had to return what was taken and add an additional 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering highlighted the importance of acknowledging one's guilt and taking concrete steps to make things right. It illuminated the relational and communal nature of sin, emphasizing that true atonement requires not only seeking God's forgiveness but also making things right with those who have been harmed.

<!-- p76 -->
## Corporate Sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific offerings for individual sin, there were daily, weekly, and monthly sacrifices and offerings for the nation's corporate sin, but the most significant among these is the Day of Atonement.

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16, designed to cleanse the entire Israelite community from their corporate sins over the previous year. This unique observance occurred once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, and it addressed the sins of the nation rather than individual transgressions. It purified the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people, symbolizing both atonement and purification for the whole community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the center of the Day of Atonement were the **two goats**, which symbolized both sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest cast lots to determine each goat's role. The first goat, designated "for the LORD," was sacrificed as a sin offering, and its blood was carried by the high priest into the Most Holy Place. There, the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat - the cover of the ark of the covenant - symbolizing atonement for the people's sins and reconciling them to God. This act of sprinkling was highly significant, as it represented the purification of the holy place and ensured that God's presence remained with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, called the **Scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest would lay his hands on that scapegoat, confess the collective sins of Israel, and thus place them upon that animal. Then the goat was sent into the wilderness to die alone, symbolizing the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act was a powerful depiction of both atonement and release from sin, showing that God not only forgave, but also removed the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God's forgiveness and cleansing, allowing the nation each year to defer the consequence of sin and to postpone judgment. This **atonement did not permanently forgive** the nation's sins, but delayed God's judgment.

## We Have All Sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible address the issue of sin in various ways. When Moses wrote the Law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he recorded specific penalties for specific sins/offenses. I am using the word ‘offense’ interchangeably here, because the books of the Law were the legal system of the day for the Israelites.

<!-- p89 -->
In the Prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in the context of the destiny of the entire nation. When the king and the people disobeyed God, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, defeated in battle, and sometimes carried away into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs, the author writes to his son like a loving father, warning him about the personal dangers of sin and a lifestyle against God's commands. In the Psalms, many of David's writings deal with the personal and emotional consequences of sin.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns against sin, calls people to personal repentance, and offers the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the apostle Paul argues brilliantly how God has planned to deal with sin, and how we can be set free from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is that we are all guilty of sin, and all must pay the price for our transgression.

This false attempt at self-justification ignores the consequences of our deeds, the people we have hurt and against whom we have sinned, and the reality of a holy and perfect God. The Scriptures say that if we deny that we sin, we are liars, and the truth is not in us!
