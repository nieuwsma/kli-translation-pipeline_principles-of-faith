# Chapter 4 - Jesus the Messiah

<!-- p1 -->
Continuing our study of sin, we concluded that sin exacts a serious price—upon those against whom sin is committed and for whom restoration is necessary, and upon those who commit sin and must make amends. The price of sin must be paid. In the Old Testament, God established a series of sacrifices that were performed by priests, individuals, and communities to deal with sin, address it, and resolve it।

<!-- p2 -->
Despite all the sacrifices for sin, they still were not enough—not because God failed, but because people kept choosing sin. While the sacrifice of animals could provide atonement to some extent, no amount of animal sacrifice could change the sinful nature of man।

<!-- p3 -->
God had a remarkable plan, the <u>Messiah</u> (in Hebrew, ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) also called the <u>Christ</u> (in Greek, ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**) meaning “the Anointed One"; a person set apart for a special role (e.g., a king, priest, or deliverer)।

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it is helpful to see what people of his time expected of the Messiah. These expectations shaped how they viewed Jesus and, in many cases, why they found it difficult to recognize him as the fulfillment of God’s plan।

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical Context

<!-- p6 -->
The Jewish people had long been waiting for the coming Messiah. In the period just before Jesus, Jewish ideas about the Messiah were shaped by Old Testament prophecies, whose interpretations varied and were not universally agreed upon. Many Jews expected the Messiah to be a political and military leader who would deliver Israel from foreign oppression (especially Roman rule) and restore the Davidic kingdom. Here is an overview of common messianic expectations and how they did (or did not) align with biblical prophecy:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish Expectations about the Messiah before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A Political and Military Deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would be a king like David, who would lead a victorious revolt against foreign powers and reestablish Israel’s independence।

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical context:** Under Roman rule the Jews were oppressed and heavily taxed, which fostered the hope of a Messiah who would overthrow Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom।

### **2. Restorer of the Davidic Kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would restore the Davidic dynasty and bring a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace।

- **Interpretation:** The focus was often on national restoration rather than spiritual renewal।

Although this kingdom is presently spiritual in nature, it will culminate in a physical reign at his second coming (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="32-33" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:4-6</span>)।

### **3. A Prophet like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief:** Some Jews believed that the Messiah would be a prophet like Moses, who would speak directly with God and speak on His behalf।

His prophetic role goes beyond Moses, as he mediates a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>).

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah will judge the wicked, uphold the righteous, and bring justice for the oppressed.

At his second coming, he will judge the wicked and establish perfect justice for eternity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>).

### Diverse beliefs about the Messiah

<!-- p29 -->
Just as the Christian world today is divided into various denominations, sects, or frameworks of belief, so too were the Jews of Jesus’ era divided. During Jesus’ time, four major Jewish sects shaped Israel’s religious and political landscape, each with different beliefs and expectations about the Messiah. Understanding the background of these diverse groups can provide meaningful context for studying the New Testament Scriptures.

<!-- p30 -->
**Pharisees** were the most influential among the common people, known for their strict adherence to the Law (Torah) and oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, emphasizing personal piety and obedience to God’s commandments. Their expectation of the Messiah focused on one who would uphold the Law, bring justice, and restore Israel both politically and religiously.

<!-- p31 -->
In contrast, the **Sadducees** were a wealthy and politically powerful group who controlled the temple and priestly offices. They rejected oral traditions and recognized only the Torah as authoritative Scripture. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, focusing instead on maintaining their power and ensuring political stability by cooperating with Rome. As a result, they placed less emphasis on the Messiah, since their chief concern was preserving the status quo.

<!-- p32 -->
The **Essenes** were a separatist group who lived apart from mainstream Jewish society, dwelling in secluded communities such as Qumran. They were deeply focused on ritual purity, apocalyptic expectations, and a disciplined lifestyle. The Essenes believed in two Messianic figures: a priestly Messiah who would restore the holiness of the temple, and a royal Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness. This dual-Messiah concept reflected their hope for both spiritual and political renewal. The Essenes are not mentioned directly in the Scriptures.

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, the **Zealots** were militant nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent revolt. They rejected Roman authority and were deeply committed to restoring Israel’s independence. Their expectation of the Messiah was of a warrior-king, like David, who would lead them in battle against their oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p34 -->
While these groups focused on earthly expectations, Jesus’ mission revealed a far greater plan—one that addresses not only political oppression but also humanity’s spiritual bondage to sin.

## The truth about the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### Jesus’ anointing

<!-- p37 -->
The views of the Pharisees and the Zealots about the Messiah illustrate a recurring pattern in human history—rejecting God’s greater plan while seeking salvation through earthly means. As we have seen throughout Scripture, people often turn away from God—even though he is always faithful. Even though God is faithful and accepts us, he is still often forgotten and rejected, leading to cycles of disobedience and decline that have afflicted the kingdoms of the earth.

<!-- p38 -->
While many expected a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a far greater purpose for his anointing—one that meets spiritual and eternal needs. If Jesus had come as a conquering military leader according to the Jewish hope and expectation, the toppling of the kingdom due to human disobedience would only have been a matter of time.

<!-- p39 -->
