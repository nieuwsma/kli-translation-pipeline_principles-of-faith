# Chapter 12 - Discipleship

<!-- p1 -->
Discipleship means following Jesus, learning from Him, and becoming like Him in the way we live and love. It is a lifelong journey of obedience, transformation, and service, guided by the Holy Spirit and rooted in the Word of God.

<!-- p2 -->
Discipleship is the primary command in the Great Commission. Jesus defined this mission as follows:

<!-- p3 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p4 -->
> **<u>Matthew 28:18-20</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And Jesus came and spoke to them, saying, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me.
>
> Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,
>
> teaching them to observe all things that I have commanded you; and behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age." Amen.
>

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

<!-- p6 -->
We, His followers, are to *“go and make disciples of all nations.”* Discipleship is about personal growth and also about multiplying that growth by teaching others to follow Christ. Discipleship is the means by which we build the Kingdom of God; the teachings of Jesus are the message.

<!-- p7 -->
The call to become a disciple is more than merely going to church or learning Bible verses—it is an invitation to live like Jesus and, together with Him, advance the Kingdom of God. Genuine discipleship makes believers spiritually mature and effective for service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:13</span>). It shapes both the heart and the skills needed to serve others and share the gospel. As Paul instructed Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, discipleship follows a pattern of spiritual multiplication, in which trained disciples keep raising up new followers so that others are brought to Christ.

<!-- p8 -->
Discipleship is both a personal calling and a global mission. On a personal level, it involves growth in faith, the development of an active spiritual life, and living in relationship with others & God. At the same time, it fuels the church’s mission to take the gospel to every nation, ensuring that the message of Christ spreads from generation to generation and continues to transform lives.

<!-- p9 -->
## Why Discipleship Matters

<!-- p10 -->
Discipleship’s impact is not limited to personal transformation—it affects the Kingdom. Paul reminded Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> to entrust what he had learned to faithful people who would be able to teach others also. This generational pattern shows that discipleship is the engine that drives the church’s growth and sustainability. Discipleship matters because it was Jesus’ chosen method for spreading the gospel and building His church.

<!-- p11 -->
Rather than focusing on large crowds, Jesus intentionally invested in a small group, teaching them, modeling faithfulness, and preparing them to advance His mission. This personal, relational approach ensures that His message multiplies, as each disciple is equipped to make other disciples.

<!-- p12 -->
In essence, discipleship is at the heart of fulfilling the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). Jesus did not call us merely to make converts, but to make disciples—disciple-followers who are grounded in the Word of God, empowered by the Holy Spirit, and equipped for service. Through discipleship, believers grow in knowledge of Scripture, develop spiritual disciplines, and learn to walk in obedience to Christ. It prepares the church to impact the world, advance the gospel, and manifest God’s glory among all nations.

<!-- p13 -->
### Nature versus Nurture

<!-- p14 -->
In the study of biology, psychology, and anthropology, we often consider the influence of nature and nurture. The question is often asked: Is a person more the result of their internal, innate genetic inheritance? Or are they more the result of their environment and upbringing? The truth is that every person is a blend of nature and nurture. Both elements meet in strong interaction to choreograph the shaping of the human soul.

<!-- p15 -->
When you accepted Jesus, the miracle of salvation made you born again from imperishable seed. You received a divine nature! Your spiritual DNA was rewritten! This divine nature stirs in our hearts a longing to stay near our heavenly Father, to know Him, to experience Him.

<!-- p16 -->
Just as a newborn child—whose good, beautiful, innocent nature must be shaped by loving parents to become a healthy and mature person—so we also need to be formed and nurtured in order to grow.

<!-- p17 -->
Discipleship is the process of spiritual growth that builds upon our divine nature and brings us to spiritual maturity. By investing in discipleship, we ensure that the faith reaches future generations, leaders are developed, and the church remains healthy and vibrant locally and globally.

<!-- p18 -->
### Didactic Model versus Discipleship Model

<!-- p19 -->
In modern Western education, the student’s primary relationship is with the content. The student’s learning is centered on materials, books, videos, assignments, etc. The transformation of learning happens through the tasks of practice. This method of education is called the *didactic model*.

<!-- p20 -->
The paradigm of the didactic model has not always been the standard. The model and method used in Jesus’ time was the *discipleship model*. This model of learning highlights that knowledge is more ‘caught’ than ‘taught’ and it focuses more on the relationship between student and teacher than on the relationship between student and curriculum.

<!-- p21 -->
The didactic model gradually became the primary method over roughly a millennium, from the early medieval universities of Europe in the 11<sup>th</sup> - 13<sup>th</sup> centuries, to the invention of the printing press in the 15<sup>th</sup> century, the Enlightenment of the 17<sup>th</sup> &amp; 18<sup>th</sup> centuries, and the effects of the Industrial Revolution on information and education in the 19<sup>th</sup> &amp; 20<sup>th</sup> centuries.

<!-- p22 -->
The advantage of the didactic model is that it can be scaled more efficiently than the discipleship model. Massively open online courses (MOOCs) can accommodate thousands of students at the same time, whereas even a very skilled master may be able to have only a few dozen disciples at once.

<!-- p23 -->
The outcome of the didactic model is that the student attains mastery of the material, but the student does not become like the teacher. The teacher is a facilitator for the curriculum. The teacher imparts knowledge.

<!-- p24 -->
In the discipleship model, the master not only shares knowledge, he also imparts wisdom. The outcome of the discipleship model is that the student becomes like the master to the extent that the student can replicate the master’s work and train disciples of their own.

<!-- p25 -->
The didactic model also has its use and place in the Kingdom of God; a good example is this book or any other Christian educational material. As a rabbi, skilled and trained in the methods of the rabbis, Jesus primarily adopted the discipleship model for his disciples.

<!-- p26 -->
Jesus describes the discipleship model here:

<!-- p27 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="40" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p28 -->
> **<u>Luke 6:40</u>** (NKJV)
>
> A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone who is perfectly trained will be like his teacher.
>

<!-- p29 -->
</span>

<!-- p30 -->
In the didactic model, the student becomes a carrier of knowledge; in the discipleship model, the student becomes like the teacher. Let us examine the key words of that passage in Luke.

<!-- p31 -->
[See Table 6](../tables/table6.md)

<!-- p32 -->
In summary, the purpose and goal of the discipleship model used in disciple-making is twofold:

<!-- p33 -->
**First, the disciple becomes fully trained**, like the teacher. This does not mean that the disciple imitates everything about their teacher. We are not told to create clones, but to prepare men and women to maturity who can take the message of Jesus to the ends of the earth.

<!-- p34 -->
**Second, now the disciple becomes a teacher for others**. The disciple must pass on what they have learned to someone else, otherwise the message of the Kingdom stops with them. This does not mean that they will necessarily have exactly the same gifts as their teacher or enter full-time ministry, but it certainly means that they must reproduce so that their training may be considered complete.

<!-- p35 -->
Any creature that does not reproduce is barren and its line is broken. A creature that cannot reproduce is not healthy; likewise, a church that cannot reproduce is also not healthy.

## Jesus’ discipleship model and process

<!-- p37 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus demonstrated a mature and complete example of discipleship with his chosen disciples. We should use these steps in detail as the foundation for our journey of being and making disciples.

<!-- p38 -->
In the discipleship model there are two roles: master and student. Every master was once a student, and hopefully every student will one day become a master. I will use the term *disciple* for the student and *disciple-maker* for the master/teacher.

<!-- p39 -->
Every discipleship relationship passes through the progression of eight stages. These stages, or steps, are not completely distinct from each other; often the relationship flows between these stages. In each stage there is a particular emphasis for the disciple and for the disciple-maker. We will study the qualities of disciple-makers in detail later in this chapter.

<!-- p40 -->
The eight stages of discipleship are:

<!-- p41 -->
1.  Invitation/Call

<!-- p42 -->
2.  Teaching

<!-- p43 -->
3.  Demonstration

<!-- p44 -->
4.  Participation/Attempt

<!-- p45 -->
5.  Correction

<!-- p46 -->
6.  Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p47 -->
7.  Multiplication

<!-- p48 -->
8.  Staying Connected

<!-- p49 -->
### Invitation/Call

<!-- p50 -->
Discipleship begins with a personal invitation from Jesus to follow Him. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span>, Jesus called His first disciples, saying, *“Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.”* This invitation was both relational and transformative—it required leaving old ways of life and stepping into a new purpose centered on God’s mission. Jesus’ call was not to be spectators, but to participate in His ministry and the work of His Kingdom.

<!-- p51 -->
Modern disciple-makers should invite others into authentic relationships that foster spiritual growth and obedience. The invitation to discipleship is deeply personal. Jesus meets people right where they are, addressing their unique needs and circumstances, yet He also calls them to go deeper—to grow, to serve, and to become like Him. This pattern reflects the relational nature of discipleship, where the focus is not merely on knowledge but on a life-transforming relationship with Christ.

<!-- p52 -->
### Teaching

<!-- p53 -->
Teaching is at the heart of discipleship. Jesus taught the principles of the Kingdom of God throughout much of His ministry, especially in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7). His teachings provided a foundation for living righteously, loving others, and walking in obedience to God. The teaching of God’s Word remains central to discipleship today, because Scripture is the primary instrument for spiritual growth, correction, and training in righteousness (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p54 -->
Disciple-makers should be committed to faithfully teaching the Bible, helping others understand it and apply it to their lives. Teaching is more than merely imparting knowledge—it requires modeling a Christlike way of life so that disciples can see faith in action. Jesus embodied the truths He taught, showing that effective teaching is both spoken and seen. Likewise, disciple-makers should strive to live what they teach, providing practical examples of obedience and faithfulness.

<!-- p55 -->
### Demonstration

<!-- p56 -->
Jesus not only taught His disciples—He also demonstrated how to live out the truths He proclaimed. Jesus showed what it means to love, to serve, and to minister in practical ways. Some examples from the Gospels include Jesus washing the disciples’ feet as an act of humility and service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="13" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 13:12-15</span>), healing the sick, and feeding the hungry. Through His actions, He modeled compassion, faith, and courage, leaving a clear example for His followers to imitate.

<!-- p57 -->
Discipleship requires not only telling but showing. Modern disciple-makers should be transparent about their journey of faith—including both struggles and victories—so that others can see how faith works in real life. Whether it means praying with someone in need, sharing testimonies, or serving the community, discipleship thrives when it is rooted in practical demonstration. Often actions speak louder than words, making demonstration a powerful tool for training others in the faith.

<!-- p58 -->
### Participation/Attempt

<!-- p59 -->
Jesus did not keep His disciples on the sidelines—He actively involved them in ministry experiences. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 9:1-6</span> and <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 10:5-15</span>, Jesus sent the disciples to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out evil spirits. These practical opportunities allowed the disciples to learn by doing, gaining confidence and skill under His guidance.

<!-- p60 -->
Modern discipleship should also include practical participation. Disciple-makers should invite disciples to participate in ministry, whether by leading prayer groups, sharing testimonies, or serving in outreach programs. Providing safe spaces for practice helps disciples grow in boldness while continuing to receive support and encouragement. Learning happens best when it is applied, and participation is the key to turning theory into action.

<!-- p61 -->
### Correction

<!-- p62 -->
Growth often requires correction, and Jesus lovingly corrected his disciples when they misunderstood his teachings or failed in their responsibilities. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-37" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Mark 9:33-37</span>, he addressed their pride by teaching about servant leadership. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="22" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="31-32" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 22:31-32</span>, he prayed for Peter, preparing him to recover from failure and strengthen others.

<!-- p63 -->
Correction in discipleship should be grace-filled and constructive—rooted in Scripture and focused on restoration (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 4:2</span>). It is not about accusation, but about helping disciples grow and avoid pitfalls. Disciple-makers should provide accountability, using God’s Word to guide and encourage growth, while themselves modeling humility in receiving correction.

<!-- p64 -->
### Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p65 -->
Jesus not only taught and corrected—he empowered his disciples to fulfill his mission. He gave them authority to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out evil spirits (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:18-20</span>). After his resurrection, he promised the Holy Spirit as the source of power for their ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>).

<!-- p66 -->
Modern discipleship should also include empowering others to operate in their spiritual gifts and serve effectively. This includes identifying gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:11-13</span>), encouraging their use, and sending disciples out for ministry. Empowerment also emphasizes dependence on the Holy Spirit for power, boldness, and wisdom, ensuring that disciples are spiritually prepared for the work to which God has called them.

<!-- p67 -->
### Multiplication

<!-- p68 -->
The goal of discipleship is not merely personal growth—it is multiplication. Jesus modeled this by equipping disciples to make other disciples (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). After his resurrection, the apostles continued this multiplying mission, spreading the gospel and planting churches throughout the known world. Paul’s charge in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> reinforces this principle, urging Timothy to teach those who will teach others.

<!-- p69 -->
Discipleship involves equipping disciples to disciple others, creating a chain reaction of faith that expands the kingdom of God. This process requires intentionality—teaching disciples how to mentor, teach, and train others, while sending them with conviction and purpose (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 10:15</span>).

<!-- p70 -->
### Stay Connected

<!-- p71 -->
Discipleship does not end when someone is sent. Jesus modeled ongoing connection with his disciples, continuing to encourage and direct them even after his resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="21" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 21:15-19</span>). The early church also maintained this pattern, regularly gathering for teaching, fellowship, and prayer (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>).

<!-- p72 -->
Modern discipleship should also cultivate ongoing relationships, providing accountability, encouragement, and support as disciples continue to grow and lead. Maintaining unity in the body of Christ ensures that disciples are never alone, but remain strengthened and encouraged in their journey of faith.

<!-- p73 -->
## Who Does Discipleship?

<!-- p74 -->
Every person in the body of Christ is responsible first to become a disciple, and then to contribute to the process of making disciples. There is diversity of gifts and roles, so our contributions may look different, but we are all responsible. In the local church, the leaders are ultimately most responsible for the state and health of discipleship (**elders and deacons - <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 14:23</span>**), but everyone plays a part.

<!-- p75 -->
Discipleship is a living, growing process—like nurturing a child to maturity. Healthy discipleship brings strength and life to people. Just as parents strengthen and nurture a child, thereby making them healthy and mature, so disciple-makers nurture, strengthen, and help disciples become mature. The ultimate goal of discipleship is not the ‘transfer of knowledge,’ but transformation.

<!-- p76 -->
We all must become complete (mature) and make disciples. Although our gifts will differ, there are some common characteristics for all disciple-makers.

<!-- p77 -->
### Characteristics of a Disciple-Maker

<!-- p78 -->
A disciple-maker is one who not only follows Christ but is also committed to helping others grow in their faith. This role requires certain specific characteristics that demonstrate both spiritual maturity and practical commitment. Disciple-makers must continually rely on God’s power, wisdom, and grace in order to overcome the challenges that come with leading others in their spiritual journeys.

<!-- p79 -->
#### Rooted in Christ

<!-- p80 -->
The foundation of a disciple-maker’s life must be deeply rooted in Christ. Jesus said, *“I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from me you can do nothing”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="15" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 15:5</span>). Effective disciple-makers know that the ability to grow and help others comes from staying close to Jesus daily. Yet resistance to personal growth and spiritual transformation can hinder this dependence. Disciple-makers should continually cultivate their personal relationship with Jesus through prayer, study of Scripture, and obedience, becoming an example to those they are discipling of what it means to abide in Christ.

<!-- p81 -->
#### Committed to the Mission

<!-- p82 -->
A disciple-maker must remain committed to the disciple-making mission given by Jesus in the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). This mission requires intentional focus and priority, recognizing that disciple-making is not an optional task but the central purpose of the church. Yet challenges such as time constraints, busyness, and personal distractions can make it difficult to stay focused. Disciple-makers should intentionally set aside time for mentoring, training, and relationship-building, and view disciple-making as a lifestyle rather than an occasional activity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 19:10</span>).

<!-- p83 -->
#### Led by the Spirit

<!-- p84 -->
The work of disciple-making cannot be done by human strength alone; it requires the guidance and empowerment of the Holy Spirit. Jesus promised His disciples that when the Holy Spirit came upon them, they would receive power (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>), equipping them to be witnesses and servants of the gospel. Disciple-makers should remain sensitive to the Spirit’s leading, and trust His wisdom so they can wisely navigate complex situations, cultural barriers, and difficult conversations. This dependence on the Holy Spirit enables disciple-makers to operate in love, boldness, and spiritual discernment, ensuring that their efforts are Spirit-driven rather than self-driven.

<!-- p85 -->
#### Humble and Teachable

<!-- p86 -->
Humility is the mark of an effective disciple-maker. As Paul taught, *“Do nothing out of selfish ambition or vain conceit. Rather, in humility value others above yourselves”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 2:3</span>). Disciple-makers recognize that they themselves are lifelong learners who must remain teachable and accountable. They welcome correction and feedback, modeling humility for those they mentor. Pride can become a major obstacle to growth, for both disciple-makers and their disciples. Embracing accountability and maintaining a servant’s heart fosters trust and creates an environment for growth and transparency.

<!-- p87 -->
#### Patient and Persevering

<!-- p88 -->
Discipleship is rarely instantaneous—it is a long-term process that requires patience and perseverance. Paul reminds believers that, *“Let us not grow weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:9</span>). Growth in faith often includes setbacks, questions, and failures, but disciple-makers are called to walk alongside others, providing encouragement and support at every step. It is easy to become discouraged when progress seems slow, but disciple-makers trust that God is at work, even when results are not immediately visible.

<!-- p89 -->
Being a disciple-maker requires a blend of spiritual dependence, commitment, and humility. It is a calling that demands patience and perseverance, while remaining Spirit-led and teachable. Disciple-makers are not expected to be perfect, but they should be faithful—to abide in Christ, invest in others, and trust God to bring growth and transformation in His timing. These qualities reveal the love of Christ and help others become mature disciples who can, in turn, make other disciples.

<!-- p90 -->
## Practical Tools for Discipleship

<!-- p91 -->
Effective discipleship requires intentional tools and practices that encourage spiritual growth, accountability, and fellowship. These tools help disciples build a strong foundation in faith, grow in closeness with God, and be equipped to make other disciples. Practical approaches such as Bible study, prayer, small groups, and mentoring provide both the structure and the support needed for ongoing growth.

<!-- p92 -->
### Bible Study

<!-- p93 -->
Deep engagement with Scripture is the foundation of discipleship. Jesus emphasized the importance of God’s Word in spiritual growth, declaring that, *“Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:4</span>). Regular Bible study and memorization enable disciples to internalize truth, counter lies, and apply God’s wisdom to daily life.

<!-- p94 -->
Disciples should be encouraged to immerse themselves in Scripture, not merely as an academic exercise, but to know God and transform their hearts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="119" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="105" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 119:105</span>). Practical methods such as reading plans, study guides, and group discussions provide opportunities to explore doctrine and discuss application in community. By establishing regular study habits, disciples lay a strong foundation for spiritual maturity and fruitful ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p95 -->
### Prayer

<!-- p96 -->
Prayer is the lifeline of discipleship, connecting believers to God’s presence, power, and guidance. Jesus taught His disciples how to pray in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 11:1-4</span>, emphasizing both dependence on God and alignment with His will. Through prayer, disciples learn to seek God’s wisdom, intercede for others, and receive strength for service.

<!-- p97 -->
Teaching disciples how to pray—individually and corporately—equips them to develop closeness with God and discern His leading (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 4:6-7</span>). Incorporating prayer journals, prayer groups, and fasting practices fosters spiritual discipline and empowerment, ensuring that disciples remain Spirit-led and mission-focused.

<!-- p98 -->
### Small Groups and Fellowship

<!-- p99 -->
Discipleship flourishes in community. The early church modeled relational discipleship through fellowship, prayer, and breaking bread together (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>). Small groups and accountability partnerships provide a safe place for disciples to grow together, share struggles, and celebrate victories.

<!-- p100 -->
The local church serves as the central hub for discipleship relationships and provides opportunities for group Bible studies, prayer meetings, and fellowship events. These gatherings enable disciples to practice love, bear one another’s burdens (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:2</span>), and encourage one another toward spiritual maturity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="24-25" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:24-25</span>). Small groups also provide a framework for multiplying leaders, as mature disciples equip and send others so that they can lead groups and mentor new believers.

<!-- p101 -->
### Mentoring

<!-- p102 -->
Mentoring provides a personal and purposeful approach to discipleship. Jesus modeled this by investing deeply in the twelve disciples, walking alongside them through teaching, correction, and encouragement. Similarly, one-to-one relationships allow disciple-makers to tailor guidance to individual needs, helping disciples grow in faith, character, and leadership.

<!-- p103 -->
Mentors serve as accountability partners, providing support, prayer, and wisdom during times of growth and struggle. Practical tools such as spiritual assessments, goal setting, and regular check-ins ensure that disciples remain focused and motivated. Peer relationships within mentoring structures also foster mutual growth, as both mentor and disciple learn from shared experiences (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PRO" data-chapter="27" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Proverbs 27:17</span>).

## **Personal Reflection Questions**

<!-- p105 -->
1.  What does being a disciple of Jesus personally mean to you? How are you actively following him in your daily life?

<!-- p106 -->
2.  Reflect on Jesus’ model of discipleship. Which aspect of his approach—inviting, teaching, showing, or empowering—stands out to you the most, and why?

<!-- p107 -->
3.  How can you multiply your faith by making disciples of others? What challenges or fears might hold you back, and how can you overcome them? (Note that I said multiply your faith, not your disciples!)

<!-- p108 -->
4.  Which qualities of a disciple-maker (e.g., patience, humility, relational focus) do you see in yourself? Which qualities do you feel prompted to develop further?

<!-- p109 -->
5.  Think about the people in your life. Whom might God be calling you to disciple? How can you take the first step in building that relationship?
