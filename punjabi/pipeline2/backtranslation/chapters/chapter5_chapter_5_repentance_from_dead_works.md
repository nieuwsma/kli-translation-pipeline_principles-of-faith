# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
Often in Christianity we consider repentance to be fundamental only for the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The core meaning of repentance is much deeper than just turning from sin and going toward salvation. Repentance from dead works means that we turn our hearts away from those things that harm us or hinder our growth-and turn toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews has placed ‘repentance from dead works’ first in the list of essential teachings after the foundational elementary principles of the Messiah (chapter 4).

### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever had a job knows what work is. Work is the collection of tasks you do in order to get paid so that you can earn your livelihood (employment). Employment is an agreement in which you exchange your talents and efforts for something of value that helps keep your life going. You do work because you get something from it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can provide a sense of purpose, often a sense of identity. People often ask, ‘What do you do for a living?’ and the answer begins with a statement of identity, ‘I am’. While work can be enjoyable and purpose-filled, it is very likely to be physically or mentally hard, difficult, challenging, and stressful. If your job is none of these, you may find it very menial and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work a 40-hour job in a week, and sleep 8 hours each night, then you spend 36% of your waking hours working, and that does not include the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting to and from work, handling household chores, and all those other necessary tasks you have to do to keep your life on track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? It can vary, but by definition because we get something valuable from it.

<!-- p13 -->
If they stopped paying you, would you keep working? For most of us, the easy and painfully obvious answer comes very quickly: no!

<!-- p14 -->
If a job were killing you, would you continue working in it? I think that depends on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
In the context of combining ***nekros*** with ***erga***, the meaning is labor or work that is dead, that has no vitality, that cannot reproduce! The author of Hebrews is warning us against labor that is dead.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, several criteria are used to determine whether a living organism is alive or not (this is a partial list used for our purpose):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living things convert energy through metabolic processes in order to grow, develop, and maintain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: Life involves growth and progression over time, with a specific life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living things have the ability to reproduce, through which they produce offspring or new individuals.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adjust itself according to changes occurring in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as temperature regulation or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, organisms can adapt to their environments through genetic changes, which makes survival in various conditions possible.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this criterion of ‘life’ to our deeds with a bit of creative license, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  do not allow personal growth or progress.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  do not reproduce, cannot produce any positive or desired outcome.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  do not respond to external stimuli, the light of God’s presence, or the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  do not maintain internal stability, become uncontrolled, are not self-controlled.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  do not adapt (are too rigid) and no longer help us survive.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s will for us. God wants to give us life!

Where dead and living are a dichotomy. Death is a spectrum. Our dead works may not yet be completely dead in every category, but they will be! Dead works are like poisons; some have stronger effects.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by stopping its normal functioning. Some poisons have minor effects, like a headache. Others affect our thinking, our way of seeing, or even whether we can have children (living fruitfully). Dead works are exactly the same. They may sometimes be only a headache, or they can also be the cause of death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are all actions that, upon review and reflection, are not bringing the abundant vitality promised by Jesus. Having extramarital relations is a very potent poison; its full and negative effect is likely to set in quickly. Being needlessly stubborn and resentful will slowly poison a person, until they have driven everyone around them away from them.

<!-- p36 -->
To be rid of dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
‘Repenting from dead works’ means turning to God and leaving behind whatever is harming our journey with Him - beginning with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Continuing the poison analogy for dead works, if you learned that your favorite snack is loaded with toxic heavy metals that, if eaten, would put you in a fatal condition, what would you do? We would, without hesitation, cast it away from ourselves, and never enjoy it again. Understanding that eating it amounts to our untimely, and perhaps painful, death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We should turn away from those things in this life that are poisoning us and leading us to both natural and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to look a little deeper into the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The word used in Hebrews is *repentance*. This is compared with the word *Repent,* for which an example is found in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
Repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or event; it means to change one's mind or purpose; to turn from sin and turn toward God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is the state of having repented; it means a change of mind; the condition of having turned from sin and turned toward God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews ‘*repentance from dead works*’ He is speaking about being in the state of having repented! We are to live in the continual state of ‘remaining turned away’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How often do we need to repent? Trick question! We must keep changing our mind until we no longer keep doing dead works. Repenting is not **asking for forgiveness, or expressing regret**, although that may be included at times. Repenting is turning away from the previous way of thinking.

### Mechanisms of change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap when it comes to changing our minds. If we never even feel the need to change our minds, then why would we ever open our eyes to look for change? Then, returning to the poison analogy, if you can’t feel that the poison is killing you, why would you stop consuming it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that a person simply has to hit ‘rock-bottom’, then he will look at his life and decide to make a change. Experience and psychology tell us that we humans are more complex. In the study of psychology there are many theories related to behavior change, one highly respected theory is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** developed by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente in the late 1970s **\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People can pass through a cycle of six stages for change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: There is no intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: There is an intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken toward change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has been changed for a short time.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has been changed and is being maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: There is no desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no set duration for how long any stage takes. Some people never progress through these stages in a meaningful way regarding certain issues in their lives. In other cases, we can move from stages 1 to 6 very quickly.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father who suddenly feels guilt after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *strict* but *loving* parent. He believes that raising his voice is necessary. He is not even thinking about change. In fact, he might even justify it- “My dad raised me like this.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, he snaps and yells at his little daughter more loudly and angrily than ever, harshly. She is startled. Her lip quivers. She walks away silently. In that moment… something changes.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, in shock. The image of her sad face keeps replaying in his mind. His stomach turns. He tells himself, *“I don’t want to become this person.”* He knows he needs to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes to her room, gets down on his knees, apologizes sincerely, and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You didn’t deserve this.”* She hugs him. Motivated to cement the change, he prays and talks with a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he starts reading books on parenting to work on developing positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He is not perfect, but something has truly changed. Now he no longer justifies the old behavior-and he has no desire to go back.

<!-- p79 -->

When Jesus would preach to the crowds, He often met people who did not know that they needed to change. We cannot forcibly change someone’s mind or repent on their behalf. That is not our place, nor is it the example of Jesus.

### Godly Sorrow and the Goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
While hitting absolute rock bottom is more a myth than a sure prerequisite for change, God can use sorrow to influence our minds. God will use sorrow to bring us to repentance. He will convict us about our actions and the state of our lives and hearts so that our eyes are opened and we can see our complete and absolute need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>the experience of sadness or distress that leads to a *metanoia*</u>, is not like worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is also the same experience of sadness or distress, but the world offers no hope, only the promise of relief and death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and grief.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the endurance of pain-physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual-especially when it is prolonged or intense.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is a multifaceted emotional response to significant personal loss, especially relational or existential.

<!-- p100 -->
Grief, pain, and sorrow are closely related concepts. In the human condition we will experience all three; we can learn and grow through all three, but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
Scripture says that every good and perfect gift is from above, from God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow will cause discomfort, but His discipline is just and loving. Just as a good father would not deliberately break his child’s arm “to teach him a lesson,” God likewise will not bring sickness or disease upon His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### An example of worldly sorrow versus godly sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick, and he intentionally hit her with it, which made her cry and left a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
Their father heard the noise and came to investigate, and when he learned what his son had done, he took the stick, broke it in half, and threw it away. He sent his son inside to think about what he had done, and comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
Coming inside to deal with his son, the father found him crying. When he asked why he was crying, his son said, “Because you broke my stick.”

<!-- p106 -->
The father’s response was, “I’m sad that you care more about your stick than your sister, whom you hurt,” and he sent him back to his room for further reflection.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later his son came back out and apologized. He said that he was still upset because his dad had broken his stick, and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example the boy experienced both worldly sorrow for his lost stick, and ultimately godly sorrow for his injured sister.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and the goodness of God are interconnected. God is a good Father who will allow us to bear the consequences of our actions but will still stand with us as we go through those consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
The unwavering goodness of God—that if we come to Him He is ready to deliver and restore us—is a positive force on our path to repentance. His goodness is not that we remain in sin, but that despite our sin He makes a way for our deliverance and that we may experience His salvation.

<!-- p112 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

Despite God’s goodness, mercy, and grace, some people do not repent and do not change; they remain trapped in a cycle of despair and pain. By contrast, for some people, merely the example of sin and its consequences in someone else’s life is enough to change their thinking.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a great capacity for change; it is woven into our very nature. Humans are highly adaptable; we can learn and develop, and find better ways of doing things. However, the capacity to change is not the same as the desire or motivation to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I don’t know why some people remain stuck and others are so ready for change, but I can say that the entire process of change is in fact by the grace of God.

Repentance can be a completely natural process, but repentance is also the work of the Spirit who calls us, opens our eyes to the truth, changes our hearts, and calls us toward being transformed. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:1</span> reminds us that some people will be ‘caught’ in a trespass (sin) and will need help to get out! ### An Example of David’s Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps there is no fuller example of repentance in the Scriptures than the story of King David’s sin found in 2 Samuel 11-12. It begins with King David remaining in Jerusalem instead of going to war, where he sees Bathsheba bathing. Despite her being the wife of Uriah—one of his loyal soldiers—David summons her, and they have an illicit relationship that results in Bathsheba becoming pregnant.

<!-- p125 -->
To cover up his actions, David calls Uriah back from the battlefield, hoping that he will go home with Bathsheba and appear to be the father of the child. But Uriah, out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, refuses to go home to Bathsheba. David then has him assigned to the front lines, where he is eventually killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David quickly marries Bathsheba to conceal the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
Yahweh is displeased with David’s actions and sends the prophet Nathan to confront him. When rebuked by the prophet, David immediately says, "*I have sinned against God*" (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify his actions or make excuses. This confession was the first step of his repentance—acknowledging his guilt directly before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I know my transgressions, and my sin is always before me,*” convey his full responsibility for his actions. He grieves not only for the sin but also for having grieved God, saying, “*Against You, You only, have I sinned.*” David displayed a “*broken and contrite heart,*” an essential element of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he deeply mourned the sin he had committed and the harm it caused. This deep regret signaled that he wanted not merely forgiveness but a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David prayed to God, “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He desired an inner transformation that would keep him from repeating the same mistakes. Rather than only asking for forgiveness, David pleaded with God to wash him, saying, “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He acknowledged that only God could purify his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be spiritually renewed further shows his genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) indicates that he hoped to remain in harmony with God in every part of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Even though God removed David’s sin, the Lord struck the child, and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded before God for the child’s life, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find a personal prayer of repentance and a public acknowledgment of his transgressions. David’s decision to write his plea for mercy—which produced one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible—shows that he was willing to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony of repentance and restoration for others, memorializing his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not merely about feeling sorrow; it was a profound change in his heart and life. Through confession, acknowledgment, and the heart-level transformation recorded in Psalm 51, David turned his sin into a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most significant examples of true repentance in the Bible.
