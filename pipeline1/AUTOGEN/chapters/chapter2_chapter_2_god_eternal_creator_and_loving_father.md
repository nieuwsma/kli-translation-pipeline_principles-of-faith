# Chapter 2 - God-Eternal Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is crucial that we establish a firm foundation in the knowledge of who God is. We can come to understand by studying:


<!-- p2 -->
- His desires, intents, and actions,


<!-- p3 -->
- His nature, character, and attributes,


<!-- p4 -->
- What is said of Him and what He says of Himself.


<!-- p5 -->
Scripture reveals God’s characteristics uniquely, with each facet deepening our understanding of the unchanging, eternal God.


<!-- p6 -->
Summarizing God in one descriptor is challenging, if not impossible, as He is too vast and multifaceted for simplification. While we can, and will, make great efforts to explain, describe, and depict God, ultimately like art and music, He must be experienced to be understood.


<!-- p7 -->
When a person listens to music, they describe it by expressing how it moves them-how it resonates within, beyond just the notes and rhythms. A melody might carry warmth or intensity, with a rhythm that pulses like a heartbeat or crescendos that create a sense of exhilaration or longing. Music can soothe like a gentle wave or electrify like a storm, lifting the listener’s spirits, grounding their thoughts, or transporting them to another place entirely. This experience is far more than the sequence of sounds; it is the soul of the music that lingers, captured in how it shapes the listener’s emotions and personal journey.


<!-- p8 -->
Similarly, a viewer of a painting looks beyond the colors or brushstrokes, exploring what these elements embody. A landscape might evoke a serene escape, while an abstract piece can stir curiosity or introspection. Certain shades can bring warmth, while a composition may create a sense of calm, chaos, or beauty. The painting becomes a living experience, provoking a shift in perspective or a deep emotional reaction that words alone can’t capture. Both music and art, then, transform into more than their physical forms-they become encounters that touch something essential, changing the listener or viewer in profound and personal ways.

## Exploring God Through the Old Testament


<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: The God Who Dwells With His Creation


<!-- p11 -->
A central theme in the narrative of God is His **desire to dwell** with His creation. This is clear in the stories throughout Genesis. Across its fifty chapters and genealogies from Adam (the first man) through Joseph (that last major figure in the book of Genesis, the last significant leader of God’s people before Moses), there is a period of approximately ~2,400 years. We can see through these stories the consistency of God’s goodness despite man’s wickedness.


<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God establishes relationship with select individuals.


<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - The first humans, created by God and placed in the Garden of Eden. God walks and talks with them, giving them instructions and establishing a relationship of mutual love and obedience. However, this relationship is impacted by their disobedience in eating from the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil (Genesis 1-3).


<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - Although Cain ultimately rebels by murdering his brother Abel, God speaks directly to him, warning him about the danger of sin and offering him guidance before the murder. Even after Cain’s punishment, God marks him to protect him from harm (Genesis 4).


<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - God establishes a unique relationship with Noah, a righteous man, in a time of widespread corruption. God commands Noah to build an ark to save his family and the animals from the Flood. Afterward, God makes a covenant with Noah, promising never again to destroy the earth with a flood and establishing the rainbow as a sign of this covenant (Genesis 6-9).


<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - One of the most significant relationships in Genesis, God calls Abram to leave his homeland and promises to make him the father of a great nation. God establishes a covenant with Abram, promising land, descendants, and blessings for all nations through him. Abram’s faith and obedience become central themes, and his name is changed to Abraham as part of the covenant (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22).


<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - An Egyptian servant of Sarah, Hagar encounters God in the wilderness after being mistreated. God comforts her, promises her a future for her son Ishmael, and instructs her to return to Abraham and Sarah. Hagar refers to God as “El Roi” (“The God Who Sees Me”), recognizing His care for her (Genesis 16).


<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - The son of Abraham and Sarah, Isaac inherits the covenant promises made to his father. God reaffirms these promises directly to Isaac, including the promise of descendants and blessings. God also guides Isaac during a famine, protecting him and establishing the covenant relationship (Genesis 26).


<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - Although her relationship with God is indirect, God answers Rebekah’s prayer about her difficult pregnancy, revealing that her twins, Jacob and Esau, will become two nations with distinct destinies (Genesis 25).


<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God establishes a personal relationship with Jacob, appearing to him in a dream and confirming the covenant promises of land, descendants, and blessings. Jacob encounters God several times, including the famous wrestling match where God changes his name to Israel, symbolizing his transformation and the establishment of Israel as a people (Genesis 28, 32, 35).


<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Although Joseph’s relationship with God isn’t based on direct appearances or covenants like those of Abraham or Jacob, God is deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, divine favor, and providence, God guides Joseph’s journey from being sold into slavery to becoming a powerful leader in Egypt. Joseph’s faith in God’s sovereignty allows him to forgive his brothers, recognizing that God used his suffering for good (Genesis 37, 39-50).


<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this narrative God, who wants to dwell with His creation, has to settle for dwelling with select individuals, and ultimately through covenant with Abraham establishes a people (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Genesis" data-chapter="17" data-verses="7" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Genesis 17:7</span>).


<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: The God Who Dwells With His People


<!-- p24 -->
God’s desire to dwell with His people is also clearly seen in the second book of the Bible, Exodus. In the book of the exodus the people of Israel, who had lived for a period of time in relative peace and harmony in Egypt, are now being oppressed by a Pharaoh who did not know of Joseph and feared the Hebrews (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Exodus" data-chapter="1" data-verses="6-8" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Exodus 1:6-8</span>). God’s people lived in Egypt for roughly ~400 years (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Exodus" data-chapter="12" data-verses="40-41" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Exodus 12:40-41</span>), of which the later portion of that was spent living as slaves (there is not a universal consensus on precisely how long Israel was in bondage to Egypt).


<!-- p25 -->
In the powerful presence of the burning bush that was not consumed, God reveals Himself to a Hebrew-born, Egyptian-raised, murderer in exile, who is tending to his father-in-law’s flock in the desert, Moses.



<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="Exodus" data-chapter="3" data-verses="14" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">


<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV) *And God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, 'I AM has sent me to you.' "*


<!-- p28 -->
</span>



<!-- p29 -->
The phrase **“I AM THAT I AM”** (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is one of the most profound self-revelations in Scripture. When Moses asks for God’s name, God responds with this phrase, which can be translated as "I Am Who I Am," "I Will Be Who I Will Be," or simply "I Am." This declaration on the surface is a bit confusing but holds deep meaning. Could you imagine introducing yourself to someone new as ‘I am’? When God reveals Himself as ‘I AM THAT I AM’ He is attesting to His nature, His eternal presence, His sovereignty. ‘I am’ is infinite in meaning and purpose.


<!-- p30 -->
God uses Moses to reveal Himself to Pharaoh:



<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="Exodus" data-chapter="7" data-verses="16" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">


<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Exodus 7:16</u>** (NKJV) *And you shall say to him, 'The LORD God of the Hebrews has sent me to you, saying, "Let My people go, that they may serve Me in the wilderness"; but indeed, until now you would not hear!*


<!-- p33 -->
</span>



<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh, God is the ‘I AM’ and that God demands the Israelites be released so that they may worship Him in the wilderness.


<!-- p35 -->
Plagues and misery ensue, but ultimately Pharaoh relents and lets the Israelites leave.



<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-type="quote" data-book="Exodus" data-chapter="12" data-verses="31" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">


<!-- p37 -->
> **<u>Exodus 12:31</u>** (NKJV) *Then he called for Moses and Aaron by night, and said, "Rise, go out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel. And go, serve the LORD as you have said.*


<!-- p38 -->
</span>



<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness God miraculously delivers His people from the vengeful Egyptian army (Exodus 14), provides protection from the sun and a pillar of fire to keep them warm in the cool night of the desert (Exodus 13), food from heaven (Exodus 16), and water to drink (Exodus 17).


<!-- p40 -->
God brings His people to Mount Sinai where God commands Moses to consecrate the people and gives them the law (including the 10 commandments) and establishes His covenant with them (the old covenant) (Exodus 19, 20, 24). This covenant (the Mosaic covenant) builds upon the Abrahamic covenant and lasts from Moses to Jesus.


<!-- p41 -->
Despite building a covenant with His people, the relationship is one of a loving and jealous God pursuing and abiding with very stubborn and ungrateful people (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Exodus" data-chapter="32" data-verses="9" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Exodus 32:9</span>). Ignoring God's miraculous intervention, they keep a rebellious attitude for large parts of the text. God even brings them to the promised land and tells them they can inherit it (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Exodus" data-chapter="33" data-verses="3" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Exodus 33:3</span>), but in fear and disobedience they ignore the supernatural deliverance that God has given them, and instead must wander the desert for 40 years until almost everyone from that generation had died (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Numbers" data-chapter="14" data-verses="33-34" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).


<!-- p42 -->
### The Rest of the Old Testament


<!-- p43 -->
In the ~1,400 years between the death of Moses and the birth of Christ the Israelites experienced a recurring cycle of devotion which brings God's favor and blessings, followed by rebellion, God’s judgment, their subsequent repentance, and ultimately God’s deliverance. This pattern is particularly evident in the books of **Judges, Kings, and Chronicles**, which recount cycles where the people turn away from God, often by worshiping other gods, resulting in periods of oppression or exile. In response to their cries, God repeatedly raised up judges, prophets, or leaders to deliver them.


<!-- p44 -->
Throughout these years, despite the Israelites' frequent failures to uphold their covenant with Him, God remains faithful, showing both justice and mercy. He continuously rescues and forgives His people, demonstrating His enduring commitment and love.


<!-- p45 -->
As we see in Scripture, God reveals Himself through actions, relationships, and even names that speak to His character. Each name represents a specific aspect of His total character. These names were given as part of a narrative of how God revealed Himself to His people. For your personal study a reference table is included in the Appendix: *Table 10 - Old Testament Names of God.* Read through these names and study the scripture references. Each reference is a story that depicts precisely how God saved, delivered, and rescued His people.


<!-- p46 -->
Also included in the Appendix is a further derived set of attributes describing His nature, descriptions of His actions and intents: *Table 11 - Attributes of God.* These attributes, while slightly more abstract than the names of God, are no less authentic!


<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand God-Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (the **Trinity**)-working together as one. In our continued study we will examine names and attributes of Jesus and the Holy Spirit in more depth.


<!-- p48 -->
God’s character and actions are fully expressed in the unity of Father, Son, and Spirit. The scriptures teach that God is one in essence, yet exists as three distinct persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Scripture affirms God’s oneness, stating, “beside Him there is no God” (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Isaiah" data-chapter="45" data-verses="5" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Isaiah 45:5</span>). However, the Godhead is understood as a **compound unity**-three distinct persons who cooperate with a singular mind and purpose, united in the truest sense. Each person of the Trinity has a unique role: the **Father creates**, the **Son redeems**, and the **Holy Spirit sanctifies**. The term “Trinity” does not appear in the Bible; it was developed as a theological expression to describe this concept, which is revealed through Scripture.


<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can be illustrated by examples in nature, which help clarify this idea of unity in diversity. Like water, which can be liquid, ice, or vapor but remains H₂O, each person of the Trinity is fully God yet distinct in personhood. Or consider a triangle, with three sides and three angles, forming one complete shape; if one side is removed, it ceases to be a triangle. Similarly, **God’s nature is incomplete without all three persons**, who work together in perfect unity.


<!-- p50 -->
- **God the Father**: He is the creator and sustainer of all things, often understood as the initiator in the Godhead. His love for humanity is central to the biblical story, exemplified in <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="John" data-chapter="3" data-verses="16" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 3:16</span>. In our study we will examine God the father as the loving father and righteous judge, who made a path for our redemption.


<!-- p51 -->
- **God the Son**: Co-existent and co-eternal with the Father, Jesus took on human nature, being conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the virgin Mary. He lived a sinless life, died to redeem humanity, and rose again, defeating sin and death. He now sits at the Father’s right hand, interceding for us (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="John" data-chapter="1" data-verses="1-3" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="Matthew" data-chapter="1" data-verses="23" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="1 Timothy" data-chapter="2" data-verses="5" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In our study we will examine Jesus as the perfect atoning sacrifice, the resurrected Lord, and head of the church.


<!-- p52 -->
- **God the Holy Spirit**: The Holy Spirit, co-existent and equal with the Father and the Son, is sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world of sin and prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. He empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-type="ref" data-book="John" data-chapter="14" data-verses="26" data-lang="en" data-version="NKJV">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In our study we will examine the Holy Spirit as the comforter, given to us by God to equip, empower, sanctify, and abide with us.


<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct persons, God’s triune nature is revealed as a perfect union in purpose, will, and love, each person playing a vital role in the divine mission of creation, redemption, and sanctification. The Trinity invites believers to experience God fully, both as a loving Father, a redeeming Savior, and a transforming Spirit.


<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion


<!-- p55 -->
We can be uniquely known by our names: husband, father, son, brother, friend. Each of these a declaration of who we are in relation to someone else. We can also be known by our attributes: faithful, patient, kind, intelligent, funny. Likewise, we can know God by His attributes and names- He is our Healer, Provider, Shepherd, and Redeemer. However, as labels and words are only a small part of us, for ourselves to be truly known by others, we must be experienced. Even more so with God. To truly and fully know Him we must experience Him.


<!-- p56 -->
God’s names reveal His character, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of what these names signify. For instance, “God my Healer” takes on a richer, more profound meaning when we understand the depths of illness, desperation, or helplessness from which God rescues. Knowing Him as our Provider is transformed when we have felt true need and found Him faithful.


<!-- p57 -->
Scriptures offer a context to these encounters, showing us God’s faithfulness to His people across time and circumstance, reinforcing how His names and attributes are not mere titles but realities He brings to life, experienced in the lives of individuals. It is through this interplay of personal experience and the scriptural narrative that we come to know God-not just as a distant description but as a present, active relationship in every season of our lives.
