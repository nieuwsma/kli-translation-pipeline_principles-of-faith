# Chapter 3 - Sin and Salvation

<!-- p1 -->
The Bible tells many stories about sin—its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin means to fall short of God’s standard, or “to miss the mark,” an archery term that describes the archer’s failure to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old and New Testaments use various words for sin, each highlighting aspects such as kind, severity, intent, victim, nature, and the appropriate punishment. The Bible uses several broad categories to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against others, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Deliberate sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- Momentary sin, habitual sin

<!-- p6 -->
- Sin that requires severe punishment such as death, sin that requires financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In modern criminal justice systems, we have similar distinctions: degrees of offense, crimes that are premeditated or crimes of passion, offenses that call for financial restitution, imprisonment, or even execution. An act may be both a crime and a sin, but crime is a human classification and sin is a divine classification.

<!-- p8 -->
The various Greek and Hebrew words for sin have significant overlap. All sins are broadly described under the umbrella term “missing the mark”; in Greek, ***Hamartia***, and in Hebrew, ***Chata’***. This umbrella term is used for the gravest sins, adultery and murder, but also for lesser sins, such as showing favoritism to the wealthy in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## Sin Requires Sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God killed animals and made garments for them from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first shedding of blood to cover the consequence of sin. There are several other similar accounts in Genesis, but in Leviticus God establishes a formal method of sacrifice for sin.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general offering for atonement and an expression of devotion to God that was often regarded as a symbol of complete surrender to God. This offering encompassed sin in a general way and covered known and unknown sins. Its ritual involved the slaughter of an unblemished male animal - usually a bull, sheep, goat, or bird. The offerer would lay his hands on the animal’s head to show identification with the offering, and then the entire animal was burned on the altar. The complete burning of the offering signified total devotion to removing sin and aligning one’s life with God. The burnt offering emphasized the seriousness of sin and the necessity of complete devotion, and by presenting something valuable, it expressed reverence and repentance.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, called ***Minchah***, was an offering of thanksgiving and devotion, not a direct offering for sin. This offering was presented alongside other offerings, such as the burnt offering and the peace offering, and its purpose was to express gratitude, worship, and dedication to God. This gift was prepared from fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, and it contained no blood. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar as a gift to God, and the rest was given to the priests for food. Although it was not directly for sin, the grain offering was an important part of corporate worship and symbolized the offerer’s dedication and gratitude for God’s provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This offering was often presented after offerings related to sin and atonement and celebrated renewed reconciliation with God after sin had been addressed. The offerer could bring an unblemished male or female animal, such as a bull, sheep, or goat. In its ritual, the fatty portions - the best parts of the animal - were burned on the altar as God’s portion, and the remaining meat was shared between the priests and the offerer in a communal meal. The peace offering was unique because it symbolized table fellowship and participation, reflecting a sense of wholeness and unity with God. After atonement for sin, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and celebrate the peace that had been established.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was specifically designated for unintentional sins or sins committed out of ignorance. When an individual or the community realized they had unintentionally sinned, they could present a sin offering to seek cleansing and forgiveness. The type of animal varied according to the person’s status: a priest or the congregation offered a bull, a leader a male goat, and an ordinary individual a female goat or a lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the offering before God, burn the fat on the altar, and if it was a communal sin, dispose of the remainder of the animal outside the camp. This offering showed that even unintentional sins are serious and provided a way for the people to remain clean and in a right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins that involved a breach of trust, harming another, or disrespecting sacred things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and often pertained to sins that needed to be repaid, such as financial offenses or violations against holy objects. To perform this offering, the sinner brought an unblemished ram for slaughter. If the sin involved harm to another person or their property, the offender also had to make restitution by returning the property along with an additional 20% as a penalty. The guilt offering emphasized the importance of owning one’s guilt and taking concrete steps to set things right. This offering highlighted the relational and communal nature of sin and served as a reminder that true atonement involves not only seeking God’s forgiveness but also setting things right with those who have been harmed by sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Corporate Sin

<!-- p77 -->
In addition to these specific offerings for individual sin, offerings and sacrifices were made daily, weekly, and monthly for the nation’s corporate sin, but the most important of them is the Day of Atonement.

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16 and was intended to cleanse the entire congregation of Israel from their corporate sins over the past year. This unique ceremony, held once a year on the tenth day of the seventh month, addressed the sins of the nation, not individual transgressions. This rite cleansed the tent of meeting, the priests, and the people, and symbolized both atonement and purification for the entire community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the center of the Day of Atonement stood two goats that were used to display both dimensions of sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest would cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat, which was designated “for the Lord,” was slaughtered as a sin offering, and its blood was brought by the high priest into the Holy of Holies. There the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat - the cover of the ark of the covenant - to symbolize atonement for the people’s sins and reconcile them to God. This act of sprinkling carried deep meaning, because it signified the cleansing of the holy place and assured that God’s presence would remain among His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, known as the **scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest would lay his hands on the scapegoat and, by confessing the collective sins of Israel, transfer them to the animal. Then the goat would be sent into the wilderness to die alone, and this was a symbol of the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act was a powerful metaphor of both atonement and deliverance from sin, and showed that God not only forgives but also removes the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God’s forgiveness and cleansing, allowing the nation to defer the consequence of sin and postpone judgment each year. This **atonement did not permanently forgive** the sins of the nation, but delayed God’s judgment.

## We have all sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible focus on the issue of sin in different ways. When Moses recorded the law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he specified particular penalties for particular sins/crimes. Here I also use the word “crime” synonymously, because the books of the Law were the legal system of the time for the Israelites.

<!-- p89 -->
In the prophetic and historical books, sin and its consequences are often described from the perspective of the fate of the entire nation. When the king and the people disobeyed God, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, were defeated in war, and were sometimes taken into captivity and exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs, the writer speaks to his son like a loving father and warns him about the personal dangers of sin and a lifestyle contrary to God’s commands. In the Psalms, many of David’s writings address the personal and emotional consequences of committing sin.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns about sin, calls people to personal repentance, and offers the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the apostle Paul presents a remarkable argument about how God has provided for dealing with sin and how we can be set free from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is that all of us are guilty with regard to sin and must pay the price for our transgression.

Scripture says that if we deny that we sin, we are liars and the truth is not in us!
