# Chapter 2 - God-Eternal Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is essential that we build a firm foundation in knowing who God is. We can arrive at understanding by studying the following:

<!-- p2 -->
- His desires, purposes, and actions,

<!-- p3 -->
- His nature, character, and attributes,

<!-- p4 -->
- What has been said about Him and what He Himself says about Himself.

<!-- p5 -->
The Bible reveals God’s characteristics in a unique way; each dimension deepens our understanding of the unchanging and eternal God.

<!-- p6 -->
To summarize God in a single descriptor is difficult, if not impossible; for He is far too vast and multifaceted to be simplified. Though we can and will strive to explain, describe, and portray God, ultimately, like art and music, He must be experienced to be understood.

<!-- p7 -->
When one listens to music, they describe it by expressing how it affects them-how it resonates within-beyond mere notes and rhythms. A melody may carry warmth or intensity, with a rhythm that beats like a heartbeat or swells that create a sense of passion, joy, or yearning. Music can soothe like a gentle wave or excite like a storm, lift the listener’s spirit, order their thoughts, or carry them entirely elsewhere. This experience is far more than a sequence of sounds; it is the soul of the music that remains, manifested in how it shapes emotions and the listener’s personal journey.

<!-- p8 -->
Similarly, a viewer of a painting looks beyond the colors or brushstrokes and explores what these elements embody. A landscape may evoke a liberating calm, while an abstract work can stir curiosity or introspection. Certain shades can bring warmth, while a composition may create a sense of calm, chaos, or beauty. The painting becomes a living experience and evokes a change in perspective or a deep emotional response that words alone cannot express. Thus both music and art become something beyond their physical forms-encounters that touch something fundamental and transform the listener or viewer in profound and personal ways.

## Exploring God through the Old Testament

<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: The God who dwells with His creation

<!-- p11 -->
A central theme in God’s narrative is His desire to dwell with His creation. This is evident in the stories of the book of Genesis. Throughout its fifty chapters and the genealogies that extend from Adam (the first human) to Joseph (the last significant figure in the book of Genesis, the last prominent leader of God’s people before Moses), we see a period of approximately ~2,400 years. Through these stories we observe the constancy of God’s goodness, despite the wickedness of humanity.

<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God relates to selected individuals.

<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - the first humans whom God created and placed in the Garden of Eden. God walks and speaks with them, commands them, and establishes a relationship of mutual love and obedience. But this relationship is damaged by their disobedience in eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 1-3).

<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - Although Cain ultimately rebels by killing his brother Abel, God speaks to him directly, warns him about the danger of sin, and guides him before the murder. Even after Cain’s punishment, God sets a mark on him to keep him from harm (Genesis 4).

<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - God, in a time of pervasive corruption, establishes a special relationship with Noah, who was a righteous man. God commands Noah to build an ark to save his family and the animals from the flood. God then makes a covenant with Noah that He will never again destroy the earth with a flood and sets the rainbow as the sign of this covenant (Genesis 6-9).

<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - Among the most important relationships in Genesis; God calls Abram to leave his land and promises to make him the father of a great nation. God makes a covenant with Abram and gives the promise of land, offspring, and blessing for all nations through him. Abram’s faith and obedience become central themes, and his name is changed to «Abraham» as part of this covenant (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22).

<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - the Egyptian maidservant of Sarah, who, after being mistreated, encounters God in the wilderness. God comforts her, promises a future for her son Ishmael, and commands her to return to Abraham and Sarah. Hagar calls God "El Roi" ("the God who sees me") and recognizes His care for her (Genesis 16).

<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - the son of Abraham and Sarah; Isaac inherits the covenant promises given to his father. God confirms these promises directly to Isaac, including the promise of offspring and blessings. God also guides Isaac during a famine, protects him, and establishes the covenantal relationship (Genesis 26).

<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - Although her relationship with God is indirect, God answers Rebekah’s prayer about her difficult pregnancy and reveals that her twins, Jacob and Esau, will become two nations with distinct destinies (Genesis 25).

<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God establishes a personal relationship with Jacob and appears to him in a dream, confirming the covenant promises concerning the land, descendants, and blessings. Jacob encounters God several times, including the famous wrestling in which God changes his name to Israel, symbolizing his transformation and the establishment of Israel as a people (Genesis 28، 32، 35).

<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Although Joseph’s relationship with God is not based on direct manifestations or covenants like Abraham’s or Jacob’s, God is deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, divine favor, and providence, God guides Joseph’s path from being sold into slavery to becoming a powerful leader in Egypt. Joseph’s faith in God’s sovereignty enables him to forgive his brothers and to recognize that God used his suffering for good (Genesis 37، 39-50).

<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this narrative, the God who desires to dwell with His creature is compelled to settle for dwelling with chosen individuals, and ultimately, through the covenant with Abraham, He establishes a people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="17" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 17:7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: The God who dwells with His people

<!-- p24 -->
God’s desire to dwell with His people is clearly seen in the second book of the Bible, Exodus. In the book of Exodus, the people of Israel, who had lived for a time in relative peace and harmony in Egypt, are now under the oppression of a Pharaoh who did not know Joseph and feared the Hebrews (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 1:6-8</span>). The people of God lived approximately ~400 years in Egypt (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="40-41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 12:40-41</span>) the latter part of which they spent in bondage (there is no universal consensus regarding the precise duration of Israel’s enslavement in Egypt).

<!-- p25 -->
In the powerful presence of a burning bush that was not consumed, God reveals Himself to Moses, a Hebrew-born man raised in Egypt, a murderer in exile, who is tending his father-in-law’s flock in the wilderness.

<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: 'I AM has sent me to you.' "
>

<!-- p28 -->
</span>

<!-- p29 -->
The expression “I AM THAT I AM” (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is one of the most profound self-revelations in Scripture. When Moses asks God His name, God answers with this phrase, which can be translated as "I am who I am," "I will be who I will be," or simply "I am." This declaration is slightly puzzling at first glance, but it carries deep meaning. Can you imagine introducing yourself to someone you’ve just met with ‘I am’? When God reveals Himself as ‘I AM THAT I AM,’ He testifies to His nature, His eternal presence, and His sovereignty. ‘I am’ is boundless in meaning and intent.

<!-- p30 -->
God uses Moses to reveal Himself to Pharaoh:

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Exodus 7:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And you shall say to him, 'The Lord God of the Hebrews has sent me to you, saying, "Let My people go, that they may serve Me in the wilderness"; but indeed, until now you would not hear!'
>

<!-- p33 -->
</span>

<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh that God is the ‘I am’ and that God demands the Israelites be set free to worship Him in the wilderness.

<!-- p35 -->
Plagues and hardships follow, but eventually Pharaoh relents and allows the Israelites to go.

<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="31" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p37 -->
> **<u>Exodus 12:31</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Then he called for Moses and Aaron by night, and said, "Rise, go out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel. And go, serve the Lord as you have said."
>

<!-- p38 -->
</span>

<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness, God miraculously delivers His people from the vengeful Egyptian army (Exodus 14), protects them from the sun by day and warms them in the cool desert night with a pillar of fire (Exodus 13), gives bread from heaven (Exodus 16), and provides water to drink (Exodus 17).

<!-- p40 -->
God brings His people to Mount Sinai, where God commands Moses to consecrate the people and gives them the Law (including the Ten Commandments) and establishes His covenant with them (the Old Covenant) (Exodus 19، 20، 24). This covenant (the Mosaic covenant) is built upon the Abrahamic covenant and continues from Moses to Jesus.

<!-- p41 -->
Despite making a covenant with His people, this relationship is that of a loving and jealous God who pursues a very stubborn and ungrateful people and dwells with them (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="32" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 32:9</span>). Disregarding God’s miraculous intervention, in large parts of the narrative they maintain a rebellious spirit. God even brings them to the Promised Land and tells them that they can inherit it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="33" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 33:3</span>), but in fear and disobedience they ignore the transcendent deliverance that God had granted them and instead must wander in the wilderness for 40 years until almost all the people of that generation pass away (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-34" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).

<!-- p42 -->
### The rest of the Old Testament

<!-- p43 -->
During the roughly 1,400 years between the death of Moses and the birth of Christ, the Israelites experienced a recurring cycle: faithfulness that brings God’s favor and blessings, then rebellion, God’s judgment, subsequent repentance, and finally deliverance from God. This pattern is especially evident in the books of **Judges, Kings, and Chronicles**؛ books that recount cycles in which the people turn away from God, often by worshiping other gods, resulting in periods of oppression or exile. In response to their cries, God repeatedly raised up judges, prophets, or leaders to save them.

<!-- p44 -->
Throughout these years, despite the Israelites’ repeated failures to remain faithful to their covenant with Him, God remains faithful and shows both justice and mercy. He continually saves and forgives His people and reveals His steadfast commitment and love.

<!-- p45 -->
As we see in the Bible, God reveals Himself through actions, relationships, and even names that speak of His character. Each name represents a particular aspect of His whole person. These names were given in a narrative form of how God made Himself known to His people. For your personal study, a reference table has been included in the appendix: *Table 10 - The Names of God in the Old Testament.* Read these names and consult the biblical references. Each reference is a story that shows exactly how God saved, delivered, and rescued His people.

<!-- p46 -->
Also in the appendix is another set of attributes that describe His nature and the outline of His actions and purposes: *Table 11 - The Attributes of God.* These attributes, though a little more abstract than the names of God, are just as valid and true!

<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand God- the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (that is, the **Trinity**)- who work together as one. As we continue our study, we will examine more deeply the names and attributes of Jesus and the Holy Spirit.

<!-- p48 -->
The character and works of God are fully revealed in the unity of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. The Bible teaches that God is one in essence, but exists as three distinct persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. The Bible affirms God’s oneness, saying: «There is no God besides Me» (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="45" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 45:5</span>). Nevertheless, the Godhead is understood as a compound unity- three distinct persons who cooperate with a single mind and purpose and are united in the truest sense. Each person of the Trinity has a unique role: **the Father creates**, **the Son redeems**, and **the Holy Spirit sanctifies**. The term “Trinity” does not appear in the Bible; this expression was formulated as a theological statement to describe this concept, which is revealed throughout the Bible.

<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can be explained with examples in nature that help clarify this idea of unity in plurality. Like water, which can be liquid, ice, or vapor yet remains H₂O; each person of the Trinity is fully God, though distinct in person. Or consider a triangle, which has three sides and three angles and forms a complete shape; if one of the sides is removed, it is no longer a triangle. In the same way, **the nature of God is incomplete without all three persons**, who work together in perfect unity.

<!-- p50 -->
- **God the Father**: He is the creator and sustainer of all things and is often regarded as the initiator within the Godhead. His love for humanity is central to the biblical narrative, exemplified in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:16</span>. In our study we will consider God the Father as a loving Father and a just Judge, who opened a way for our redemption.

<!-- p51 -->
- **God the Son**: Jesus is coexistent and coeternal with the Father; He took on human nature, was conceived by the Holy Spirit, and was born of the virgin Mary. He lived a sinless life, died to redeem humanity, and rose again, conquering sin and death. He is now seated at the right hand of the Father and intercedes for us (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In our study we will consider Jesus as the perfect atoning sacrifice, the risen Lord, and the head of the Church.

<!-- p52 -->
- **God the Holy Spirit**: The Holy Spirit, who is coexistent and coequal with the Father and the Son, is sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world of sin and to prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. He empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In our study, we will consider the Holy Spirit as the Comforter whom God has given to equip, empower, sanctify, and accompany us.

<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct persons, the triune nature of God is revealed as a perfect union in purpose, will, and love; each person plays an essential role in the divine mission of creation, redemption, and sanctification. The Trinity invites believers to experience God fully; as a loving Father, a redeeming Savior, and a transforming Spirit.

<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion

<!-- p55 -->
We can be uniquely known by our names: husband, father, son, brother, friend. Each of these declares who we are in relation to another. We can also be known by our attributes: faithful, patient, kind, intelligent, funny. Likewise, we can know God through His attributes and names- He is our Healer, Provider, Shepherd, and Redeemer. But since labels and words are only a small part of us, to truly be known by others, we must be experienced. This is all the more true of God. To know Him truly and fully, we must experience Him.

<!-- p56 -->
The names of God reveal His character, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of what these names mean. For example, «my God the Healer» takes on a richer and deeper meaning when we have grasped the depths of the illness, desperation, or helplessness from which God delivers us. Knowing Him as our Provider is transformed when we have tasted real need and found Him faithful.

<!-- p57 -->
The Bible provides a framework for these encounters and shows us God’s faithfulness to His people across time and circumstances؛ and it emphasizes that His names and attributes are not merely titles, but realities that He brings to life and that are experienced in the lives of individuals. It is through this interplay of personal experience and the biblical narrative that we come to know God- not just as a distant description, but as a present and active relationship in every season of our lives.
