# Chapter 4 - Jesus the Messiah

<!-- p1 -->
Relying on our study about sin, we concluded that sin demands a heavy price, against those who have been sinned against and must be compensated, and for those who sin and must make restitution. The price of sin must be paid. In the Old Testament God established a set of sacrifices that were carried out by priests, individuals, and communities to confront sin, settle it, and judge it.

<!-- p2 -->
Even with all the sacrifices for sin, they were still not sufficient—not because God failed, but because people continued to choose sin. Although an animal sacrifice could provide limited atonement, no amount of animal sacrifice was able to transform the sinful nature of human beings.

<!-- p3 -->
God had an astonishing plan, <u>the Messiah</u> (in Hebrew ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) who is also known as <u>the Christ</u> (in Greek ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**) meaning “anointed”; a person who is consecrated for a special role (e.g., a king, priest, or deliverer).

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it is helpful to look at what people in his time hoped the Messiah would be like. These expectations shaped how they viewed Jesus and, in many cases, why they had difficulty recognizing him as the fulfillment of God’s plan.

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical background

<!-- p6 -->
The Jewish people had long been awaiting the coming of the Messiah. Jewish beliefs about the Messiah in the time shortly before Jesus had been shaped by Old Testament prophecies, whose interpretations varied and were not universally agreed upon. Most Jews expected the Messiah to be a political and military leader who would free Israel from foreign oppression (primarily Roman rule) and restore the Davidic kingdom. Here is a survey of common messianic expectations and how they aligned (or did not align) with biblical prophecy:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish expectations of the Messiah before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A political and military deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would be a king like David who would lead Israel in a victorious revolt against foreign powers and restore Israel’s independence.

<!-- p10 -->
- **Referenced verses:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 9:6-7</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JER" data-chapter="23" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Jeremiah 23:5-6</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ZEC" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Zechariah 9:9-10</span>.

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical context:** The Jews under Roman rule were oppressed and paid heavy taxes, which led to the hope for a Messiah who would overthrow Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p12 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus is King, but his kingdom is not earthly (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="36" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 18:36</span>). He established a spiritual kingdom through his first coming, but he will return as the conquering King to judge and to reign in righteousness, as described in the book of Revelation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 19:11-16</span>).

<!-- p13 -->
### **2. Restorer of the Davidic kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would restore the Davidic dynasty and bring a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace.

<!-- p15 -->
- **Referenced verses:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 7:12-16</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="AMO" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Amos 9:11</span>.

<!-- p16 -->
- **Interpretation:** The focus was often on national restoration rather than spiritual renewal.

<!-- p17 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus, as a descendant of David, fulfills this promise by establishing an eternal kingdom. Although this kingdom is spiritual in nature now, it will culminate in a physical reign at his second coming (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="32-33" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:4-6</span>).

<!-- p18 -->
### **3. A prophet like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief:** Some Jews believed that the Messiah would be a prophet, like Moses, who would speak directly with God and speak on his behalf.

<!-- p20 -->
- **Referenced verses:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="DEU" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Deuteronomy 18:15-19</span>.

<!-- p21 -->
- **Expectation:** While many awaited a prophetic figure, they often did not see this role as central to the Messiah, but regarded it as an added aspect.

<!-- p22 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus fulfilled this prophecy as the ultimate Prophet; he spoke the word of God and directly revealed the Father (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:18</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:10</span>). His prophetic role surpasses Moses, for he is the mediator of a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### **4. A Leader Who Will Bring Justice**

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah will judge the wicked, uphold the righteous, and bring justice to the oppressed.

<!-- p25 -->
- **Referenced verses:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 11:3-5</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="72" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 72:1-4</span>.

<!-- p26 -->
- **Interpretation:** The expected justice was largely political and social, not spiritual.

<!-- p27 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus brings ultimate justice. In his first coming, he offered salvation and spiritual justice through the cross (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Colossians 2:13-15</span>). In his second coming, he will judge the wicked and establish complete justice forever (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>).

<!-- p28 -->
### Diverse Messianic Beliefs

<!-- p29 -->
Just as today's Christian world is divided into denominations, sects, or distinct doctrinal structures, the Jews of Jesus’ day were likewise divided. In Jesus’ time, four major Jewish sects shaped the religious and political landscape of Israel, each with distinctive beliefs and expectations regarding the Messiah. Familiarity with the background of these various groups can provide meaningful context for the study of the New Testament writings.

<!-- p30 -->
**Pharisees** were the most influential among ordinary people and were known for their strict adherence to the Law (Torah) and the oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, and they emphasized personal piety and obedience to God’s commands. Their Messianic expectation focused on a figure who would uphold the Law, bring justice, and restore Israel both politically and religiously.

<!-- p31 -->
**Sadducees**, by contrast, were a wealthy and politically powerful group who controlled the Temple and the priesthood. They rejected the oral traditions and accepted only the Torah as authoritative Scripture. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, and instead focused on preserving their power and cooperating with Rome to secure political stability. They therefore placed little emphasis on the Messiah, since their chief concern was maintaining the status quo.

<!-- p32 -->
**Essenes** were a separatist group who withdrew from mainstream Jewish society and lived in isolated communities such as Qumran. They were intensely focused on ritual purity, eschatological expectations, and a disciplined way of life. The Essenes believed in two Messianic figures: a priestly Messiah who would restore the sanctity of the Temple, and a royal Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness. This two-Messiah concept reflected their hope for both spiritual and political renewal. The Essenes are not mentioned directly in the Scriptures.

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, **Zealots** were militant nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent revolt. They rejected Rome’s authority and were deeply committed to restoring Israel’s sovereignty. Their Messianic expectation was of a warrior-king Messiah like David who would lead them in battle against oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p34 -->
While these groups focused on earthly expectations, Jesus’ mission revealed a far greater plan—a plan that addressed not only political oppression but also humanity’s spiritual bondage to sin.

## The Truth of the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### The Anointing of Jesus

<!-- p37 -->
The views of the Pharisees and the Zealots regarding the Messiah reflect a recurring pattern in human history—the search for salvation by earthly means while rejecting God’s greater plan. As we have seen throughout Scripture, people often turn away from God—even though he is always faithful. Even when God is faithful and receives us, he is often forgotten and rejected, and this leads to cycles of disobedience and corruption that ensnared earthly kingdoms.

<!-- p38 -->
While many expected a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a far greater purpose for his anointing—a purpose that met spiritual and eternal needs. If Jesus had come, as the Jews hoped and expected, as a triumphant commander, it would have been only a matter of time before the kingdom fell to ruin because of human disobedience.

<!-- p39 -->
Instead, Jesus established a kingdom that cannot be shaken. His mission was founded not on military power or political supremacy, but on a divine purpose. As the Anointed One, Jesus explained what he was anointed to do and how he would accomplish it:

<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16-21" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p43 -->
Jesus was anointed by God-separated-and was sent with a divine mission, full of purpose and power.

<!-- p44 -->
Jesus proclaimed:

<!-- p45 -->

<!-- p46 -->
- Release - pardon, forgiveness of sin, freedom.

<!-- p47 -->
- The acceptable year (season) of the Lord.

<!-- p48 -->
Those to whom He came were:

<!-- p49 -->
- Brokenhearted - emotionally and spiritually shattered.

<!-- p50 -->
- Blind - spiritually, and sometimes physically.

<!-- p51 -->
- Poor - destitute, in poverty (spiritually and/or physically).

<!-- p52 -->
- Captives - meaning ‘taken with a spear’ which represents those who have been held in bondage against their will.

<!-- p53 -->
Jesus transformed these broken people as follows:

<!-- p54 -->
- Restoration of sight

<!-- p55 -->
- Setting at liberty

<!-- p56 -->
- Binding up, healing, restoring

<!-- p57 -->
The aim of the ministry and service of Jesus was both personal and global, both immediate and enduring. His purpose does not have a single focus, but is part of God’s divine plan to reconcile humanity to God. After sin entered the world, a separation arose between God and man. God was compelled to put man out of the garden and out of His presence. Throughout the Old Testament, man could be with God, but only to a degree. It was very rare that the Spirit of God was given to people, and, on the whole, people lived separated from God. He found a people among the Jews, but He wanted to have a permanent, sure, and complete way to restore His relationship with humanity.

<!-- p58 -->
The presence of God on earth in the time of Jesus was largely limited to the innermost part of the temple, the Holy of Holies. Here, behind the thick veil that separated the inner part from the Holy Place, the presence of God was together with the Ark of the Covenant. Man was separated from God, and God was estranged from His creation.

<!-- p59 -->
God sent Jesus to repair, rebuild, restore humanity, and to reconcile it with Himself. (2 Corinthians 5:17-21)

<!-- p60 -->
### The Essential Work of Jesus

<!-- p61 -->
For the full reconciliation of humanity with God, the mission of Christ required not only proclamation and healing, but also the final act of atonement. Jesus had to become the perfect and complete sacrifice through his death on the cross.

<!-- p62 -->
The book of Hebrews, throughout its 13 chapters, explains in great detail how Jesus became the perfect sacrifice and was both the offering for sin and the High Priest to present it (Hebrews 3 & 4). Jesus’ priesthood is not from the line of Levi (from the tribe of Levi), but in the manner of Melchizedek, who is said to have no beginning or end; that is, not bound by human patterns and customs (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 7:3</span>). The limitation of the Levitical priesthood is that it relies on the blood of bulls and goats (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:12-13</span>) whose sacrifices are not sufficient for the permanent and enduring forgiveness of sins. From our study of sin and sacrifice we know that the consequences of the people’s sins could be postponed for a year (on the Day of Atonement) (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:3</span>), but this sacrifice had to be continually renewed.

<!-- p63 -->
By dying on the cross (accounts of which are found in Matthew 27, Mark 15, John 19, and Luke 23), Jesus shed his blood and paid the price for the past, present, and future sins of all humanity. Isaiah 53 reveals Jesus as the suffering servant who took upon himself the full weight of punishment and sorrow. His once-for-all death became the ultimate and final sacrifice. Jesus is the Lamb of God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="29" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:29</span>), without spot or wrinkle (sin); because he was perfect, complete, and holy, the shedding of his innocent blood made atonement possible. **No other sacrifice is needed or accepted any longer. The old sacrifices were only temporary—but Jesus’ sacrifice was complete and final.**

<!-- p64 -->
Through his death and resurrection, we have a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6</span>); a covenant guaranteed by the blood of God’s own Son. Jesus is the mediator of this covenant; he is the intermediary. Through Christ’s sacrifice, we receive salvation, and he is the author of *‘eternal salvation for all who obey him’* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 5:9</span>).

<!-- p65 -->
The word salvation in Greek is **Sōtēria** (*so-tay-REE-ah - **G4991)**.* Primarily it means **salvation, deliverance, or preservation**. The word encompasses the act of being saved both in a spiritual sense (eternal salvation through Christ) and in physical contexts (deliverance from danger or harm). The term conveys the idea of safety, wholeness, and restoration.

<!-- p66 -->
**Salvation or deliverance:**

<!-- p67 -->
- Refers to the deliverance of humanity from sin and its consequences through Jesus Christ.

<!-- p68 -->
- Used in contexts of eternal salvation (justification, sanctification, and glorification).

<!-- p69 -->
**Preservation:**

<!-- p70 -->
- Connotes being kept, protected, or shielded from danger.

<!-- p71 -->
**Wholeness or healing:**

<!-- p72 -->
- Involves being restored to a state of completeness or health.

<!-- p73 -->
Jesus’ sacrifice paid the price so that we may be forgiven and opened a way for us to have a relationship with God, and established a path for us to remain with him; a path that depends only on our obedience, not on our perfection or our ability to avoid sin.

<!-- p74 -->
When we receive Jesus by faith (to be discussed later), we receive his salvation. This salvation joins us to God and places our name in the ***Lamb’s Book of Life*** (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>). The Gospel of John says:

<!-- p75 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p76 -->
> **<u>John 3:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life.
>

<!-- p77 -->
</span>

<!-- p78 -->
Perishing means being ruined—like fruit that rots before it can be enjoyed. It is a life that falls short of what it was created for. Jesus came to give us a lasting, or eternal, life and to keep us from perishing. Eternal life is not a life that begins after death, but a life that begins the very moment we accept Jesus as our salvation, when we are born “again.”

### Born Again

<!-- p80 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

This happens by faith - by choosing to place complete trust in him, like entering a marriage covenant, with the heart and with the mouth -.

<!-- p84 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

When we were born on earth (born of flesh and water) many things happened: we drew our first breaths and were declared alive, were accepted by our parents, received a name, became heirs, received nourishment and protection, and continued to grow.

When we place our trust in Jesus and receive him, we are born again, not of the flesh, but of the Spirit. We become spiritually alive! Just as in physical birth many things accompany it, spiritual birth also comes with many things:

<!-- p89 -->
1.  When we accepted Jesus, God removed our sins and declared us forgiven (**justified**). He gave us a fresh start. The wall of separation between us and him has been removed. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="TIT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Titus 3:5</span>)

3.  We were **adopted as children** into God’s family and became heirs. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 1:5</span>)

<!-- p92 -->
4.  We receive a source of spiritual nourishment and eternal protection. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="1PE" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Peter 2:2</span>)
