# Chapter 6 - Faith in God

<!-- p1 -->
Faith is a foundational concept in Christianity. The word faith is so common in Christianity that at times its meaning is replaced by subtle misunderstandings and misapprehensions. But it isn’t something you can hold in your hands-this is something that takes shape in your heart when you trust God. It is the persuasion of belief.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews teaches us that we must have ‘faith toward God’.

### Definition of faith

<!-- p10 -->
Faith is an aspect of Christianity that is often misunderstood and misrepresented. As they say, “there is a ditch on both sides of the road”! Because the word faith is used so much in our contemporary Christianity, without proper explanation, it can acquire incorrect definitions that are amplified through misunderstanding. Faith is often portrayed either as a generic assertion of an abstract belief or as a nearly mystical force or “magic,” something you possess in a certain limited measure.

<!-- p11 -->
#### What faith is not

<!-- p12 -->
Faith that is misrepresented, in an <u>abstract</u> way, has no concrete certainty or persuasion. This often becomes apparent with a bit of probing, because this portrayal of faith cannot be specified. If you ask, ‘What do you have faith in’ or ‘Why do you have faith’, a person with this view of faith often cannot explain beyond ‘I have faith’. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1PE" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Peter 3:15</span> says we should be ready to give an answer for the hope that is in us.

<!-- p13 -->
Sometimes people imagine faith as a magical force-but Scripture does not teach this. Faith is about a real relationship built on trust. In Scripture, faith is much more dynamic and relational-it is rooted in persuasion, trust, and confidence in God’s character and promises. Faith is not an impersonal force, but an engaged response to God that shapes thoughts, feelings, and actions.

<!-- p14 -->
Why is belief related to persuasion?

<!-- p15 -->
Our beliefs shape how we see and interact with the world around us. Belief molds perception and motivates our actions. This is how our minds are structured-we live according to what we consider possible.

<!-- p16 -->
We act on what we deem possible and what we perceive and believe. When we walk down a hallway, our brain recognizes the boundaries of the floor and the walls and automatically tells our feet where we can step and what we should avoid. This truth goes beyond our physical navigation of the world around us.

<!-- p17 -->
Suppose you believe it is impossible for you to become financially independent or to have a healthy, active lifestyle. In that case, your actions will follow that belief, and you will, according to your belief, not do what you consider impossible or likely not even try.

<!-- p18 -->
If you believe that God has an unloving and punitive character, that perception of Him will keep you from walking with Him as His child.

<!-- p19 -->
#### Faith is persuasion

<!-- p20 -->
So, what is faith? Persuasion! The Greek word ***pistis*** means persuasion, or the result of being persuaded. We must be persuaded toward God. In English we might say faith (persuasion) ‘**in** God’. If you are married, it is hoped that you have faith ‘**in’** your spouse. You believe that they will do what they have said, and you believe that they are honest and true. This trust should extend beyond what they have said and generally be applied to the person themselves.

<!-- p21 -->
Growing faith, persuasion, and trust in a relationship takes time and experience. Hopefully in life you have learned how to build a relationship based on trust with another person. The principles you have learned that enable you to increase your trust in someone you know also enable you to increase your faith in God. Faith in a relationship grows over time, in experience, with steadfastness, and through small and large actions that build your trust in the other person's character and nature.

<!-- p22 -->
‘Faith toward God’ is a relationship. Relationships are built by two parties, not one. The good news about our faith in God is that God is responsible for His side of the relationship (made possible by the atoning death of Jesus, and being filled with His Spirit in the new birth), and He also helps us on our side (through the work of the Comforter - the Holy Spirit). God does almost all the work; our part (as we will discuss later in this chapter) is to walk *with* Him in obedience.

<!-- p23 -->
### Jesus matures our faith

<!-- p24 -->
Jesus is the founder and finisher of our faith (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 12:2</span>). Our faith is designed to continually grow and expand. We do not reach a single point at which we have enough “faith”; rather, our faith increases. Consider a deep and personal relationship you have in your life today. As with any relationship, we do not reach a distinct milestone at which we now “know” that person. We continue to get to know them and increase our knowledge of them, and this process refines, shapes, and grows our relationship.

<!-- p25 -->
How do we grow our faith in God? By continually experiencing Him across His many revelations, manifestations, and attributes. Again, consider a deep personal relationship in your life. You increase the depth of the relationship by engaging with them in various ways, in different forms, across different media, in different situations. You talk with them, send them messages, perhaps write to them. You see them alone and also in groups. You talk about what matters to you and perhaps challenge one another. These countless ways go on to infinity. Amid these varied expressions, you come to know that person more deeply.

<!-- p26 -->
We all must, through Jesus, know God as our Savior. Think about the names of God (*see the appendix),* merely knowing God as Savior does not mean we have also experienced Him as Redeemer, Healer, Counselor, Lord.

<!-- p27 -->
If you are in a place where you have been persuaded (faith) to know God as your Savior, building this relationship will produce trust in His character within you so that you may know Him in other ways as well.

<!-- p28 -->
I am fortunate to have several very deep relationships in my life. One of these relationships is with my spouse. In our relationship, because of the level of trust (faith) we have in each other, my spouse can tell me things they are going to do—matters whose fulfillment I have not yet seen—and I confidently believe their word because of their proven character.

<!-- p29 -->
This example shows how our deep relationship with God can be experienced with trust and confidence! We may not yet have fully experienced Him (indeed, in this earthly life we will never fully experience Him), but as we mature in our relationship, we can trust Him beyond the bounds of what I personally know and what I have seen happen so far.

<!-- p30 -->
Jesus increases our faith through experience with Him. An important way we can and should experience Him is through His Word.

When we replace the word *faith* with *persuasion*, we read it like this:

<!-- p37 -->
*‘So persuasion comes from hearing, and hearing from the word of God’*. This truth is naturally self-evident. One of the primary ways someone arrives at persuasion is to have been persuaded, and we are persuaded when someone presents an ‘argument’-or a clear and well-reasoned explanation—.

<!-- p38 -->
In Scripture there are two Greek words for ‘Word’-**logos** and **rhema**. Understanding the difference between them helps us see how faith grows.

<!-- p39 -->
**Logos**: Generally refers to the **concept, idea, reason, or broader expression of the Word of God**. This term encompasses a universal and fundamental truth or message and is often used to describe the eternal wisdom of God and Christ Himself as the living Word.

<!-- p40 -->
**Rhema (G4487)**: emphasizes a **specific word or spoken utterance**. It usually refers to a particular word or message given at a specific moment, especially a spoken or revealed word that directly pertains to a person’s situation.

<!-- p41 -->
*Logos* is like the overall message of a book, whereas *rhema* is like a particular section or sentence that, at a specific moment, speaks directly to someone. While *logos* is the overarching truth of God, *rhema* is when a portion of that truth is spoken personally and practically to an individual. Together, they represent the fullness of God’s communication: *logos* as the broad, foundational truths of the character and will of God, and *rhema* as the specific, applied word that brings that truth to life in a person’s circumstances.

<!-- p42 -->
### An example of a Rhema word in action

<!-- p43 -->
God promises revelation and wisdom to all who ask. When He speaks to us, that utterance is a *rhema* word. Rhema words are from God and serve to confirm, guide, and direct us. They do not replace the *logos* and do not have biblical authority. *Rhema* words should be measured by the same standard by which prophecy or the interpretation of tongues is measured (for a deeper study on this subject, see the chapter on the Holy Spirit).

<!-- p44 -->
At several points in my career path, I have prayed for divine guidance. A few years ago, I was thinking about beginning a search for a fresh opportunity, and during a time of prayer I heard God say to me: ‘*Just wait for Me; I have an opportunity for you.*’ I had started looking for a new job because I was dissatisfied with my current role and felt discouraged. God’s guiding word, ‘*Wait for Me*,’ helped me have peace and assurance in a season that otherwise would have been restless.

<!-- p45 -->
Three or four months passed; I continued to work hard and faithfully and kept my attitude positive. God reminded me: ‘Wait for Me,’ then His promise was fulfilled. I was given an opportunity for a promotion and received a significant pay increase.

<!-- p46 -->
I could have continued my job search; I could have left. I could have started somewhere new. No doubt someone else would have gotten that promotion! But because I sought the Lord’s counsel and was satisfied with His **rhema word**, I obeyed and found the benefit. God’s words are **living and personal**, and He promises to give us wisdom abundantly and generously (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:5</span>).

<!-- p47 -->
There have been dozens, if not hundreds, of times that I have prayed and asked God what to do, and He has given me a rhema word. Not each one had a result as dramatic, but all of them brought a sense of peace and assurance that I was obeying God and that He would sort things out. They were words I needed at that very moment! Often when God brings a rhema word, it is from the logos. He brings a portion of Scripture to my mind: “*Do not fear, for I am with you!*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="41" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 41:10</span>), “*By His wounds we are healed*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="53" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 53:5</span>), “*Do not grow weary in doing good!*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:9</span>), and the many examples go on!

<!-- p48 -->
Receiving a rhema word not only strengthens our faith, but also deepens our relationship with God, because we trust Him more fully in our daily decisions. We will continue our study on learning to hear God’s voice in the chapter on the Holy Spirit.

<!-- p49 -->
Our faith is also strengthened by what we see God doing, but sometimes what we see in the natural world challenges what we hear from God. Reconciling the dissonance between what we see and what we hear is an important ministry of the Holy Spirit, who has promised to guide us into all truth (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="16" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 16:13</span>).

Thomas believed in Jesus and trusted his fellow disciples, but he did not believe that Jesus had risen bodily; he insisted he could not actively believe until he personally experienced it.

When Jesus appeared to His disciples and calmed them (I imagine that when someone appears among you through locked doors, it’s truly terrifying), He spoke to Thomas. He did not rebuke or scold Thomas, nor did He call him part of an evil and crooked generation seeking a sign (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="16" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 16:4</span>) but rather revealed Himself to Thomas.

<!-- p60 -->
Jesus goes on to say: ‘*Blessed are those who have not seen and have believed*’d. This statement is not Jesus encouraging ‘blind or uninformed faith’ or rejecting the use of empirical evidence. Rather, He is saying that we are more blessed when we can believe without needing empirical proof. Why? If our faith, our trust, were limited solely to what we have empirically seen, we would never be able to have confidence regarding future work. We would have great trust concerning what has been done, but with regard to our tomorrows and our future we would not walk in the peace of God.

<!-- p61 -->
With a relational example, how challenging would my relationship with my wife become if my attitude were always ‘<u>I’ll believe it when I see it!</u>’? Instead, a trust-based relationship says, ‘I know you are a trustworthy person.’
