# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
We often think of repentance in Christianity as something foundational only for the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The essence of repentance is much deeper than turning from sin toward salvation. Repentance from dead works means living with a heart that has turned away from the things that harm us or stop our growth—and turned toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews places ‘repentance from dead works’ as the first item in the list of foundational teachings after the elementary principles of the Messiah (Chapter 4).

### Dead works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever been employed knows what work is. Work is a set of tasks you do in order to get paid and earn a livelihood (employment). Employment is an agreement to exchange your talents and efforts for something of value that helps sustain your life. You work because you get something out of it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can bring a sense of purpose, and often a sense of identity. It is very common to be asked, ‘What do you do?’ and the answer begins with an identity statement, ‘I am.’ While work can be enjoyable and purposeful, it is also likely to be physically or mentally hard, difficult, challenging, and stressful. If your job is none of these, you may find it too mundane and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you have a job with 40 hours a week and sleep 8 hours every night, you spend 36% of your waking hours working, and this does not include the time spent getting ready for work, commuting to work, tending to the house, and other necessary tasks required to keep your life running.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we work so much? It varies, but by definition, because we gain something valuable from it.

<!-- p13 -->
If the pay were stopped, would you continue working at that job? For most of us the answer is an easy and painfully obvious one: a resounding “No”!

<!-- p14 -->
If a job were killing you, would you keep working? I suppose it depends on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
The sense of ***nekros*** in connection with ***erga*** is an effort or work that is dead, lacks vitality, and cannot reproduce! The author of Hebrews warns us against effort that is dead.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, various criteria are used to determine whether an organism is alive (this is a partial list used for our purpose):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living organisms convert energy through metabolic processes in order to grow, develop, and maintain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: Life involves growth and progression over time, with a defined life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living organisms have the ability to reproduce and bring forth offspring or new organisms.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light، heat، or sound) and can adapt to environmental changes.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain their internal stability، such as regulating temperature or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time، living organisms can adapt to their environment through genetic changes and be able to survive in diverse conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this criterion of ‘life’ to our deeds with a bit of creative tweaking، then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  do not allow growth or personal development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  do not reproduce، cannot bring about a positive or desirable result.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  do not respond to external stimuli، the light of God’s presence or His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  do not maintain inner stability، become dysregulated، lack self-control.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  do not adapt (are overly rigid and inflexible) and no longer help our survival.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s will for us. God wants to give us life!

Whereas «dead» versus «alive» is a binary state. «Dying» is a spectrum. Our dead works may not yet be completely dead in every single category، but they will be! Dead works are like poisons، some have greater effects.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body، because it prevents its normal functioning. Some poisons have minor effects، like a headache. Others affect the way we think and see، or even whether we can have children (live fruitfully). Dead works are exactly like that. They may be just a headache، or they may cause death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are anything that، upon review and reflection، do not bring the abundant life that Jesus promised. Having a relationship outside of marriage is a very strong toxin، and will most likely very soon have a fully negative effect. Being stubborn and resentful without cause gradually poisons a person until they have driven everyone away.

<!-- p36 -->
To be freed from dead works، we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
‘Repentance from dead works’ means turning to God and setting aside anything that harms our walk with Him-it begins with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Relying on the metaphor of poison for dead works، if you found out that your favorite food was full of toxic heavy metals that، if consumed، would give you a deadly disease، what would you do? Without any hesitation we would set it aside and never enjoy it again. Because we realize that consuming it means our premature and probably painful death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must turn away from the things of this life that poison us and draw us toward our natural and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to take a deeper look at the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The term used in Hebrews is *repentance*. This is in contrast with the term *to repent,* an example of which can be found in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and said: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
Repenting (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or an event; that is, to change one’s mind or intention; to turn away from sin and turn to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is the state of having repented; that is, a transformation of mind; a condition in which you have turned away from sin and turned toward God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews 'repentance from dead works,' He is saying: be in a state of having repented! We must live in a continual state of 'turning away' from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times do we need to repent? A trick question! We must continually change our minds so that we no longer do dead works. Repenting **does not mean asking for forgiveness or apologizing**, though it may involve that as well. Repentance means turning away from the former way of thinking.

### Systems of change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in changing our mind. If we do not perceive the need to change our mind, then why should we even open our eyes to look for change? Again, let’s return to the poison analogy, if you cannot understand that the poison is killing you, why would you stop consuming it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some believe that a person must hit ‘rock bottom’; then they look at their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that we humans are more complex. In the study of psychology there are many theories related to behavior change; one of the most prominent is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change**, developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People may pass through a six-stage cycle for change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: there is no intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: there is an intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: some steps toward change have been taken.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: behavior has changed for a short time.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: behavior has changed and continues to be sustained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: there is no desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no fixed duration for any of the stages. Some people never meaningfully progress through the stages regarding particular issues in their lives. In other cases, we can move very quickly from stage 1 to 6.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotion Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father who has a sudden realization after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *firm* but *kind* parent. He considers raising his voice necessary. He is not thinking about change. He might even justify it- “My father raised me the same way.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, he loses his temper and sharply yells at his young daughter, louder and angrier than ever. She is startled. Her lip quivers. She quietly walks away. In that moment… something **changes**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. The image of her hurt face plays in his mind over and over. His stomach churns. He says to himself, *“This is not the person I want to be.”* He knows he needs to do something different-*right now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes into her room, kneels down, sincerely apologizes, and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You didn’t deserve that.”* He hugs her. To reinforce the change he finds motivation, prays, and talks with a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he begins reading parenting books to work on cultivating positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He isn’t perfect, but something really has changed. He no longer justifies the previous behavior- and has no desire to go back.

### Helping Others to Change

<!-- p79 -->
A large part of our discussion has focused on the act and necessity of repentance from the perspective of the person who needs to change. However, we will encounter people who are in great need of change but are stuck in **Precontemplation**/**Contemplation** - blind to their need to change. We will also likely meet those who are eager to change but have no idea what to do (**Preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
Jesus, when preaching to the crowds, often encountered people who did not know they needed to change. We cannot forcefully change someone’s mind or repent in their place. This is not within our domain, and Jesus’ example was not like that either.

<!-- p81 -->

### Godly Sorrow and the Goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although hitting rock bottom is more myth than a definitive prerequisite for change, God can use sorrow to influence our minds. God uses sorrow to bring us to repentance. He convicts us regarding our actions and the state of our lives and hearts to open our eyes so that we see our total and absolute need for Himself.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>the experience of grief or despair that leads to a *metanoia*</u>, is not the same thing as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of grief or despair, but the world offers no hope, only relief and the promise of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and grief.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the endurance of pain - physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual - especially when it is prolonged or severe.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is a multifaceted emotional response to a significant personal loss, especially relational or existential.

<!-- p100 -->
Sorrow, pain, and grief are concepts close to one another. In our human condition we will experience all three; we can learn from all three and grow, but God does not bring death and destruction into our lives in order to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
The Bible says every good and perfect gift is from above, from God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow causes distress, but His discipline is just and loving. Just as a good father would not deliberately break his child's hand to 'teach a lesson,' God does not inflict disease or sickness on His children to instruct us.

<!-- p102 -->
### An example of worldly sorrow versus godly sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick and deliberately hit her, which made her cry and left a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
Their father, hearing the commotion, investigated, and when he learned what his son had done, he took the stick, broke it in two, and threw it away. He sent his son inside to think about what he had done and comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came inside to deal with his son, he saw that he was crying. When he asked why he was crying, the boy said, “Because you broke my stick.”

<!-- p106 -->
The father replied, “I am sad that you are more concerned about your stick than about your sister whom you hurt,” and sent him back to his room for further reflection.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later his son came out and apologized. He said he was still sad, both because his father had broken his stick and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example, the boy experienced both worldly sorrow for his lost stick and, ultimately, godly sorrow for his sister who had been hurt.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and the goodness of God are interconnected. God is a good Father who allows us to experience the consequences of our actions, yet still stands with us as we go through those consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
The relentless goodness of God—namely, that if we come to Him, He is willing to redeem us and restore us—is a positive factor in our path of repentance. His goodness is not that we continue in sin, but that despite our sin He opens a way for us to be redeemed and to experience His salvation.

<!-- p112 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

> Or do you despise the riches of His goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?

</span>

<!-- p113 -->
Despite God's goodness, mercy, and grace, some do not repent and are not changed; they become trapped in a cycle of failure and pain. Conversely, for some, simply seeing an instance of sin and its consequence in another person's life is enough to change their thinking.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a vast capacity for change; it is woven into the depths of our nature. Humans are highly adaptable; we can learn and grow and find better ways to do things. However, the capacity to change is not the same as the willingness or persuasion to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some get stuck while others are so ready to change, but I can say that the entire process of change is, in fact, by the grace of God.

Repentance can be an entirely natural process, but repentance is also the work of the Spirit who calls us, opens our eyes to the truth, changes our hearts, and invites us to be transformed. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:1</span> reminds us that some are ‘caught’ in a trespass (sin) and need help to be set free! That help comes in the form of mature men and women who, in a spirit of gentleness, restore us.

The Holy Spirit is the greatest agent of change a person can engage with, and this is supported by both Scripture and research. The research of “Lyons et al. (2010) found that up to 82% of clients who experienced a spiritual awakening during substance abuse treatment and recovery were completely abstinent at a one-year follow-up, compared to 55% of clients who had not experienced a spiritual awakening.” \[2\] While people can initiate and mediate change, when we walk with the Spirit of God, we can accomplish far more.

### David’s example of repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no narrative in Scripture offers a richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin in 2 Samuel 11-12. The account begins when King David, instead of going to war, stays in Jerusalem and sees Bathsheba bathing. Although he knew that she was the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David sent for her and slept with her, which resulted in Bathsheba becoming pregnant.

<!-- p125 -->
To cover up his actions, David summoned Uriah from the battlefield, hoping that he would go to Bathsheba and appear to be the child’s father. But out of loyalty to his comrades, Uriah refused to go to his home to be with Bathsheba. Then David arranged for Uriah to be sent to the front lines, where he was ultimately killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David hastily married Bathsheba to conceal the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was displeased with David’s actions and sent his prophet Nathan to confront him. When the prophet addressed him, David immediately confessed, “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify his actions or make excuses. This confession was the first step in his repentance; a direct acknowledgment of his wrongdoing before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I acknowledge my transgressions, and my sin is always before me,*” show a full acceptance of responsibility for his actions. He grieves not only over the sin itself but also over how he had grieved God, saying, “*Against You, You only, have I sinned.*” David displayed a “*broken heart and a contrite spirit,*” which is an essential part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he deeply mourned the sin he had committed and the harm it had caused. This depth of remorse showed that he sought not only forgiveness but a transformed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked of God: “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He was seeking an inner transformation that would prevent the repetition of the same mistakes. Rather than merely apologizing, David asked God to purify him, saying, “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He recognized that only God could cleanse his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s longing for spiritual renewal shows a genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways from then on. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) reflects his hope to be aligned with God in every part of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God put away David’s sin, the Lord struck the child and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God to spare the child, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we encounter a personal prayer of repentance and a public acknowledgment of his transgressions. David’s decision to write a petition for mercy, which produced one of the most enduring psalms in Scripture, shows his willingness to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony of repentance and restoration for others to learn from and to memorialize his journey from sin to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not merely about feeling sorry; it was a profound change in his heart and life. Through the confession, acceptance, and heart-level transformation recorded in Psalm 51, David left his sin as a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most significant examples of true repentance in Scripture.
