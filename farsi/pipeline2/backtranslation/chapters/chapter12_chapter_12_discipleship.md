# Chapter 12 - Discipleship

<!-- p1 -->
Discipleship means following Jesus, learning from Him, and becoming more like Him in the way we live and love. It involves a lifelong journey of obedience, transformation, and service, led by the Holy Spirit and rooted in the Word of God.

<!-- p2 -->
Discipleship is the primary command in the Great Commission. Jesus defined this mission as follows:

<!-- p3 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p4 -->
> **<u>Matthew 28:18-20</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And Jesus came to them and spoke to them: “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me.
>
> Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,
>
> and teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you; and behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age.” Amen.
>

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

<!-- p6 -->
We, His followers, must “go and make disciples of all nations.” Discipleship is both about personal growth and about multiplying that growth by teaching others to follow Christ. Discipleship is the means by which we build the kingdom of God; the teachings of Jesus are the message.

<!-- p7 -->
The call to discipleship goes beyond merely attending church or learning Bible verses—this is an invitation to live like Jesus and to partner with Him in advancing the kingdom of God. True discipleship helps believers become spiritually mature and effective for ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:13</span>). Discipleship shapes both the heart and the skills needed to serve others and share the gospel. As Paul taught Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, discipleship follows a pattern of spiritual multiplication in which trained disciples continually raise up new followers to lead others to Christ.

<!-- p8 -->
Discipleship is both an individual calling and a global mission. On a personal level, it involves growing in faith, cultivating an active spiritual life, and being in relationship with others and with God. At the same time, it fuels the church’s mission to bring the gospel to every people and nation, ensuring that Christ’s message spreads across generations and transforms lives.

<!-- p9 -->
## Why Discipleship Matters

<!-- p10 -->
The impact of discipleship goes beyond personal transformation—it has a kingdom effect. Paul, in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, reminded Timothy to entrust what he had learned to faithful men so that they would also teach others. This generational pattern shows that discipleship is the driving force behind the growth and endurance of the church. Discipleship matters because it was Jesus’ chosen method for spreading the gospel and building His church.

<!-- p11 -->
Rather than focusing on crowds, Jesus intentionally invested in a small group of followers; He taught them, modeled faithfulness for them, and prepared them to carry forward His mission. This personal, relational approach ensured that His message would be multiplied, because each disciple was equipped to make more disciples.

<!-- p12 -->
At its core, discipleship is central to fulfilling the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). Jesus did not call us only to make converts, but to make disciples—followers who are rooted in the Word of God, empowered by the Holy Spirit, and equipped for service. Through discipleship, believers grow in their knowledge of the Scriptures, cultivate spiritual disciplines, and learn to walk in obedience to Christ. Discipleship prepares the church to impact the world, advance the gospel, and reflect the glory of God to all peoples.

<!-- p13 -->
### Nature versus Nurture

<!-- p14 -->
In the fields of biology, psychology, and anthropology, we often examine the influence of nature versus nurture. The common question is: Is a human being more the result of intrinsic, inborn genetic heritage, or more the product of environment and upbringing? The truth is that every human being is a blend of nature and nurture. Both elements, in powerful interaction, direct the process by which the human soul is formed.

<!-- p15 -->
When you received Jesus, the miracle of salvation caused you to be born again from imperishable seed. You received a divine nature! Your spiritual DNA was rewritten! This divine nature awakens in our hearts a longing to be close to our heavenly Father, to know Him, and to experience Him.

<!-- p16 -->
Just as an infant with a marvelous, beautiful, innocent nature must be shaped by loving parents to become a healthy, mature individual, we too must be formed and nurtured in order to grow up.

<!-- p17 -->
Discipleship is the process of spiritual growth that builds on our divine nature and brings us to spiritual maturity. By investing in discipleship, we ensure that the faith is passed on, leaders are developed, and the church—both locally and globally—remains healthy and vibrant.

<!-- p18 -->
### The Classroom Model versus the Apprenticeship Model

<!-- p19 -->
In the modern Western educational system, the primary relationship a student has is with content. A student’s learning centers on content, materials, books, videos, assignments, and so on. Educational transformation takes place through the curriculum. This mode of instruction is called the classroom model.

<!-- p20 -->
The paradigm of the didactic model has not always been the norm. The model and method used in the time of Jesus was the *discipleship pattern.* This learning model emphasizes that knowledge is more ‘caught than taught,’ and its focus is more on the relationship between the student and the teacher than on the relationship between the student and the curriculum.

<!-- p21 -->
The didactic model gradually became the default method over roughly a millennium, from the first universities of medieval Europe in the 11<sup>th</sup> - 13<sup>th</sup> centuries, the invention of the printing press in the 15<sup>th</sup> century, the Enlightenment of the 17<sup>th</sup> &amp; 18<sup>th</sup> centuries, and the impact of the Industrial Revolution on information and education in the 19<sup>th</sup> &amp; 20<sup>th</sup> centuries.

<!-- p22 -->
The advantage of the didactic model is that it can be more scalable than the discipleship pattern. Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) can have thousands of students at once, whereas even a highly skilled master may only be able to have several dozen disciples at a time.

<!-- p23 -->
The result of the didactic model is that the student masters the content, but the student does not become like the teacher. The teacher is the facilitator of the curriculum. The teacher transmits knowledge.

<!-- p24 -->
In the discipleship pattern, the master shares more than knowledge; he imparts wisdom. The result of the discipleship pattern is that the disciple has become so like the master that he can reproduce the master’s work and take on disciples himself.

<!-- p25 -->
The didactic model has its use and place in the Kingdom of God; a good example is this very book or any other Christian instructional material. Jesus, as a rabbi, skilled and trained in the rabbinic method, employed the discipleship pattern as the primary model for his disciples.

<!-- p26 -->
Jesus describes the discipleship pattern as follows:

<!-- p27 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="40" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p28 -->
> **<u>Luke 6:40</u>** (NKJV)
>
> A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone who is perfectly trained will be like his teacher.
>

<!-- p29 -->
</span>

<!-- p30 -->
In the didactic model the student becomes a bearer of knowledge; in the discipleship pattern, the student becomes like the teacher. Let’s examine the key terms of that section of Luke.

<!-- p31 -->
[See Table 6](../tables/table6.md)

<!-- p32 -->
In summary, the purpose and aim of the discipleship pattern used in disciple-making have two aspects:

<!-- p33 -->
**First, the disciple is fully trained**, like the teacher. This does not mean that the disciple imitates everything his teacher does. We are not commanded to make clones, but to bring men and women to maturity who can carry the message of Jesus to the ends of the earth.

<!-- p34 -->
**Second, the disciple now becomes a teacher for others**. The disciple must pass on what he has learned to another; otherwise the message of the Kingdom stops with him. This does not mean that he will necessarily have exactly the same gifts as his teacher, or that he will enter full-time ministry, but it does mean that he must multiply in order for his own training to be complete.

<!-- p35 -->
Every living thing that does not reproduce is sterile and its lineage is cut off. An animal that cannot reproduce is not healthy; likewise, a church that cannot reproduce is unhealthy.

## Jesus’ Pattern and Process of Disciple-Making

<!-- p37 -->
Jesus in the Gospels displayed with his chosen disciples the mature and complete example of disciple-making. We should generally use these steps as a foundation for our own path in becoming disciples and making disciples.

<!-- p38 -->
The discipleship pattern has two roles: master and disciple. Every master was once a disciple, and we hope every disciple will one day become a master. I will use the terms *disciple* to refer to the student and *disciple-maker* to refer to the master/teacher.

<!-- p39 -->
Every disciple-making relationship passes through an eight-stage progression. These stages, or steps, are not completely distinct; often the relationship flows between stages. We will examine the characteristics of disciple-makers in detail later in this chapter.

<!-- p40 -->
The eight stages of discipleship are:

<!-- p41 -->
1.  Invitation/Calling

<!-- p42 -->
2.  Teaching

<!-- p43 -->
3.  Modeling

<!-- p44 -->
4.  Participation/Testing

<!-- p45 -->
5.  Correction

<!-- p46 -->
6.  Empowerment/Delegation of Authority

<!-- p47 -->
7.  Multiplication

<!-- p48 -->
8.  Staying Connected

<!-- p49 -->
### Invitation/Calling

<!-- p50 -->
Discipleship begins with a personal invitation from Jesus to follow Him. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span>, Jesus called His first disciples and said, *«Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.»* This invitation was both relational and transformative—requiring the abandonment of old ways of life and stepping into a new purpose centered on God’s mission. Jesus’ invitation was not to be spectators, but to participate in His ministry and the work of His kingdom.

<!-- p51 -->
Today’s disciple-makers should invite others into authentic relationships that spark spiritual growth and obedience. The call to discipleship is deeply personal. Jesus meets people where they are and addresses their unique needs and circumstances, but He also calls them to go deeper—to grow, serve, and become more like Him. This pattern reflects the relational nature of discipleship, where the focus is not merely on knowing, but on a transforming relationship with Christ.

<!-- p52 -->
### Teaching

<!-- p53 -->
Teaching is at the heart of discipleship. Jesus devoted much of His ministry to teaching the principles of God’s kingdom, especially in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7). His teachings provided a foundation for living righteously, loving others, and walking in obedience to God. Instruction in God’s Word remains central to discipleship today, as Scripture is the primary instrument for spiritual growth, correction, and training in righteousness (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16–17</span>).

<!-- p54 -->
Disciple-makers should commit themselves to faithful teaching of Scripture and help others understand it and apply it to their lives. Teaching goes beyond the transfer of knowledge—it requires modeling a Christlike life so that disciples see faith in action. Jesus embodied the truths He taught, demonstrating that effective teaching is both spoken and seen. Likewise, disciple-makers should strive to live what they teach and provide practical examples of obedience and faithfulness.

<!-- p55 -->
### Modeling

<!-- p56 -->
Jesus did not only teach His disciples—He showed them how to apply the truths He proclaimed in life. Jesus practically demonstrated what it means to love, to serve, and to minister. Some examples from the Gospels include Jesus washing the disciples’ feet as an act of humility and service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="13" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 13:12–15</span>), healing the sick, and feeding the hungry. Through His actions He displayed compassion, faith, and boldness, leaving a clear pattern for His followers to imitate.

<!-- p57 -->
Discipleship requires showing, not just telling. Today’s disciple-makers should be transparent about their faith journey, including struggles and victories, so that others can see how faith works in real life. Whether it is praying with someone in need, sharing testimonies, or serving the community, discipleship flourishes when it is rooted in practical modeling. Actions often speak louder than words, making modeling a powerful tool for teaching others in the faith.

<!-- p58 -->
### Participation/Testing

<!-- p59 -->
Jesus did not keep His disciples on the sidelines—He actively involved them in ministry experiences. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 9:1–6</span> and <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 10:5–15</span>, Jesus sent the disciples to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out unclean spirits. These hands-on opportunities allowed the disciples to learn by doing and, under His guidance, to gain confidence and skill.

<!-- p60 -->
Contemporary discipleship should also include practical participation. Disciple-makers should invite disciples to participate in service, whether through leading prayer groups, sharing testimonies, or serving at evangelistic events. Providing safe spaces for practice allows disciples to grow in boldness while being supported and encouraged. Learning yields the best results when it is put into practice, and participation is the key to turning theory into action.

<!-- p61 -->
### Correction

<!-- p62 -->
Growth often requires correction, and Jesus lovingly corrected his disciples when they misunderstood his teachings or failed in their tasks. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-37" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Mark 9:33-37</span>, he addressed their pride by teaching about servant leadership. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="22" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="31-32" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 22:31-32</span>, he prayed for Peter to prepare him to rise from failure and strengthen others.

<!-- p63 -->
Correction in discipleship should be full of grace and constructive-rooted in Scripture and aimed at restoration (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 4:2</span>). It is not about condemnation, but about helping disciples grow and avoid stumbling blocks. Disciple-makers should provide accountability, use the Word of God to guide and encourage growth, and themselves be models of humility in receiving correction.

<!-- p64 -->
### Empowerment/Delegation

<!-- p65 -->
Jesus not only taught and corrected- he empowered his disciples to carry out his mission. He gave them authority to proclaim the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:18-20</span>). After his resurrection, he promised the Holy Spirit as the source of power for their ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>).

<!-- p66 -->
Contemporary discipleship should include empowering others to operate in their spiritual gifts and serve effectively. This includes identifying gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:11-13</span>), encouraging their use, and releasing disciples to serve. Empowerment also emphasizes reliance on the Holy Spirit for strength, boldness, and wisdom, to ensure that disciples are spiritually equipped for the work God has called them to.

<!-- p67 -->
### Multiplication

<!-- p68 -->
The goal of discipleship is not merely personal growth-it is multiplication. Jesus modeled this by teaching disciples to make more disciples (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). After his resurrection, the apostles continued this multiplying mission, spreading the gospel and planting churches throughout the known world. Paul’s instruction in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> reinforces this principle, urging Timothy to teach those who will also teach others.

<!-- p69 -->
Discipleship involves equipping disciples to make other disciples and creates a chain reaction of faith that expands the kingdom of God. This process requires intentionality-teaching disciples how to mentor, teach, and train others, while sending them out with confidence and purpose (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 10:15</span>).

<!-- p70 -->
### Stay Connected

<!-- p71 -->
Discipleship does not end when someone is sent. Jesus modeled ongoing connection with his disciples and continued to encourage and teach them even after his resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="21" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 21:15-19</span>). The early church maintained this pattern, gathering regularly for teaching, fellowship, and prayer (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>).

<!-- p72 -->
Contemporary discipleship should also foster ongoing relationships, providing accountability, encouragement, and support as disciples continue to grow and lead. Preserving unity in the body of Christ ensures that disciples are never isolated, but strengthened and encouraged in their faith journey.

<!-- p73 -->
## Who makes disciples?

<!-- p74 -->
Everyone in the body of Christ is responsible first to be discipled and then to take part in the process of discipleship. Gifts and functions are diverse, so our roles may look different, but we are all responsible. Ultimately, the primary responsibility for the state and health of discipleship in the local church rests with the leaders (**elders and deacons - <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 14:23</span>**), but we all have a share.

<!-- p75 -->
Discipleship is a living and growing process-like nurturing a child to maturity. Healthy discipleship brings strength and life to people. Just as parents strengthen and nourish a child and thereby make him healthy and mature, disciple-makers also help to nourish, strengthen, and bring disciples to maturity. The ultimate goal of discipleship is not the 'transfer of knowledge,' but transformation.

<!-- p76 -->
We must all become complete (mature) and make disciples. Although our gifts will vary, there are shared characteristics for all disciple-makers.

<!-- p77 -->
### Characteristics of a disciple-maker

<!-- p78 -->
A disciple-maker is someone who not only follows Christ, but is committed to helping others grow in their faith. This role requires specific characteristics that reflect both spiritual maturity and practical commitment. Disciple-makers must continually rely on God’s strength, wisdom, and grace to overcome the challenges that come with leading others in their spiritual journeys.

<!-- p79 -->
#### Rooted in Christ

<!-- p80 -->
The foundation of a disciple-maker’s life must be deeply rooted in Christ. Jesus said, *«I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; for apart from me you can do nothing»* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="15" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 15:5</span>). Effective disciple-makers know that the ability to grow and to help others springs from staying close to Jesus each day. However, resisting personal growth and spiritual change can disrupt this dependence. Disciple-makers must continually cultivate their personal relationship with Jesus through prayer, study of the Word, and obedience, and be examples to those they disciple of what it means to abide in Christ.

<!-- p81 -->
#### Committed to the Mission

<!-- p82 -->
A disciple-maker must remain committed to the mission of discipling as Jesus commanded in the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). This mission requires intentional focus and prioritization, with the recognition that discipling is not optional but the central purpose of the church. However, challenges such as lack of time, busyness, and personal distractions can make focus difficult. Disciple-makers should intentionally set aside time for mentoring, teaching, and building relationships, and regard discipling not as an occasional activity, but as a lifestyle (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 19:10</span>).

<!-- p83 -->
#### Led by the Spirit

<!-- p84 -->
Discipling cannot be done by human strength alone؛ it requires the guidance and empowerment of the Holy Spirit. Jesus promised his disciples that when the Holy Spirit came upon them, they would receive power (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>)، and be equipped to witness and serve the gospel. Disciple-makers should be sensitive to the Spirit’s guidance and rely on his wisdom to navigate complex situations, cultural barriers, and difficult conversations. This dependence on the Holy Spirit empowers disciple-makers to act in love, boldness, and spiritual discernment, ensuring that their efforts are Spirit-centered, not self-centered.

<!-- p85 -->
#### Humble and Teachable

<!-- p86 -->
Humility is a distinguishing mark of an effective disciple-maker. As Paul taught, *«Do nothing out of ambition or conceit؛ rather, with humility, consider others better than yourselves»* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 2:3</span>). Disciple-makers realize that they themselves are lifelong learners who must remain teachable and accountable. They receive correction and feedback and become models of humility for those they mentor. Pride can be a significant obstacle to growth, both for disciple-makers and for their disciples. Embracing accountability and maintaining a servant’s heart fosters trust and creates an environment for growth and transparency.

<!-- p87 -->
#### Patient and Persistent

<!-- p88 -->
Discipleship is rarely instant-this is a long-term process that requires patience and perseverance. Paul reminds believers that *«Let us not grow weary of doing good، for if we do not give up, we will reap at the proper time»* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:9</span>). Growth in faith often includes setbacks, questions, and failures, but disciple-makers are called to walk alongside others, offering encouragement and support at every step. When progress seems slow, it is easy to become discouraged؛ but disciple-makers trust that God is at work, even when results are not immediately visible.

<!-- p89 -->
Being a disciple-maker requires a combination of spiritual dependence, commitment, and humility. It is a calling that demands patience and perseverance, while also requiring remaining under the Spirit’s guidance and being teachable. Disciple-makers are not expected to be perfect, but they must be faithful-to abide in Christ, invest in others, and trust that God, in his own time, brings about growth and transformation. These qualities reflect the love of Christ and help others become mature disciples who, in turn, can make disciples.

## Practical Tools for Discipleship

<!-- p91 -->
Effective discipleship requires intentional tools and practices that strengthen spiritual growth, accountability, and community. These tools help disciples build a firm foundation in the faith, grow in intimacy with God, and become equipped to disciple others. Practical methods such as Bible study, prayer, small groups, and mentoring provide both the structure and the support needed for ongoing growth.

<!-- p92 -->
### Bible Study

<!-- p93 -->
Deep engagement with God’s Word is the foundation of discipleship. Jesus emphasized the importance of God’s Word in spiritual growth, declaring that *«Man shall not live by bread alone، but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God»* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:4</span>). Regular study of Scripture and memorization of verses enable disciples to internalize truth, confront falsehood, and apply God’s wisdom in daily life.

<!-- p94 -->
Disciples should be encouraged to immerse themselves in the Word, not merely as an academic exercise, but to know God and see their hearts transformed (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="119" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="105" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 119:105</span>). Practical approaches, such as reading plans, study guides, and group discussions, provide opportunities to explore the teachings and discuss application in community. By building the habit of regular study, disciples lay a firm foundation for spiritual maturity and fruitful ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p95 -->
### Prayer

<!-- p96 -->
Prayer is the lifeline of discipleship that connects believers to God’s presence, power, and guidance. Jesus taught his disciples how to pray in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 11:1-4</span> and emphasized both reliance on God and spiritual alignment with his will. Through prayer, disciples learn to seek God’s wisdom, intercede for others, and receive strength for service.

<!-- p97 -->
Learning how to pray—for both individuals and groups—equips disciples to cultivate intimacy with God and discern his guidance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 4:6-7</span>). Incorporating prayer journals, prayer groups, and fasting practices cultivates spiritual discipline and empowerment and ensures that disciples remain Spirit-led and focused on the mission.

<!-- p98 -->
### Small Groups and Community

<!-- p99 -->
Discipleship flourishes in the context of community. The early church modeled relational discipleship through fellowship, prayer, and breaking bread together (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>). Small groups and accountability partnerships provide a safe space for disciples to grow together, share struggles, and celebrate victories.

<!-- p100 -->
The local church functions as the primary hub of disciple-making relationships and provides opportunities for group Bible studies, prayer meetings, and fellowship events. These gatherings enable disciples to practice love, bear one another’s burdens (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:2</span>), and encourage one another toward spiritual maturity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="24-25" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:24-25</span>). Small groups also provide a framework for multiplying leaders, as mature disciples teach others and send them to lead groups and mentor new believers.

<!-- p101 -->
### Mentoring

<!-- p102 -->
Mentoring provides a personal and intentional approach to disciple-making. Jesus modeled this by investing deeply in the twelve disciples and walking with them through teaching, correction, and encouragement. Likewise, one-on-one relationships allow disciple-makers to tailor guidance to individual needs and help disciples grow in faith, character, and leadership.

<!-- p103 -->
Mentors serve as accountability partners and provide support, prayer, and wisdom in times of growth and challenge. Practical tools such as spiritual assessments, goal-setting, and regular follow-ups ensure that disciples remain focused and motivated. Peer relationships within mentoring structures also cultivate mutual growth, as both mentor and disciple learn from shared experiences (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PRO" data-chapter="27" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Proverbs 27:17</span>).

## **Personal Reflection Questions**

<!-- p105 -->
1.  What does being a disciple of Jesus personally mean to you? How do you actively follow him in your daily life?

<!-- p106 -->
2.  Reflect on Jesus’ model of disciple-making. Which aspect of his approach—inviting, teaching, showing, or empowering—stands out most to you, and why?

<!-- p107 -->
3.  How can you multiply your faith by discipling others? What challenges or fears might hold you back, and how can you overcome them? (Notice I said multiply your faith, not your own disciples!)

<!-- p108 -->
4.  What characteristics of a disciple-maker (e.g., patience, humility, relational focus) do you see in yourself? Which qualities do you feel you need to cultivate more?

<!-- p109 -->
5.  Think about the people in your life. Whom might God be calling you to disciple? How can you take the first step in building that relationship?

## Group Discussion Questions (20-30 minutes)

<!-- p111 -->
1.  Icebreaker question: Who has had the greatest impact on your spiritual growth, and what did they do that impacted you?

<!-- p112 -->
2.  Examining Jesus’ model: Read <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span>. How does Jesus’ invitation to ‘Follow me’ summarize the essence of discipleship? What does this look like today?

<!-- p113 -->
3.  The call to disciple-making: Discuss <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>. Why is disciple-making at the heart of the church’s mission? How can we, as individuals, participate in this mission?

<!-- p114 -->

<!-- p115 -->
