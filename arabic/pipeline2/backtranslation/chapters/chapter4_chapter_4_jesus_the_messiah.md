# Chapter 4 - Jesus-the-Messiah

<!-- p1 -->
Building on our study of sin, we concluded that sin carries a heavy price, upon those against whom sin has been committed and to whom justice must be restored, and upon those who commit sin and must provide restitution. The price of sin must be paid. In the Old Testament, God established a series of sacrifices offered by priests, individuals, and communities to address, resolve, and adjudicate sin.

<!-- p2 -->
Even with all the sacrifices for sin, they were not sufficient—not because God failed, but because people continued to choose sin. And although animal sacrifices could provide limited atonement, no amount of animal sacrifices was able to change the sinful nature of humanity.

<!-- p3 -->
God had a marvelous plan, the <u>Messiah</u> (in Hebrew ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) also known as the <u>Christ</u> (in Greek ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**) meaning “the Anointed”; a person consecrated for a special role (e.g., a king, priest, or savior).

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it helps to look at what people in his time hoped the Messiah would be. These expectations shaped their view of Jesus and were often the reason for their difficulty in recognizing him as the fulfillment of God’s plan.

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical Context

<!-- p6 -->
The Jewish people had long awaited the coming of a Messiah. Jewish beliefs about the Messiah in the period immediately preceding Jesus were shaped by Old Testament prophecies, and their interpretations were diverse and not universally agreed upon. Most Jews expected the Messiah to be a political and military leader who would deliver Israel from foreign oppression (especially Roman rule) and restore the Davidic kingdom. Here is an overview of the common messianic expectations and how they aligned (or did not align) with biblical prophecy:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish expectations of the Messiah before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A political and military deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would be a king like David, leading Israel in a victorious revolt against foreign powers and restoring Israel’s independence.

<!-- p10 -->
- **Referenced Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 9:6-7</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JER" data-chapter="23" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Jeremiah 23:5-6</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ZEC" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Zechariah 9:9-10</span>.

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical context:** Under Roman rule, the Jews were oppressed and burdened with taxes, which gave rise to hopes for a Messiah who would topple Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p12 -->
- **Reality:** Jesus is a king, but his kingdom is not earthly (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="36" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 18:36</span>). He established a spiritual kingdom at his first coming, but he will return as a conquering king to judge and rule in righteousness, as described in Revelation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 19:11-16</span>).

<!-- p13 -->
### **2. Restorer of the Davidic kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would rebuild the Davidic dynasty and usher in a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace.

<!-- p15 -->
- **Referenced Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 7:12-16</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="AMO" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Amos 9:11</span>.

<!-- p16 -->
- **Interpretation:** The focus was often on national restoration rather than spiritual renewal.

<!-- p17 -->
- **Reality:** Jesus, as a descendant of David, fulfills this promise by establishing an eternal kingdom. While this kingdom is spiritual in nature now, it will be crowned with a visible reign at his second coming (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="32-33" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:4-6</span>).

<!-- p18 -->
### **3. A prophet like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief:** Some Jews believed that the Messiah would be a prophet like Moses, speaking directly with God and on his behalf.

<!-- p20 -->
- **Referenced Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="DEU" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Deuteronomy 18:15-19</span>.

<!-- p21 -->
- **Expectation:** While many anticipated a prophetic figure, they often did not see this role as central to the Messiah but as an additional aspect.

<!-- p22 -->
- **Fact:** Jesus fulfilled this prophecy as the supreme Prophet, speaking God’s word and revealing the Father directly (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:18</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:10</span>). His prophetic role surpasses that of Moses, as he mediates a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### **4. A Leader Who Will Bring Justice**

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah will judge the wicked, uphold the righteous, and bring justice to the oppressed.

<!-- p25 -->
- **Referenced Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 11:3-5</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="72" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 72:1-4</span>.

<!-- p26 -->
- **Interpretation:** Justice was expected to be primarily political and societal rather than spiritual.

<!-- p27 -->
- **Fact:** Jesus brings ultimate justice. In his first coming, he provided spiritual redemption and justice through the cross (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Colossians 2:13-15</span>). In his second coming, he will execute judgment on the wicked and establish perfect justice forever (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>).

<!-- p28 -->
### Diverse Messianic Beliefs

<!-- p29 -->
Just as the Christian world today is divided into denominations and sects or distinct doctrinal frameworks, so too were the Jews of Jesus’ time divided. In the time of Jesus, four major Jewish sects shaped the religious and political landscape of Israel, each with distinctive beliefs and expectations regarding the Messiah. Understanding some background about these different groups can help provide meaningful context for studying the books of the New Testament.

<!-- p30 -->
The **Pharisees** were the most influential among the common people, known for their strict adherence to the Law (Torah) and the oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection, life after death, and the existence of angels, and they emphasized personal piety and obedience to God’s commandments. Their messianic expectations centered on a figure who upheld the Law, brought justice, and restored Israel’s glory both politically and religiously.

<!-- p31 -->
The **Sadducees**, by contrast, were a wealthy, politically influential group that controlled the Temple and the priesthood. They rejected the oral traditions and accepted only the Torah as an authoritative text. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, life after death, and the existence of angels, focusing instead on preserving their power and cooperating with Rome to ensure political stability. Consequently, they did not place great importance on the Messiah, as their chief concern was maintaining the status quo.

<!-- p32 -->
The **Essenes** were a separatist group that withdrew from mainstream Jewish society, living in isolated communities such as Qumran. They placed great emphasis on ritual purity, eschatological expectations, and a disciplined way of life. The Essenes believed in two Messiahs: a priestly Messiah who would restore the holiness of the Temple, and a royal Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness. This dual-Messiah concept reflected their hope for both spiritual and political renewal. The Essenes are not referred to directly in the Scriptures.

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, the **Zealots** were armed nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent rebellion. They rejected Roman authority and were adamant about restoring Israel’s sovereignty. Their messianic expectations leaned toward a king-warrior Messiah in the mold of David who would lead them in battle against their oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p34 -->
While these groups focused on earthly expectations, Jesus’ message unveiled a far greater plan-—not only addressing political oppression, but humanity’s spiritual bondage to sin.

## The Truth about the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### The Anointing of Jesus

<!-- p37 -->
The Pharisees’ and Zealots’ vision of the Messiah reflects a recurring pattern in human history-the pursuit of salvation by earthly means while rejecting God’s greater plan. As we have seen throughout the Scriptures, people often turn away from God—even though he is always faithful. And even when God is faithful and receives us, he is often forgotten and rejected, leading to cycles of disobedience and decay that have plagued earthly kingdoms.

<!-- p38 -->
While many expected a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a greater purpose for his anointing-one that meets spiritual and eternal needs. Had Jesus come as a conquering military leader, as the Jews had hoped and expected, it would have been only a matter of time before the kingdom fell into ruin because of human disobedience.

<!-- p39 -->
Instead, Jesus established an unshakable kingdom. His message was not rooted in military might or political dominance, but in a divine purpose. And as the Anointed One, Jesus explained what he was anointed for and how he would accomplish it:

<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16-21" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

Jesus was anointed - set apart by God - and sent with a divine message, full of purpose and power.

<!-- p46 -->

<!-- p47 -->

<!-- p49 -->

<!-- p50 -->

<!-- p51 -->

<!-- p54 -->

<!-- p55 -->
- Setting them free

<!-- p56 -->
- Binding up, healing, and restoration

<!-- p57 -->
The aim of Jesus’s ministry and work was both individual and global, both immediate and long-term. His purpose was not single-focused, but was part of God’s divine plan to reconcile humanity to God. After sin entered the world, it caused a division between God and man. God had to drive man out of the garden and from His presence. Throughout the Old Testament, man could be with God, but only partially. It was very rare for the Spirit of God to be given to people, and in general people lived in isolation from God. He found a people in the Jews, but He wanted a permanent, assured, and complete way to restore His relationship with man.

<!-- p58 -->
The presence of God on earth in the time of Jesus was, for the most part, confined to the holiest place in the temple, the Holy of Holies. There, behind the thick veil that separated the Most Holy Place from the Holy Place, the presence of God rested with the Ark of the Covenant. Man was isolated from God, and God was separated from His creation.

<!-- p59 -->
God sent Jesus to repair, rebuild, restore, and reconcile human beings to Himself. (2 Corinthians 5:17-21)

<!-- p60 -->
### The Essential Work of Jesus

<!-- p61 -->
In order to fully reconcile humanity with God, the mission of Christ required not only preaching and healing, but also the final work of atonement. Jesus had to become the perfect, complete sacrifice, through His death on the cross.

<!-- p62 -->
The Epistle to the Hebrews explains at length across its thirteen chapters how Jesus became the perfect sacrifice, and how He Himself was the offering for sin, and also the High Priest who presents it (Hebrews 3 & 4). Jesus’ priesthood is not from the Levitical tribe (according to the tribe of Levi), but according to the order of Melchizedek, who is said to be without beginning or end, that is, not bound by the patterns and customs of men (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 7:3</span>). The limitation of the Levitical priesthood is that it relies on the blood of bulls and goats (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:12-13</span>), whose sacrifices are not sufficient for a permanent and abiding forgiveness of sins. And from our study of sin and sacrifice we know that the consequences of the people’s sins could be postponed for one year (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:3</span>) on the Day of Atonement, but that sacrifice had to be renewed continually.

<!-- p63 -->
Jesus, by His death on the cross (accounts of which appear in Matthew 27, Mark 15, John 19, and Luke 23), shed His blood, and paid the price for past, present, and future sins for all humanity. Isaiah 53 reveals Jesus as the suffering servant, who took upon Himself the full weight of punishment and pains. His once-for-all death became the ultimate, final sacrifice. Jesus is the Lamb of God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="29" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:29</span>), without blemish or wrinkle (sin); and because He was perfect and complete and holy, the shedding of His innocent blood made atonement possible. **No more sacrifices are required or accepted. The former were only temporary-but Jesus’ sacrifice was complete and final.**

<!-- p64 -->
Through death and resurrection we have a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6</span>), a covenant sealed with the blood of the Son of God Himself; Jesus is the mediator of the covenant, the one who bridges the two parties. And through the sacrifice of Christ we receive salvation, and He became the cause of *‘eternal salvation for all who obey Him*’ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 5:9</span>).

<!-- p65 -->
The word for salvation in Greek is **Sōtēria** (*so-tay-REE-ah - **G4991)**.* It basically means **deliverance or rescue or preservation**. It includes the act of being saved, whether in the spiritual sense (eternal salvation in Christ) or in physical contexts (rescue from danger or harm). The word expresses the idea of safety, wholeness, and restoration.

<!-- p66 -->
**Salvation or deliverance:**

<!-- p67 -->
- Refers to God’s rescue of humanity from sin and its consequences through Jesus Christ.

<!-- p68 -->
- Used in contexts of eternal salvation (justification, sanctification, and glorification).

<!-- p69 -->
**Preservation:**

<!-- p70 -->
- Connotes remaining safe, protection, or being kept from danger.

<!-- p71 -->
**Wholeness or healing:**

<!-- p72 -->
- Involves restoration to a state of completeness or soundness.

<!-- p73 -->
Jesus’ sacrifice paid the price so that we may be forgiven, made a way for us to have a relationship with God, and established for us a path to remain with Him that depends only on our obedience, not on our perfection or our ability to refrain from sin.

<!-- p74 -->
When we accept Jesus, by faith (to be discussed later), we receive His salvation. This salvation connects us to God and writes our name in the ***Book of Life of the Lamb*** (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>). And the Gospel of John says:

<!-- p75 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p76 -->
> **<u>John 3:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life.
>

<!-- p77 -->
</span>

<!-- p78 -->
To perish means to be ruined—like a piece of fruit that spoils before it is enjoyed. It is a life that misses what it was made for. Jesus came to give us enduring, or eternal, life, and to protect us from perishing. Eternal life is not a life that begins after we die, but a life that begins the moment we accept Jesus as our salvation, when we are born again.

<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">
