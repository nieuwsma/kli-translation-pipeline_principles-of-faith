# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
The Bible tells us many stories about sin—its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin is to fall short of God’s standard, or «miss the mark», a term from archery that describes the archer’s failure to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old and New Testaments use multiple terms for sin, each highlighting aspects such as its type, severity, intent, victim, nature, and the punishment required. There are several general categories the Bible uses to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against people, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Intentional sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- Momentary sin, sin committed habitually

<!-- p6 -->
- Sin that warrants severe punishment such as death, sin that warrants financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In our modern criminal justice systems, we have similar distinctions: degrees of crime, a premeditated crime or a crime in the heat of passion, offenses that warrant financial restitution, or imprisonment, or even execution. An act may be both a crime and a sin, but crime is a human classification, whereas sin is a divine classification.

<!-- p8 -->
The various Greek and Hebrew terms for sin overlap to a great extent. All sins are generally described by the umbrella term «missing the mark», ***Hamartia*** in Greek, and ***Chata’*** in Hebrew. This umbrella term is used for more serious sins, such as adultery and murder, but it is also used for less severe sins, such as showing favoritism to the rich in our church meetings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## Sin Requires a Sacrifice

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God kills animals and makes garments of skin for them (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first shedding of blood to cover the consequence of sin. There are a few other similar accounts in Genesis, but God formally instituted a way to offer sacrifice for sin in Leviticus.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt Offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, or ***Olah***, was a general offering for atonement and consecration to God, often symbolizing complete surrender. This sacrifice addressed sin in its broad sense, covering both known and unknown sins. The ritual involved slaughtering a male animal—usually a bull, a sheep, a goat, or a bird—without blemish. The offerer would lay his hands on the animal’s head, symbolizing identification with the sacrifice, and then the entire offering was burned on the altar. This act, the burning of the offering in its entirety, represented total consecration to remove sin and align life with God. The burnt offering underscored the seriousness of sin and the need for complete consecration, displaying reverence and repentance through the offering of something valuable.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain Offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, known as ***Minchah***, was an offering of thanksgiving and consecration, not a direct offering for sin. It accompanied other sacrifices, such as the burnt offering and the peace offering, and was intended to express gratitude, worship, and dedication to God. It was prepared from fine flour mixed with oil and frankincense, without the shedding of blood. A portion of the offering was burned on the altar as a gift to God, while the remainder was given to the priests as food. Although not designated for sin, the grain offering was an important part of corporate worship, symbolizing the offerer’s dedication and gratitude for God’s provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace Offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, or ***Shelamim***, focused on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This sacrifice often followed sin and atonement offerings, celebrating the restored relationship with God after sin had been addressed. The offerer could bring a male or female animal without blemish, such as a cow, a sheep, or a goat. In the ritual, the fatty portions—the best parts of the animal—were burned on the altar as God’s portion, while the remaining meat was shared between the priests and the offerer in a communal feast. The peace offering was distinctive in that it symbolized fellowship and union, reflecting a sense of wholeness and unity with God. After atonement for sin had been made, this offering allowed the community to rejoice in God’s acceptance and celebrate a restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin Offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, or ***Chatat***, was specifically designated for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When an individual or the community realized they had sinned unintentionally, they could bring a sin offering to seek cleansing and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the person’s status: the priest or the community would offer a bull, a leader would offer a male goat, and an individual would offer a female goat or a lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the sacrifice before the Lord, burn the fat on the altar, and dispose of the remainder of the animal outside the camp if the sin was communal. This offering showed the seriousness of even unintentional sins and provided a means for the people to remain pure and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt Offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, or ***Asham***, addressed sins that involved breach of trust, harm to others, or desecration of sacred things. Unlike the general burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins that demanded making amends, such as property offenses or violations of sacred things. To present this offering, the offender would bring an unblemished ram to be offered. If the sin involved harm to another person or property, the wrongdoer was also required to provide restitution by returning what was taken plus 20 percent as a penalty. The guilt offering highlighted the importance of acknowledging guilt and taking concrete steps to set things right. It emphasized the relational and communal nature of sin, affirming that true atonement requires not only seeking God’s forgiveness but also repairing what was wronged for those affected by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Corporate Sin

<!-- p77 -->
Alongside these offerings specified for the sins of individuals, there were also sacrifices and offerings for the nation’s corporate sin presented daily, weekly, and monthly, but the most important was the **Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16, designed to cleanse the whole congregation of Israel from their corporate sins over the past year. This unique observance took place once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, and addressed the sins of the nation rather than individual transgressions. It served to purify the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people, symbolizing both atonement and cleansing for the entire community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the heart of the Day of Atonement were two goats used to represent both sacrifice and the removal of sin. The high priest would cast lots to determine the role of each goat. The first goat, designated “for the Lord,” was offered as a sin offering, and the high priest carried its blood into the Most Holy Place. There the blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat - the cover of the Ark of the Covenant - as a symbol of atonement for the people’s sins and their reconciliation with God. This act of sprinkling carried deep significance, as it represented the purification of the holy place and ensured that God’s presence remained with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, known as **the scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest would place his hands on the scapegoat, confessing the collective sins of Israel, thereby transferring them to the animal. Then the goat was sent into the wilderness to die alone, a symbol of the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act was a powerful illustration of atonement and liberation from sin, showing that God not only forgives but also removes the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God's forgiveness and cleansing, allowing the nation to defer the consequences of sin, and to defer judgment each year. This **atonement did not permanently forgive** the sins of the nation, but rather postponed God's judgment.

## We have all sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible focus on the issue of sin in various ways. When Moses recorded the law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament) he recorded specific penalties for specific sins/crimes. I use the word crime interchangeably here, because the books of the Law were the legal system at that time for the children of Israel.

<!-- p89 -->
In the Prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in terms of the fate of the entire nation. When the king and the people disobeyed God, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, defeated in battles, and in some cases carried off into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In the book of Proverbs, the writer writes as a loving father to his son, warning him of the personal dangers of sin, and of a lifestyle that goes against God's commandments. And in the book of Psalms, many of David's writings address the personal and emotional consequences of committing sin.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns against sin, calls people to personal repentance, and offers the ultimate sacrifice for sin. And in Romans, the apostle Paul presents a great argument for how God planned to deal with sin, and how we can be set free from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is that we are all guilty of sin, and we all must pay the price for our disobedience.

This false self-justification ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and wronged, and it ignores the reality of the holy and perfect God. The Scriptures say that if we deny that we sin, we are liars, and the truth is not in us!
