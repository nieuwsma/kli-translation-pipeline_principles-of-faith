# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
We often think in Christianity of repentance as something essential only to the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The essence of repentance is much deeper than merely turning away from sin toward salvation. Repentance from dead works means that we live with our hearts turned away from the things that harm us or hinder our growth—and turned toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of the Epistle to the Hebrews lists “repentance from dead works” as the first item in the list of foundational teachings after the elementary principles of Christ (chapter 4).

### Dead works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever been an employee knows what work is. Work is the set of tasks you do to receive a paycheck so you can make a living (employment). Employment is an agreement to exchange your talents and efforts for something of value that helps you keep going. You work because you get something in return.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can give a sense of purpose, and often a sense of identity. It is very common to be asked: ‘What do you do for a living?’ and the answer begins with an identity statement: ‘I am...’. And while work may be enjoyable and purposeful, it is also likely to be physically or mentally tiring, hard, full of challenges, and stressful. And if your job is none of these things, you may find it very boring and monotonous.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work 40 hours a week and sleep 8 hours a night, you spend 36% of your waking hours at work, and this does not include the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting to and from it, taking care of the house, and all the other necessary tasks you have to do to keep your life on track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? It varies, but by definition because we get something of value from it.

<!-- p13 -->
Would you keep working at a job if they stopped paying you? For most of us, the easy and painfully obvious answer is: a resounding no!

<!-- p14 -->
Would you keep working at a job if it were killing you? I suppose it depends on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
The context of pairing ***nekros*** with ***erga*** is effort or work that is dead, lifeless, unable to reproduce! The author of Hebrews warns us against effort that is dead.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, there are several criteria used to determine whether an organism is alive (and this is a partial list for our purposes):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living organisms transform energy through metabolic processes in order to grow, develop, and maintain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and development**: Life involves growth and progress over time, with a defined life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living organisms have the ability to reproduce, forming offspring or new entities.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adapt to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as regulating temperature or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living organisms can adapt to their environments through genetic changes, allowing survival in different conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this criterion for ‘life’ to our works with a bit of creative flexibility, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow growth or personal development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, and cannot produce a positive or desirable outcome.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God’s presence or to His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Do not maintain internal stability, become disordered, and lack self-control.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (too rigid) and no longer help us to survive.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s desire for us. God wants to give us life!

<!-- p30 -->

> The thief does not come except to steal, and to kill, and to destroy. I have come that they may have life, and that they may have it more abundantly.

<!-- p32 -->
</span>

<!-- p33 -->
Between the dead and the living, there is a binary. Dying, however, is a continuum. Our dead works may not yet be completely dead in every category, but they will become so! Dead works are like poisons; some have stronger effects.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning normally. Some poisons have minor effects, like a headache. Others affect how we think and see, or even our ability to have children (living fruitfully). Dead works are exactly like that. They may be just a headache, and they may cause death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are any actions which, when reviewed and reflected upon, do not bring the abundant life that Jesus promised. Entering into a relationship outside the framework of marriage is a very potent poison, and it is likely to have a full and negative effect soon. And being needlessly stubborn and resentful will slowly poison a person until it drives everyone away from them.

<!-- p36 -->
To be saved from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
To ‘repent of dead works’ is to turn back to God and leave behind everything that harms our walk with Him-starting with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
And based on the poison analogy for dead works, if you discovered that your favorite snack was full of toxic heavy metals that would cause you a fatal condition if consumed, what would you do? We would put it far away from us without hesitation, and we would never enjoy it again. Aware that consuming it means our premature, and possibly painful, death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must turn away from the things of this life that poison us and lead us to both our natural and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To understand this fully we need to look a little deeper into the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The term used in Hebrews is *repentance*. And this is paired with the term *repent,* and there is an example of that in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
Repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb, it is an action or occurrence, and it means to change one’s mind or intention; to turn away from sin and turn to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun, it is a state of repentance, and it means a change in mind has taken place; a state of turning away from sin and turning toward God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews ‘repentance from dead works’ He is saying that we are to be in a state of repentance! We are to live in a continual state of having ‘turned away’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How often do we need to repent? A trick question! We need to change our minds constantly so that we no longer perform dead works. Repentance is not asking for forgiveness, or apologizing, although that may be included. To repent means to turn away from the previous way of thinking.

### Systems of change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in changing our opinions. If we do not perceive a need to change our opinions, why would we even open our eyes in the first place to look for change? Once again, returning to the poison analogy, if you cannot perceive that the poison is killing you, why would you stop consuming it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that one must hit ‘rock bottom,’ at which point they will reflect on their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that we humans are more complex. There are many theories related to behavior change in psychology, and one well-regarded theory is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **[1].**

<!-- p62 -->
People may go through a cycle of six stages of change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: There is no intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: There is an intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken to change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has been changed for a short period.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has been changed and is being maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: There is no desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no set timeframe for how long any stage may take. Some people, with regard to certain issues in their lives, never make meaningful progress through the stages. In other cases, we can move from stage 1 to 6 very quickly.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotion Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father experiences a sudden conviction after shouting at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *firm* but *loving* father. He believes raising his voice is necessary. He does not think about changing. In fact, he may even justify it- “This is how my father raised me.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, he blows up in anger and yells at his little daughter harshly, louder and angrier than ever before. She trembles. Her lip quivers. She walks away quietly. In that moment… something **shifts**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. The image of her hurt face plays over and over in his mind. His stomach turns. He says to himself, *“This is not the person I want to be.”* He knows he needs to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He enters her room, kneels, sincerely apologizes, and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You didn’t deserve that.”* She hugs him. Motivated to cement the change he prays and talks to a friend to seek support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he begins reading parenting books to work on developing positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He is not perfect, but something has truly changed. He no longer justifies the old behavior- and he has no desire to go back.

### Helping Others Change

<!-- p79 -->
Most of our discussion has centered on the act of repentance and its necessity from the perspective of the person who needs to change. However, we will encounter people who are in great need of change but are stuck in **Precontemplation**/**Contemplation** - blind to their need to change. We are also likely to encounter those who long to change, but do not know what to do (**Preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
Jesus, while preaching to the crowds, often encountered people who did not know they needed to change. We cannot forcefully change someone’s mind or repent on their behalf. That is not our place, nor is it the example of Jesus.

Remember that it is the Spirit’s work to bring conviction and illumination.

### Godly sorrow and the goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although hitting rock bottom is more a myth than a guaranteed prerequisite for change, God can use sorrow to affect our minds. God will use sorrow to lead us to repentance. He will convict us about our actions and the state of our lives and hearts to open our eyes so we see our complete and total need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> “For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.”
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>the experience of sorrow or distress that leads to *metanoia*</u>, is not the same as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of sorrow or distress, but the world offers no hope—only relief and the promise of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and mourning.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is enduring pain—physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual—especially when it is prolonged or severe.

<!-- p99 -->
Mourning is a multi-faceted emotional response to a significant personal loss, especially one related to relationships or existence.

<!-- p100 -->
Sorrow, suffering, and distress are closely interrelated concepts. In the human condition we will experience all three, and we can learn and grow through all three, but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
The Scriptures say that every good and perfect gift is from God above (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). And He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow causes discomfort, but His discipline is just and loving. And just as a good father does not deliberately break his son’s arm to teach him a “lesson,” so God does not inflict sickness or disease on His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### An Example of Worldly Sorrow versus Godly Sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick and deliberately hit her, making her cry and leaving a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
Their father heard the commotion and looked into it, and when he learned what his son had done he took the stick, broke it in two, and threw it away. He brought his son inside to think about what he had done, and he comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came in to address the matter with his son, he found him crying. He asked why he was crying, and the boy said, “Because you broke my stick.”

<!-- p106 -->
The father replied, “I am sad that you care more about your stick than about your sister whom you hurt,” and sent him back to his room for further reflection.

<!-- p107 -->
After a few minutes the boy returned and apologized. He said he was still sad because his father had broken his stick, and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example, the boy experienced both kinds of sorrow: worldly sorrow over the stick he lost, and finally godly sorrow over his sister whom he hurt.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and the goodness of God are connected. God is a good Father who allows us to experience the consequences of our actions, but He stands by us as we pass through those consequences.

<!-- p111 -->
God’s unwavering goodness, and His readiness to redeem and restore us if we come to Him, is a positive factor on the path of our repentance. His goodness is not so that we continue in sin; rather, despite our sins He makes a way for us to be redeemed and to experience His salvation.

<!-- p112 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">



> **<u>Romans 2:4</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Or do you despise the riches of His goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?
>



</span>

<!-- p113 -->
Despite God’s goodness, mercy, and grace, some people do not repent and do not change, but get stuck in a cycle of frustration and pain. By complete contrast, for some people, merely seeing an example of sin and its outcome in another person’s life is enough to move them to change their thinking.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a great capacity for change; it is rooted in the core of our being. Humans are highly adaptable; we can learn, grow, and find better ways of doing things. However, having the capacity to change is not the same as having the willingness or resolve to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some people get stuck while others are very willing to change, but I can say that the entire process of change is by the grace of God.

<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="8-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

Repentance may be a purely natural process, but repentance is also the work of the Spirit who calls us, opens our eyes to the truth, changes our hearts, and urges us to change. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:1</span> reminds us that some people will be ‘caught’ in a trespass (sin) and need help to get out of it! That help comes in the form of mature men and women who assist in restoring us in a spirit of gentleness.

<!-- p121 -->
The Holy Spirit is the greatest agent of change that humanity can engage with, and this is supported by both Scripture and research. The study “Lyons et al. (2010) found that up to 82% of clients who experienced a spiritual awakening during treatment and recovery from substance abuse were completely abstinent at one-year follow-up compared to 55% of clients who did not experience a spiritual awakening.” \[2\] And while human beings can initiate and mediate change, we can accomplish far more when we walk with the Spirit of God.

<!-- p122 -->
In our subsequent study of the Holy Spirit and sanctification, we will see more deeply how the Spirit calls and transforms human hearts. Let us look at an example of this, the story of King David and his example of repentance.

### David’s example of repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no account in Scripture offers a richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin recorded in 2 Samuel 11-12. It begins with King David remaining in Jerusalem instead of going out to war, where he sees Bathsheba as she is bathing. Although he knows she is the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David sends for her and sleeps with her, and Bathsheba conceives.

<!-- p125 -->
To cover up his actions, David summons Uriah from the battlefield, hoping he will spend time with Bathsheba and appear to be the child’s father. But Uriah, out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, refuses to go to his house to be with Bathsheba. David then arranges to send Uriah to the front lines where he is ultimately killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David hastily marries Bathsheba to conceal the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
What David had done displeased the Lord, so He sent the prophet Nathan to confront him. And when the prophet confronted him, David immediately confessed: “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify his actions or make excuses for them. This confession was the first step in his repentance, as he acknowledged his wrongdoing directly before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me*,” show his full ownership of his actions. He expresses not only sorrow for the sin itself but also for his offense against God, saying: “*Against You, You only, have I sinned*.” David displayed “*a broken and contrite heart*,” which is an essential part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he mourned deeply over the sin he had committed and the harm it caused. The depth of his remorse was a sign that he wanted not only forgiveness but a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God: “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He sought an inner transformation that would keep him from repeating the same mistakes. Instead of merely apologizing, David pleaded with God to purify him, saying: “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He recognized that only God could cleanse his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire for spiritual renewal reflects a genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways thereafter. His request for a “*a willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) shows his hope to be in harmony with God in every aspect of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
And although God put away David’s sin, the Lord struck the child and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God to keep the child alive, but when the child died he accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find a personal prayer of repentance and a public acknowledgment of his transgressions. David’s decision to record his plea for mercy, creating one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible, shows his willingness to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony to repentance and restoration for others to learn from, immortalizing his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not merely a feeling of regret; it was a profound change in his heart and life. Through the confession, acceptance, and inner transformation recorded in Psalm 51, David memorialized his sin as a lesson on God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most prominent examples of true repentance in the Bible.
