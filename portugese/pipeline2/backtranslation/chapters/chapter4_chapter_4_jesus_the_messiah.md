# Chapter 4 - Jesus-The Messiah

<!-- p1 -->
From our study of sin, we conclude that sin exacts a serious price, both from those against whom sin is committed and who must be restored, and from those who commit sin and must make restitution. The price of sin must be paid. In the Old Testament, God established a series of sacrifices conducted by priests, individuals, and communities to deal with, resolve, and judge sin.

<!-- p2 -->
Even with all the sacrifices for sin, they still were not sufficient-not because God failed, but because people continued to choose sin. Although animal sacrifice could provide limited atonement, no amount of animal sacrifices was able to change the sinful nature of men.

<!-- p3 -->
God had a wonderful plan, the <u>Messiah</u> (in Hebrew ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) also known as the <u>Christ</u> (in Greek ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**) meaning “Anointed"; someone consecrated for a special role (for example, king, priest, or deliverer).

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it helps to look at what the people of His time expected the Messiah to be. These expectations shaped how they saw Jesus and, in many cases, why they had difficulty recognizing Him as the fulfillment of God’s plan.

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical Context

<!-- p6 -->
The Jewish people had long awaited the coming of a Messiah. Jewish beliefs about the Messiah during the period immediately preceding Jesus were shaped by Old Testament prophecies, whose interpretations were diverse and not universally agreed upon. Most Jews expected the Messiah to be a political and military leader who would free Israel from foreign oppression (primarily from Roman rule) and restore the Davidic kingdom. Here is an overview of common messianic expectations and how they aligned (or did not) with biblical prophecy:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish Expectations of the Messiah Before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A Political and Military Deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would be a king like David, leading Israel in a victorious revolt against foreign powers and restoring Israel’s independence.

<!-- p10 -->
- **Scriptures Referenced:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 9:6-7</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JER" data-chapter="23" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Jeremiah 23:5-6</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ZEC" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Zechariah 9:9-10</span>.

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical Context:** Jews under Roman rule were oppressed and heavily taxed, leading to hopes for a Messiah who would overthrow Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p12 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus is a King, but His kingdom is not earthly (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="36" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 18:36</span>). He established a spiritual kingdom through His first coming, but will return as a conquering King to judge and reign in righteousness, as described in the book of Revelation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 19:11-16</span>).

<!-- p13 -->
### **2. A Restorer of the Davidic Kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would rebuild David’s dynasty and bring a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace.

<!-- p15 -->
- **Scriptures Referenced:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 7:12-16</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="AMO" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Amos 9:11</span>.

<!-- p16 -->
- **Interpretation:** The focus was often on national restoration rather than spiritual renewal.

<!-- p17 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus, as a descendant of David, fulfills this promise by establishing an eternal kingdom. Although this kingdom is spiritual in nature now, it will culminate in a physical reign during His second coming (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="32-33" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:4-6</span>).

<!-- p18 -->
### **3. A Prophet like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief:** Some Jews believed that the Messiah would be a prophet, like Moses, who would speak directly with God and in His name.

<!-- p20 -->
- **Referenced Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="DEU" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Deuteronomy 18:15-19</span>.

<!-- p21 -->
- **Expectation:** Although many anticipated a prophetic figure, they often did not see this role as central to the Messiah, but as an additional aspect.

<!-- p22 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus fulfilled this prophecy as the supreme Prophet, speaking the Word of God and directly revealing the Father (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:18</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:10</span>). His prophetic role goes beyond Moses, for He mediates a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### **4. A Leader Who Would Bring Justice**

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would judge the wicked, uphold the righteous, and bring justice to the oppressed.

<!-- p25 -->
- **Referenced Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 11:3-5</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="72" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 72:1-4</span>.

<!-- p26 -->
- **Interpretation:** Justice was expected to be primarily political and social, not spiritual.

<!-- p27 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus brings ultimate justice. At His first coming, He offered spiritual redemption and righteousness through the cross (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Colossians 2:13-15</span>). At His second coming, He will execute judgment on the wicked and establish perfect justice for all eternity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>).

<!-- p28 -->
### Diverse Messianic Beliefs

<!-- p29 -->
Just as Christendom today is divided into sects, denominations, or separate belief structures, so too were the Jews in Jesus' time divided. During the time of Jesus, four major Jewish sects shaped the religious and political landscape of Israel, each with distinct beliefs and expectations about the Messiah. Understanding a bit of the background about these different groups can help provide meaningful context for a study of the New Testament Scriptures.

<!-- p30 -->
The **Pharisees** were the most influential among the common people, known for their strict observance of the Law (Torah) and the oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, emphasizing personal piety and obedience to God's commandments. Their messianic expectation centered on a figure who would uphold the Law, bring justice, and restore Israel both politically and religiously.

<!-- p31 -->
The **Sadducees**, by contrast, were a wealthy and politically powerful group that controlled the Temple and the priesthood. They rejected the oral traditions and accepted only the Torah as authoritative Scripture. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, focusing on maintaining their power and collaborating with Rome to ensure political stability. Consequently, they placed little emphasis on a Messiah, since their primary concern was to preserve the status quo.

<!-- p32 -->
The **Essenes** were a separatist group that withdrew from mainstream Jewish society, living in isolated communities such as Qumran. They were deeply focused on ritual purity, apocalyptic expectations, and a disciplined lifestyle. The Essenes believed in two messianic figures: a priestly Messiah who would restore the holiness of the Temple and a royal Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness. This concept of two Messiahs reflected their hope for a spiritual and political renewal. The Essenes are not mentioned directly in Scripture.

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, the **Zealots** were militant nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent rebellion. They rejected Roman authority and were deeply committed to the restoration of Israel's sovereignty. Their messianic expectation was for a warrior-king Messiah, like David, who would lead them into battle against their oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom.

<!-- p34 -->
While these groups focused on earthly expectations, Jesus' mission revealed a much greater plan-one that addressed not only political oppression but humanity's spiritual slavery to sin.

<!-- p35 -->
## The Truth of the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### The Anointing of Jesus

<!-- p37 -->
The Pharisees' and Zealots' view of a Messiah reflects a recurring pattern in human history-seeking salvation by earthly means while rejecting God's greater plan. As we have seen throughout the Scriptures, people often turn away from God-although He is always faithful. Even when God is faithful and accepts us, He is often forgotten and rejected, leading to cycles of disobedience and decay that plagued earthly kingdoms.

<!-- p38 -->
Although many expected a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a much greater purpose for His anointing-one that met spiritual and eternal needs. If Jesus had come as a conquering military leader, as the Jews expected, it would have been only a matter of time before the kingdom fell into decay because of human disobedience.

<!-- p39 -->
Instead, Jesus established a kingdom that cannot be shaken. His mission was not rooted in military power or political rule, but in divine purpose. As the Anointed One, Jesus explained what He was anointed to do and how He would accomplish it:

<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16-21" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

>

>

>
> "THE SPIRIT OF THE LORD IS UPON ME, BECAUSE HE HAS ANOINTED ME TO PREACH THE GOSPEL TO THE POOR; HE HAS SENT ME TO HEAL THE BROKENHEARTED, TO PROCLAIM LIBERTY TO THE CAPTIVES AND RECOVERY OF SIGHT TO THE BLIND, TO SET AT LIBERTY THOSE WHO ARE OPPRESSED;

Jesus was anointed-set apart by God-and sent with a divine mission, full of purpose and power.

<!-- p44 -->
Jesus proclaimed:

<!-- p45 -->
- The good news (gospel) of the Kingdom.

<!-- p46 -->
- Deliverance - forgiveness, remission of sin, freedom.

<!-- p47 -->
- The acceptable year (time) of the Lord.

<!-- p48 -->
The people to whom He came were:

<!-- p49 -->
- Brokenhearted - shattered emotionally and spiritually.

<!-- p50 -->
- Blind - spiritually and, at times, physically.

<!-- p51 -->
- Poor - destitute, in poverty (spiritual and/or physical).

<!-- p52 -->
- Captives - meaning ‘taken with a spear’ representing those held in captivity against their own will.

<!-- p53 -->
Jesus transformed these broken people by:

<!-- p54 -->
- Restoring sight

<!-- p55 -->
- Putting into a state of freedom

<!-- p56 -->
- Binding up, healing, restoring

<!-- p57 -->
The purpose of Jesus’s ministry and service was both individual and global, immediate and lasting. His purpose does not have a single focus, but is part of God’s divine plan to reconcile humanity to God. After sin entered the world, it caused a rift between God and humanity. God had to cast man out of the garden and from His presence. Throughout the Old Testament, man could be with God, but only partially. It was very rare for the Spirit of God to be given to men and, for the most part, men lived in isolation from God. He found a people in the Jews, but wanted a permanent, secure, and complete means of restoring His relationship with humanity.

<!-- p58 -->
The presence of God on earth at the time of Jesus was, for the most part, restricted to the innermost part of the temple, the Holy of Holies. There, behind the thick veil that separated the innermost part from the Holy Place, the presence of God rested by the ark of the covenant. Man was isolated from God, and God was separated from His creation.

<!-- p59 -->
God sent Jesus to repair, rebuild, restore, and reconcile humanity to Himself. (2 Corinthians 5:17-21)

<!-- p60 -->
### The Necessary Work of Jesus

<!-- p61 -->
To fully reconcile humanity with God, the mission of the Messiah required not only proclamation and healing, but also the supreme act of atonement. Jesus had to become the perfect and complete sacrifice, through His death on the cross.

<!-- p62 -->
The book of Hebrews explains extensively, over its 13 chapters, how Jesus became the perfect sacrifice, being at the same time the sin offering and the High Priest who offers it (Hebrews 3 &amp; 4). Jesus’ priesthood is not from the Levitical lineage (from the tribe of Levi), but is according to the order of Melchizedek, of whom it is said there is no beginning or end, which means it is not limited to the standards and habits of men (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 7:3</span>). The limitation of the Levitical priesthood is that it depends on the blood of bulls and goats (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:12-13</span>), whose sacrifice is insufficient for the permanent and lasting remission of sins. From our study on sin and sacrifice, we know that the penalty for the people’s sins could be postponed for a year (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:3</span>) during the Day of Atonement, but that sacrifice needed to be constantly renewed.

<!-- p63 -->
Jesus, in dying on the cross (whose accounts are in Matthew 27, Mark 15, John 19, and Luke 23), shed His blood and made the payment for the sins, past, present, and future, of all humanity. Isaiah 53 reveals Jesus as the Suffering Servant, who took upon Himself the full weight of punishment and anguish. His death, once for all, became the final and supreme sacrifice. Jesus is the Lamb of God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="29" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:29</span>), without spot or wrinkle (sin); because He was perfect, complete, and holy, the shedding of His innocent blood made atonement possible. **No further sacrifices are necessary or acceptable. The former were only temporary-but Jesus’ was complete and final.**

<!-- p64 -->
Through death and resurrection, we have a new and superior covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6</span>), secured with the blood of the very Son of God; Jesus is the mediator of the covenant, He is the intermediary. Through the sacrifice of Christ, we receive salvation, and He is the author of *‘eternal salvation for all who obey Him*’ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 5:9</span>).

<!-- p65 -->
The word salvation in Greek is **Sōtēria** (*so-tay-REE-ah - **G4991)**.* It primarily means **salvation, deliverance, or preservation**. It encompasses the act of being saved, both in a spiritual sense (eternal salvation through Christ) and in physical contexts (rescue from danger or harm). The term conveys the idea of safety, wholeness, and restoration.

<!-- p66 -->
**Salvation or Deliverance:**

<!-- p67 -->
- Refers to the deliverance, brought about by God, of humanity from sin and its consequences through Jesus Christ.

<!-- p68 -->
- Used in contexts of eternal salvation (justification, sanctification, and glorification).

<!-- p69 -->
**Preservation:**

<!-- p70 -->
- Implies being kept safe, protected, or preserved from danger.

<!-- p71 -->
**Wholeness or Healing:**

<!-- p72 -->
- Includes restoration to a state of completeness or wholeness.

<!-- p73 -->
Jesus’ sacrifice paid the price for us to be forgiven and opened a way for us to have a relationship with God, establishing for us a path of fellowship with Him that depended only on our obedience, not on our perfection or on our ability to abstain from sin.

<!-- p74 -->
When we accept Jesus, by faith (discussed later), we receive His salvation. This salvation connects us with God and puts our name in the ***Lamb’s Book of Life*** (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>). The Gospel of John says:

<!-- p75 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p76 -->
> **<u>John 3:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that everyone who believes in Him should not perish, but have eternal life.
>

<!-- p77 -->
</span>

<!-- p78 -->
To perish means to be ruined - like a fruit that spoils before it can be enjoyed. It is a life that loses what it was made for. Jesus came to give us perpetual, or eternal, life, and to keep us from perishing. Eternal life is not the life that begins after we die, it is the life that begins at the moment we accept Jesus as our salvation, when we are born again.

### Born Again

<!-- p80 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

When we accept Jesus, we become ‘born again.’ This expression is used by Jesus to describe a point of transition, when we become born of the Spirit (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:1-15</span>). This happens through faith-by choosing to trust Him fully, like entering into a marriage covenant, with the heart and with words.

<!-- p84 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

When we were born on earth (born of the flesh and of water), many things happened: we took our first breaths and were declared alive, we were welcomed by our parents, we received a name, we became heirs, we received food and protection, and we continued to grow.

<!-- p88 -->
When we place our trust in Jesus and receive Him, we are born again, not of the flesh, but of the Spirit. We become spiritually alive! Just as in physical birth, many things accompany spiritual birth:

<!-- p89 -->
1.  When we accept Jesus, God has wiped away our sins and declared us forgiven (**justified**). He gave us a fresh start. The wall of separation between us and Him is removed. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="TIT" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Titus 3:5</span>)

3.  We were **adopted** into the family of God and became heirs. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 1:5</span>)

<!-- p92 -->
4.  We receive a source of spiritual nourishment, and eternal protection. (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="1PE" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Peter 2:2</span>)
