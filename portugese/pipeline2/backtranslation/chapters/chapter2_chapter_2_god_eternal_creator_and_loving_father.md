# Chapter 2 - God-Eternal Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is crucial that we establish a firm foundation in the knowledge of who God is. We can come to understand by studying:

<!-- p2 -->
- His desires, intentions, and actions,

<!-- p3 -->
- His nature, character, and attributes,

<!-- p4 -->
- What is said of Him and what He says of Himself.

<!-- p5 -->
The Scriptures uniquely reveal the characteristics of God, with each facet deepening our understanding of the immutable and eternal God.

<!-- p6 -->
Summarizing God in a single descriptor is challenging, if not impossible, for He is far too vast and multifaceted for simplifications. Although we can, and will, make great efforts to explain, describe, and portray God, ultimately, like art and music, He must be experienced to be understood.

<!-- p7 -->
When a person hears music, they describe it by expressing how it moves them-how it resonates within, beyond just the notes and rhythms. A melody can carry warmth or intensity, with a rhythm that pulses like a heartbeat, or crescendos that create a sense of euphoria or longing. Music can soothe like a gentle wave or electrify like a storm, lifting the listener’s spirits, settling their thoughts, or transporting them to another place entirely. That experience is much more than the sequence of sounds; it is the soul of the music that remains, captured in the way it shapes the listener’s emotions and personal journey.

<!-- p8 -->
Similarly, one who contemplates a painting looks beyond the colors or brushstrokes, exploring what those elements represent. A landscape can evoke a serene escape, while an abstract work can awaken curiosity or introspection. Certain tones may bring warmth, while a composition can create a sense of calm, chaos, or beauty. The painting becomes a living experience, provoking a shift in perspective or a deep emotional reaction that words alone cannot capture. Both music and art, then, become more than their physical forms-they become encounters that touch something essential, changing the listener or the viewer in profound and personal ways.

## Exploring God through the Old Testament

<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: The God who Dwells with His Creation

<!-- p11 -->
A central theme in God’s narrative is His **desire to dwell** with His creation. This is made clear in the stories throughout Genesis. Through its fifty chapters and genealogies from Adam (the first man) to Joseph (the last major figure in the book of Genesis, the last significant leader of God’s people before Moses), there is a period of approximately ~2.400 years. We can see, through these stories, the constancy of God’s goodness despite the evil of man.

<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God establishes relationships with specific individuals.

<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - The first humans, created by God and placed in the Garden of Eden. God walks and talks with them, giving them instructions and establishing a relationship of mutual love and obedience. However, this relationship is impacted by their disobedience in eating from the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil (Genesis 1-3).

<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - Although Cain ultimately rebels by murdering his brother Abel, God speaks directly to him, warning him about the danger of sin and offering him guidance before the murder. Even after Cain’s punishment, God marks him to protect him from harm (Genesis 4).

<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - God establishes a unique relationship with Noah, a righteous man, in a time of widespread corruption. God commands Noah to build an ark to save his family and the animals from the Flood. Afterwards, God makes a covenant with Noah, promising never again to destroy the earth with a flood and establishing the rainbow as the sign of that covenant (Genesis 6-9).

<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - One of the most significant relationships in Genesis, God calls Abram to leave his homeland and promises to make him the father of a great nation. God establishes a covenant with Abram, promising land, offspring, and blessings for all nations through him. Abram’s faith and obedience become central themes, and his name is changed to Abraham as part of the covenant (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22).

<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - An Egyptian servant of Sarah, Hagar encounters God in the wilderness after being mistreated. God comforts her, promises her a future for her son Ishmael, and instructs her to return to Abraham and Sarah. Hagar refers to God as “El Roi” (“The God who sees me”), recognizing His care for her (Genesis 16).

<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - The son of Abraham and Sarah, Isaac inherits the covenant promises made to his father. God reaffirms these promises directly to Isaac, including the promise of offspring and blessings. God also guides Isaac during a famine, protecting him and establishing the covenant relationship (Genesis 26).

<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - Although her relationship with God is indirect, God answers Rebekah’s prayer about her difficult pregnancy, revealing that her twins, Jacob and Esau, will become two nations with distinct destinies (Genesis 25).

<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God establishes a personal relationship with Jacob, appearing to him in a dream and confirming the covenant promises of land, offspring, and blessings. Jacob encounters God several times, including the famous wrestling match in which God changes his name to Israel, symbolizing his transformation and the establishment of Israel as a people (Genesis 28, 32, 35).

<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Although Joseph’s relationship with God is not based on direct appearances or covenants like those of Abraham or Jacob, God is deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, divine favor, and providence, God guides Joseph’s journey, from being sold as a slave to becoming a powerful leader in Egypt. Joseph’s faith in God’s sovereignty enables him to forgive his brothers, recognizing that God used his suffering for good (Genesis 37, 39-50).

<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this narrative, God, who desires to dwell with His creation, must be content to dwell with selected individuals and, finally, through the covenant with Abraham, establishes a people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="17" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 17:7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: The God Who Dwells with His People

<!-- p24 -->
God’s desire to dwell with His people is also clearly seen in the second book of the Bible, Exodus. In the book of Exodus, the people of Israel, who for a period lived in relative peace and harmony in Egypt, are now being oppressed by a Pharaoh who did not know Joseph and feared the Hebrews (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 1:6-8</span>). God’s people lived in Egypt for about ~400 years (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="40-41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 12:40-41</span>), of which the latter part was spent as slaves (there is no universal consensus on exactly how long Israel was in captivity in Egypt).

<!-- p25 -->
In the powerful presence of the burning bush that was not consumed, God reveals Himself to a Hebrew by birth, raised as an Egyptian, a murderer in exile, who was shepherding his father-in-law’s flock in the wilderness, Moses.

<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And God said to Moses: "I AM WHO I AM." And He said: "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: 'I AM has sent me to you.'"
>

<!-- p28 -->
</span>

<!-- p29 -->
The expression **“I AM WHO I AM”** (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is one of the deepest self-revelations in the Scriptures. When Moses asks for God’s name, God answers with this phrase, which can be translated as "I Am Who I Am," "I Will Be Who I Will Be," or simply "I Am." This statement, at first glance, is a bit puzzling, but it carries profound meaning. Can you imagine introducing yourself to someone new as ‘I am’? When God reveals Himself as ‘I AM WHO I AM’ He is attesting to His nature, His eternal presence, His sovereignty. ‘I am’ is infinite in meaning and purpose.

<!-- p30 -->
God uses Moses to reveal Himself to Pharaoh:

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Exodus 7:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And you shall say to him: 'The LORD God of the Hebrews has sent me to you, saying: "Let My people go, that they may serve Me in the wilderness"; but indeed, until now you have not been willing to listen!'
>

<!-- p33 -->
</span>

<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh that God is the ‘I AM’ and that God demands that the Israelites be freed so that they may worship Him in the wilderness.

<!-- p35 -->
Plagues and misery follow, but, in the end, Pharaoh relents and allows the Israelites to leave.

<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="31" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p37 -->
> **<u>Exodus 12:31</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Then he called for Moses and Aaron by night and said: "Rise, go out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel. Go, serve the LORD, as you have said.
>

<!-- p38 -->
</span>

<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness, God miraculously delivers His people from the vengeful Egyptian army (Exodus 14), provides protection from the sun and a pillar of fire to keep them warm in the cold desert night (Exodus 13), food from heaven (Exodus 16), and water to drink (Exodus 17).

<!-- p40 -->
God leads His people to Mount Sinai, where God commands Moses to consecrate the people and gives them the law (including the 10 commandments) and establishes with them His covenant (the old covenant) (Exodus 19, 20, 24). That covenant (the Mosaic covenant) is based on the Abrahamic covenant and endures from Moses to Jesus.

<!-- p41 -->
Despite entering into a covenant with His people, the relationship is that of a loving and jealous God who seeks and remains with a very stubborn and ungrateful people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="32" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 32:9</span>). Ignoring God’s miraculous intervention, they maintain a rebellious attitude through large parts of the text. God even brings them to the promised land and tells them they may inherit it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="33" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 33:3</span>), but, out of fear and disobedience, they ignore the supernatural deliverance that God had granted them and, instead, must wander in the wilderness for 40 years, until almost all of that generation had died (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-34" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).

<!-- p42 -->
### The rest of the Old Testament

<!-- p43 -->
In the ~1,400 years between the death of Moses and the birth of Christ, the Israelites experienced a recurring cycle of devotion that brings God’s favor and blessings, followed by rebellion, God’s judgment, their subsequent repentance, and, finally, God’s deliverance. This pattern is particularly evident in the books of **Judges, Kings, and Chronicles**, which recount cycles in which the people turn away from God, often worshiping other gods, resulting in periods of oppression or exile. In response to their cries, God repeatedly raised up judges, prophets, or leaders to deliver them.

<!-- p44 -->
Throughout those years, despite the Israelites’ frequent failures to keep their covenant with Him, God remains faithful, demonstrating both justice and mercy. He continually rescues and forgives His people, demonstrating His enduring commitment and love.

<!-- p45 -->
As we see in Scripture, God reveals Himself through actions, relationships, and even names that speak of His character. Each name represents a specific aspect of His total character. These names were given as part of a narrative of how God revealed Himself to His people. For your personal study, a reference table is included in the Appendix: *Table 10 - Names of God in the Old Testament.* Read these names and study the biblical references. Each reference is a story that precisely describes how God saved, delivered, and rescued His people.

<!-- p46 -->
Also included in the Appendix is an additional set of attributes that describe His nature, descriptions of His actions and intentions: *Table 11 - Attributes of God.* These attributes, though slightly more abstract than the names of God, are no less authentic!

<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (the **Trinity**)-working together as one. In our ongoing study, we will examine in greater depth the names and attributes of Jesus and of the Holy Spirit.

<!-- p48 -->
The character and actions of God are fully expressed in the unity of Father, Son, and Spirit. Scripture teaches that God is one in essence, but exists as three distinct persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Scripture affirms the oneness of God, declaring: “besides Him there is no God” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="45" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 45:5</span>). However, the Godhead is understood as a composite unity - three distinct persons who cooperate with a single mind and purpose, united in the truest sense. Each person of the Trinity has a unique role: the **Father creates**, the **Son redeems**, and the **Holy Spirit sanctifies**. The term “Trinity” does not appear in the Bible; it was developed as a theological expression to describe this concept, which is revealed in Scripture.

<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can be illustrated by examples in nature, which help clarify this idea of unity in diversity. Like water, which can be liquid, ice, or vapor, yet remains H₂O, each person of the Trinity is fully God, yet distinct in personhood. Or consider a triangle, with three sides and three angles, forming a single complete figure; if one side is removed, it ceases to be a triangle. Similarly, **the nature of God is incomplete without all three persons**, who work together in perfect unity.

<!-- p50 -->
- **God the Father**: He is the creator and sustainer of all things, often understood as the initiator within the Godhead. His love for humanity is central in the biblical story, exemplified in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:16</span>. In our study, we will examine God the Father as the loving Father and righteous Judge, who opened a way for our redemption.

<!-- p51 -->
- **God the Son**: Coexistent and coeternal with the Father, Jesus took on human nature, being conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the virgin Mary. He lived a sinless life, died to redeem humanity, and rose again, conquering sin and death. He now sits at the right hand of the Father, interceding for us (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In our study, we will examine Jesus as the perfect atoning sacrifice, the risen Lord, and head of the church.

<!-- p52 -->
- **God the Holy Spirit**: The Holy Spirit, coexistent and equal with the Father and the Son, is sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world of sin and prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. He empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In our study, we will examine the Holy Spirit as the Comforter, given by God to equip, empower, sanctify, and dwell with us.

<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct persons, the triune nature of God is revealed as a perfect union in purpose, will, and love, each person playing a vital role in the divine mission of creation, redemption, and sanctification. The Trinity invites believers to experience God fully, as a loving Father, a redeeming Savior, and a transforming Spirit.

<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion

<!-- p55 -->
We can be uniquely known by our names: husband, father, son, brother, friend. Each of these is a statement of who we are in relation to another person. We can also be known by our attributes: faithful, patient, kind, intelligent, funny. In the same way, we can know God by His attributes and names - He is our Healer, Provider, Shepherd, and Redeemer. However, as labels and words are only a small part of us, for us to be truly known by others, we need to be experienced. Even more so with God. To truly know Him fully, we need to experience Him.

<!-- p56 -->
The names of God reveal His character, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of what those names mean. For example, “God, my Healer” takes on a richer and deeper meaning when we understand the depths of illness, despair, or the helplessness from which God rescues us. Knowing Him as our Provider is transformative when we feel true need and find Him faithful.

<!-- p57 -->
Scripture offers a context for these encounters, showing us God’s faithfulness to His people across time and circumstances, reinforcing how His names and attributes are not mere titles, but realities that He brings to life, experienced in the lives of individuals. It is through this interrelationship between personal experience and the biblical narrative that we come to know God - not just as a distant description, but as a present and active relationship in every season of our lives.
