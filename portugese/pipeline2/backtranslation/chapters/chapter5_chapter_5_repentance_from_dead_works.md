# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
Many times in Christianity we think of repentance as something fundamental only for the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The heart of repentance goes much deeper than turning away from sin for salvation. Repentance from dead works means living with our hearts turned away from the things that harm us or hinder our growth-and turned toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of Hebrews places ‘repentance from dead works’ as the first item on the list of the fundamental doctrines after the foundational principles of the Messiah (Chapter 4).

### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Every person who has ever been employed knows what work is. Work is the set of tasks you perform in order to receive a wage so that you can earn a living (employment). Employment is an agreement to exchange your talents and efforts for something of value that will help sustain you. You work because you get something out of it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important, it can bring a sense of purpose, often a sense of identity. It is very common to be asked ‘What do you do for a living?’ and the answer begins with a statement of identity ‘I am’. Although work can be enjoyable and have purpose, it is also very likely to be physically or mentally hard, difficult, challenging and stressful. If your work is none of these things, you may find it overly simple and boring.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work 40 hours a week and sleep 8 hours a night, you spend 36% of your waking hours working, and that excludes the time you spend getting ready for work, commuting to work, taking care of a home, and all the other necessary tasks you need to do to keep your life on track.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? It varies, but, by definition, because we get something of value from it.

<!-- p13 -->
Would you keep working at a job if they stopped paying you? For most of us the easy and painfully obvious answer is a resounding, NO!

<!-- p14 -->
Would you keep working at a job if it were killing you? I suppose it depends on how quickly.

<!-- p15 -->
The context of ***nekros*** joined with ***erga*** is work or labor that is dead, has no vitality, cannot reproduce! The author of Hebrews is warning us about a work that is dead.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, there are several criteria that are used to determine whether an organism is alive (this is a partial list used for our purpose):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living beings convert energy through metabolic processes to grow, develop and sustain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and Development**: Life involves growth and progression over time, with a specific life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living beings have the ability to reproduce, creating offspring or new entities.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adjust to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as temperature regulation or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living beings can adapt to their environments through genetic changes, allowing survival under various conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this ‘life’ pattern to our works with a bit of creative license, then dead works are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, are unable to produce a positive or desirable outcome.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God’s presence, or to the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Do not maintain internal stability, become dysregulated, are not self-controlled.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (are too rigid) and no longer help us survive.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s desire for us. God wants to give us life!

Although dead versus alive is binary. Dying is a gradient. Our dead works may not be completely dead in every category yet, but they will become so! Dead works are like poisons; some have greater effects.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning normally. Some poisons have small effects, like a headache. Others affect how we think, see, or even whether we can have children (live fruitfully). Dead works are exactly like that. They may be just a headache, or they may cause death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are any works that, when reviewed and considered, are not bringing the abundant life that Jesus promised. Having an extramarital affair is a very strong poison; it is likely to have a full and negative effect very soon. Being an unnecessarily stubborn and resentful person will slowly poison someone until they have driven everyone away from them.

<!-- p36 -->
To be freed from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
Repenting of ‘dead works’ is turning to God and leaving behind everything that is hurting our walk with Him-beginning with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Starting from the poison analogy for dead works, if you discovered that your favorite snack was full of toxic heavy metals that would give you a fatal condition if consumed, what would you do? Without hesitation, we would put it away from us, and never enjoy it again. Realizing that consuming it means our premature and likely painful death.

<!-- p40 -->
We need to repent! We need to turn away from the things of this life that are poisoning us and leading us to both our natural and our spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To fully understand this, we need to examine the Greek language a little more deeply.

<!-- p42 -->
The term used in Hebrews is *repentance*. This is contrasted with the term *to repent,* and an example is found in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
To repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or occurrence; it means to change one’s mind or purpose; to turn away from sin and turn to God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is a state of having repented; it means to have a change of mind; the state of having turned away from sin and turned to God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews ‘repentance from dead works’ He is saying to be in the state of one who has already repented! We must live in a continual state of having ‘turned away’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times do we need to repent? Trick question! We need to change our mind continually until we are no longer practicing dead works. To repent is **not to ask for forgiveness, or to apologize**, although that may be involved. To repent is to turn away from the former way of thinking.

### Systems of Change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in changing your mind. If we do not perceive the need to change our mind, then why would we open our eyes to look for change? Returning to the poison analogy, if you cannot perceive that the poison is killing you, why would you stop consuming it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that someone simply needs to hit “rock bottom,” then will look at their own life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us that we humans are more complex. There are many theories related to behavior change in the study of psychology; a highly regarded theory is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** developed in the late 1970s by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente **\[1\].**

<!-- p62 -->
People can go through a cycle of six stages of change:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: There is no intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: There is an intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken to change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has been changed for a short period.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has been changed and continues to be maintained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: There is no desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no set duration for how long each stage may take. Some people, with respect to certain aspects of their lives, never progress meaningfully through the stages. In other situations, we can go from stage 1 to 6 very quickly.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father who experiences a sudden conviction after yelling at his child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *firm* but *loving* father. He believes that raising his voice is necessary. He is not considering changing. In fact, he may even justify it - “This is how my father raised me.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, he loses control and yells at his little daughter harshly, louder and angrier than ever. She flinches. Her lip trembles. She moves away in silence. In that moment… something **changes**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. The image of her hurt face plays over and over in his mind. His stomach churns. He says to himself, *“This is not who I want to be.”* He knows he needs to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes into her room, kneels, sincerely apologizes, and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You didn’t deserve that.”* She hugs him. Motivated to consolidate the change, he prays and talks with a friend seeking support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day, he begins reading parenting books to work on developing positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: Months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He is not perfect, but something has really changed. He no longer justifies the old behavior - and has no desire to go back.

### Godly Sorrow and the Goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
Although hitting rock bottom is more a myth than a sure condition for change, God can use sorrow to influence our minds. God will use sorrow to bring us to repentance. He will convict us of our actions and the state of our lives and hearts to open our eyes to see our complete and total need of Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>the experience of sorrow or anguish that leads to a *metanoia*</u>, is not the same thing as worldly sorrow. Worldly sorrow is the same experience of sorrow or anguish, but the world offers no hope, only relief and the promise of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and grief.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is the resistance to pain-physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual-especially when prolonged or intense.

<!-- p99 -->
Grief is a multifaceted emotional response to a significant personal loss, especially relational or existential.

<!-- p100 -->
Sadness, suffering, and sorrow are closely related ideas. In the human condition, we will experience all three, we can learn and grow through all three, but God will not bring death and destruction into our lives to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
The Scriptures say that every good gift and every perfect gift comes from God above (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow will cause discomfort, but His discipline is just and loving. Just as a good father would not intentionally break his child’s arm to ‘teach him a lesson,’ God will not inflict sickness or disease on His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### Example of Worldly Sorrow vs Godly Sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick and, intentionally, hit her, making her cry and leaving a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
The father, upon hearing the commotion, went to check and, upon learning what his son had done, took the stick, broke it in half, and threw it away. He sent the boy inside to think about what he had done and comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
The father, coming in to deal with the boy, found him crying. When he asked why he was crying, the boy said: “because you broke my stick”.

<!-- p106 -->
The father’s response was: “I am sad that you are more concerned about your stick than about your sister, whom you hurt” and he sent him back to the room to reflect more.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later, the boy returned and apologized. He said he was still sad because his father had broken his stick and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example, the boy experienced both worldly sorrow, for the lost stick, and then, finally, godly sorrow, for the sister he hurt.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and the goodness of God are connected. God is a good Father who allows us to experience the consequences of our actions, yet still stands by our side as we face them.

<!-- p111 -->
God’s relentless goodness, in being willing to redeem and restore us if we turn to Him, is a positive agent in our path of repentance. His goodness is not that we continue in sin, but that, despite our sin, He opens a way for us to be redeemed and to experience His salvation.

On the opposite end, for some people, the simple example of sin and consequence in someone else’s life is enough to lead them to change their way of thinking.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a great capacity to change; this is inscribed in our very essence. Humans are highly adaptable; we can learn and grow and find better ways of doing things. However, the capacity to change is not the same as the willingness or conviction to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some people get stuck and others are so ready to change, but I can say that the entire process of change indeed comes from the grace of God.

### David’s Example of Repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps no account in Scripture has a richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin found in 2 Samuel 11-12. It begins with King David remaining in Jerusalem instead of going to war, where he sees Bathsheba bathing. Despite knowing that she is the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal soldiers, David sends for her, and they have an affair, resulting in Bathsheba’s pregnancy.

<!-- p125 -->
To cover up his actions, David calls Uriah back from the battlefield, hoping that he will spend time with Bathsheba and appear to be the father of the child. However, Uriah, out of loyalty to his fellow soldiers, refuses to go to his house with Bathsheba. David then arranges for Uriah to be sent to the front line, where he ends up being killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David hastily marries Bathsheba to cover up the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord is displeased with David’s actions and sends the prophet Nathan to confront him. When confronted by the prophet, David immediately admitted: “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify or excuse his actions. This confession was the first step in his repentance, acknowledging his transgression directly to God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me,*” show his complete responsibility for his actions. He expresses sorrow not only for the sin itself, but also for how it offended God, saying: “*Against you, you only, have I sinned.*” David demonstrated “*a broken and contrite heart,*” an essential part of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he deeply mourned the sin he committed and the pain he caused. This depth of remorse was a sign that he wanted not only forgiveness, but a transformed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God: “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He sought an inner transformation that would keep him from repeating the same mistakes. Instead of merely apologizing, David pleaded with God to cleanse him, saying: “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He recognized that only God could purify his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be spiritually renewed reflects a genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways from now on. His request for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) demonstrates his hope to be aligned with God in every area of his life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God forgave David’s sin, the Lord struck the boy, and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God to spare the boy, but, when the boy died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find a personal prayer of repentance and a public acknowledgment of his transgressions. David’s decision to write his cry for mercy, creating one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible, shows his willingness to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony of repentance and restoration from which others can learn, memorializing his journey from guilt to grace.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not simply about feeling remorse; it was a profound change in his heart and in his life. Through the confession, the acceptance, and the transformation of the heart recorded in Psalm 51, David turned his sin into a lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most significant examples of genuine repentance in the Bible.
