# Chapter 3 - Sin and Redemption

<!-- p1 -->
The Scriptures tell many stories about sin—its causes, its consequences, and how God responds to it. To sin means to fall short of God's standard, or to ‘miss the mark,’ a term from archery that describes a situation where the archer fails to hit the target.

<!-- p2 -->
Both the Old and the New Covenants use different terms for sin, which highlight aspects such as its type, severity, intent, victim, nature, and the required punishment. There are some broad categories that Scripture uses to describe sin:

<!-- p3 -->
- Sin against God, sin against people, sin against oneself

<!-- p4 -->
- Willful sin, unintentional sin

<!-- p5 -->
- A sin committed in a moment, a habitual sin

<!-- p6 -->
- Sins that demand harsh punishments like the death penalty, sins that require financial restitution

<!-- p7 -->
In our modern criminal justice system there are similar distinctions: degrees of crimes, crimes committed with premeditation or in the heat of passion, crimes that demand financial restitution, those punished by imprisonment, or even by the death penalty. An act can be both a crime and a sin, but crime is a human classification, sin is God's.

<!-- p8 -->
The various Greek and Hebrew words used for sin largely overlap with one another. All sin is broadly described under the umbrella term ‘to miss the mark,’ in Greek ***Hamartia***, and in Hebrew ***Chata’***. This umbrella term is used not only for grave sins—adultery, murder—but also for less serious sins such as showing favoritism toward the rich in our church gatherings (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 2:9</span>).

## A sacrifice is necessary for sin

<!-- p64 -->
In the story of Adam and Eve, God killed animals and made garments for them from their skins (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 3:20</span>). This was the first instance of blood being shed to cover the consequences of sin. There are a few other such accounts in the book of Genesis, but in Leviticus God formally established the procedure for sacrifices for sin.

<!-- p65 -->
[See Table 3](../tables/table3.md)

<!-- p66 -->
### **Burnt offering (*Olah*)**

<!-- p67 -->
The burnt offering, that is, ***Olah***, was a common sacrifice for atonement and devotion to God, which often symbolized total surrender. This offering addressed sin in a broad sense, encompassing both known and unknown sins. In this rite, a male animal without blemish- typically a bull, a sheep, a goat, or a bird - was offered. The one bringing the offering would lay hands on the animal's head, signifying identification with the offering, and then the whole animal was burned on the altar. This act of burning the entire offering represented complete surrender to remove sin and align one's life with God. The burnt offering emphasized the gravity of sin and the necessity of complete devotion, showing reverence and repentance by offering something valuable.

<!-- p68 -->
### **Grain offering (*Minchah*)**

<!-- p69 -->
The grain offering, called ***Minchah***, was a sacrifice of thanksgiving and dedication rather than a direct sacrifice for sin. This offering was presented alongside other offerings such as the burnt and peace offerings, and was to express gratitude, worship, and devotion to God. Made by mixing fine flour with oil and frankincense, this grain offering contained no blood. A portion of the grain was burned on the altar as an offering to God, and the rest was given to the priests for food. Though not specifically for sin, the grain offering was an important part of communal worship, symbolically expressing the offerer's devotion and gratitude toward God's provision and grace.

<!-- p70 -->
### **Peace offering (*Shelamim*)**

<!-- p71 -->
The peace offering, that is, ***Shelamim***, was centered on fellowship and symbolized peace with God. This offering was often presented after sin and atonement offerings, to celebrate a restored relationship with God once sin had been addressed. The offerer could bring an unblemished female or male, with fat- such as a cow, sheep, or goat. In the rite, the fat portions- the choicest parts of the animal - were burned on the altar as God's portion, while the remaining meat was shared by the priests and the offerer together in a communal feast. The peace offering was distinctive because it symbolized participation and fellowship, reflecting a sense of wholeness and unity with God. After atonement for sin, this offering allowed the community to celebrate God's acceptance and restored peace.

<!-- p72 -->
### **Sin offering (*Chatat*)**

<!-- p73 -->
The sin offering, that is, ***Chatat***, was specifically designated for unintentional sins or sins committed in ignorance. When an individual or the community realized they had sinned unintentionally, they could offer a sin offering to seek purification and forgiveness. The type of animal offered varied according to the status of the person concerned: a priest or the community would offer a bull, a leader would offer a male goat, and an ordinary person would offer a female goat or a lamb. The priest would sprinkle the blood of the sacrifice before the Lord, the fat portions would be burned on the altar, and if it was a communal sin the rest of the animal was discarded outside the camp. This offering showed how serious even unintentional sins are and provided the means for the people to remain purified and in right relationship with God.

<!-- p74 -->
### **Guilt offering (*Asham*)**

<!-- p75 -->
The guilt offering, that is, ***Asham***, addressed sins related to betrayal, causing harm to others, or the desecration of holy things. Unlike the ordinary burnt offering, the guilt offering required restitution and was often associated with sins where compensation was necessary, such as property offenses or violations against sacred things. To make this offering, the sinner would bring an unblemished ram to be offered. If the sin had harmed another person or any property, the offender had to return the property and also pay an additional 20% as a fine. The guilt offering highlighted the importance of acknowledging one's guilt and taking concrete steps to make things right. It underscored the relational and communal nature of sin, and emphasized that true repentance requires not only asking for God's forgiveness, but also making amends with those affected by the sin.

<!-- p76 -->
## Collective sin

<!-- p77 -->
Besides these specific offerings for individual sins, sacrifices and offerings were also made daily, weekly, and monthly for the nation's collective sins, but the most important among these is **the Day of Atonement.**

<!-- p78 -->
The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) was an annual rite described in Leviticus 16, designed to cleanse the entire Israelite community from the collective sins of the previous year. This unique ceremony was held once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, and it addressed the sins of the nation rather than individual offenses. It cleansed the tabernacle, the priesthood, and the people, symbolically portraying both atonement and purification for the whole community.

<!-- p79 -->
At the center of the Day of Atonement were **two goats**, which represented both the aspect of sacrifice and the removal of sin. To determine the role of each goat, the high priest would cast lots. The first goat, designated “for the Lord,” was offered as a sin offering, and its blood the high priest would take into the Most Holy Place. There, that blood was sprinkled on the mercy seat- the cover of the ark of the covenant - symbolizing atonement for the people's sins and their reconciliation with God. This act of sprinkling was highly significant, because it represented the purification of the holy place and ensured that God's presence would remain with His people.

<!-- p80 -->
The second goat, known as the **scapegoat (Azazel)**, symbolically bore the sins of the community. The high priest would lay his hands on the scapegoat, confessing Israel’s collective sins, thereby transferring those sins onto the animal. Then the goat was sent into the wilderness to die alone, symbolizing the complete removal of sin from the camp. This visible act served as a powerful depiction of atonement and deliverance from sin, showing that God not only forgave, He also removed the sins of His people.

<!-- p81 -->
The Day of Atonement was a profound expression of God’s forgiveness and cleansing, which allowed the nation to delay the consequences of sin and to postpone judgment each year. This atonement did not permanently forgive the nation’s sins, but it delayed God’s judgment.

## We have all sinned

<!-- p88 -->
The books of the Bible focus on the subject of sin in different ways. When Moses wrote the Law in the Torah (the first five books of the Old Testament), he recorded specific punishments for specific sins/offenses. Here I use the word “offense” interchangeably, because the books of the Law were the legal system for the Israelites at that time.

<!-- p89 -->
In the Prophets and the historical books, sin and its consequences are often described in terms of the fate of the entire nation. When the king and the people did not obey God’s commands, the Israelites were oppressed by their neighbors, defeated in war, and in some cases even taken into exile.

<!-- p90 -->
In Proverbs, the author writes as a loving father to his son, warning about the personal risks of sin and a lifestyle against God’s commands. In the Psalms, many of David’s writings relate to the personal and emotional consequences of having sinned.

<!-- p91 -->
In the Gospels, Jesus warns about sin, calls people to personal repentance, and gives the ultimate sacrifice for sin. In Romans, the apostle Paul presents a powerful argument about how God has planned to deal with sin and how we can be free from its grip.

<!-- p92 -->
The reality is that we are all guilty of sin, and we all must pay the price of our offense.

It may be easy for us to justify our actions and say that we have not committed very serious sins such as murder or adultery, and that we are generally moral and good people. This misguided attempt at self-justification ignores the consequences of our actions, the people we have hurt and against whom we have sinned, and the reality of a holy and perfect God. Scripture says that if we say we have not sinned, we are liars, and the truth is not in us!
