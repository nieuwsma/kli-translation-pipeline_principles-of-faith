# Chapter 4 - Jesus-the Messiah

<!-- p1 -->
Based on our study of sin, we have concluded that sin demands a serious price, both for the One against whom sin is committed and who must be restored, and for those who commit sin and must make restitution. The price of sin must be paid. In the Old Covenant, God established a series of sacrifices to be performed by priests, individuals, and communities to address, deal with, and resolve sin.

<!-- p2 -->
Despite all the sacrifices for sin, they were still not sufficient- not because God failed, but because people kept choosing sin. Animal sacrifices could provide limited atonement, but no matter how many animal sacrifices there were, they could not change the sinful nature of man.

<!-- p3 -->
God had a wonderful plan, the <u>Messiah</u> (in Hebrew ***Mashiach,*** pronounced *Mah-shee-ahkh -* **H4899**) who is also known as <u>Christ</u> (in Greek ***Christos,*** pronounced *Khris-tos* - **G5547**) which means “Anointed”; someone set apart for a special role (e.g., king, priest, or savior).

<!-- p4 -->
To understand who Jesus is, it is helpful to look at what people in His time were expecting the Messiah to be like. These expectations determined how they viewed Jesus, and in many cases, why they struggled to recognize Him as the fulfillment of God’s plan.

<!-- p5 -->
## Historical Context

<!-- p6 -->
The Jews had long awaited the coming Messiah. In the time just before Jesus, Jewish beliefs about the Messiah were shaped by Old Testament prophecies, whose interpretations varied and were not universally agreed upon. Most Jews expected the Messiah to be a political and military leader who would deliver Israel from foreign oppression (primarily Roman rule) and restore the Davidic kingdom. Here is an overview of common messianic expectations and how they did (or did not) align with biblical prophecies:

<!-- p7 -->
### Common Jewish Expectations about the Messiah before Jesus

<!-- p8 -->
### **1. A political and military deliverer**

<!-- p9 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would be a king like David, who would restore Israel’s independence by leading a victorious revolt against foreign powers।

<!-- p10 -->
- **Cited Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 9:6-7</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JER" data-chapter="23" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Jeremiah 23:5-6</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ZEC" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Zechariah 9:9-10</span>।

<!-- p11 -->
- **Historical context:** Jews under Roman rule were oppressed and had to pay heavy taxes, which fueled hopes for a Messiah who would topple Rome and establish a powerful earthly kingdom।

<!-- p12 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus is a King, but His kingdom is not of this world (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="36" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 18:36</span>)। He established a spiritual kingdom at His first coming, but He will return as a conquering King to judge and rule in righteousness, as described in the book of Revelation (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 19:11-16</span>)।

<!-- p13 -->
### **2. Restorer of the Davidic kingdom**

<!-- p14 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah would rebuild the Davidic dynasty and bring a golden age of prosperity, justice, and peace।

<!-- p15 -->
- **Cited Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 7:12-16</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="AMO" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Amos 9:11</span>।

<!-- p16 -->
- **Interpretation:** The focus was often on national restoration rather than spiritual renewal।

<!-- p17 -->
- **Truth:** As a descendant of David, Jesus fulfills this promise by establishing an eternal kingdom। Though this kingdom is spiritual in nature now, at His second coming it will become a physical reign (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="32-33" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 1:32-33</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:4-6</span>)।

<!-- p18 -->
### **3. A prophet like Moses**

<!-- p19 -->
- **Belief:** Some Jews believed the Messiah would be a prophet like Moses, who would speak directly with God and speak on His behalf।

<!-- p20 -->
- **Referenced Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="DEU" data-chapter="18" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Deuteronomy 18:15-19</span>.

<!-- p21 -->
- **Expectation:** Although many expected a prophetic figure, this role was often seen not as a central aspect of the Messiah but as an additional aspect।

<!-- p22 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus fulfilled this prophecy as the supreme Prophet by speaking God’s word and directly revealing the Father (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:18</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:10</span>)। His prophetic role goes beyond Moses, because he mediated a new and better covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6-7</span>)।

<!-- p23 -->
### **4. A leader who brings justice**

<!-- p24 -->
- **Belief:** The Messiah will judge the wicked, uphold the righteous, and bring justice to the oppressed।

<!-- p25 -->
- **Referenced Scriptures:** <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3-5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 11:3-5</span>, <span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="72" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalms 72:1-4</span>।

<!-- p26 -->
- **Interpretation:** Justice was expected more in political and social terms than spiritual।

<!-- p27 -->
- **Truth:** Jesus will bring ultimate justice। At his first coming he provided spiritual deliverance and justice through the cross (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="COL" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Colossians 2:13-15</span>)। At his second coming he will execute judgment on the wicked and establish perfect justice forever (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>)।

<!-- p28 -->
### Various beliefs about the Messiah

<!-- p29 -->
Just like today’s Christian world, which is divided into different sects, denominations, or belief structures, the Jews of Jesus’ era were also divided। In Jesus’ time, four major Jewish sects shaped Israel’s religious and political landscape, each with different beliefs and expectations about the Messiah। Understanding some background of these various groups helps provide meaningful context for the study of New Testament Scriptures।

<!-- p30 -->
**Pharisees** were the most influential among the general populace, known for strict adherence to the Law (Torah) and to oral traditions। They believed in the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, and emphasized personal piety and obedience to God’s commandments। Their Messianic expectation focused on one who would uphold the Law, bring justice, and restore Israel both politically and religiously।

<!-- p31 -->
**Sadducees**, by contrast, were a wealthy and politically powerful group who controlled the temple and the priesthood। They rejected oral traditions and accepted only the Torah as authoritative Scripture। The Sadducees denied the resurrection, the afterlife, and the existence of angels, and instead focused on cooperating with Rome to preserve their power and ensure political stability। Therefore, they placed little emphasis on the Messiah, since their primary concern was maintaining the status quo।

<!-- p32 -->
**Essenes** were a separatist group who withdrew from mainstream Jewish society and lived in secluded communities such as Qumran। They were deeply focused on ritual purity, eschatological expectations, and a disciplined lifestyle। The Essenes believed in two Messianic figures: a priestly Messiah who would restore the sanctity of the temple and a royal Messiah who would lead Israel in righteousness। This dual-Messiah concept reflected their hope for spiritual and political renewal। The Essenes are not mentioned directly in Scripture।

<!-- p33 -->
Finally, **Zealots** were militant nationalists who sought to overthrow Roman rule through violent revolt। They rejected Roman authority and were deeply committed to restoring Israel’s sovereignty। Their Messianic expectation was of a warrior-king Messiah who, like David, would lead them in battle against their oppressors and establish a powerful earthly kingdom।

<!-- p34 -->
Although these groups were focused on earthly expectations, Jesus’ mission revealed a far greater plan-a plan that addressed not only political oppression but also humanity’s spiritual slavery to sin।

## The truth about the Messiah

<!-- p36 -->
### Jesus’ anointing

<!-- p37 -->
The views of the Pharisees and the Zealots about the Messiah reflect a pattern repeated in human history-seeking salvation by earthly means while rejecting God’s greater plan। As we see throughout Scripture, people often turn away from God-even though he is always faithful। Even when God remains faithful and receives us, he is often forgotten and rejected, giving rise to cycles of disobedience and decline that plague earthly kingdoms।

<!-- p38 -->
Although many expected a political Messiah, Jesus revealed a far greater purpose for his anointing-a purpose that met spiritual and eternal needs। If Jesus had come as the victorious military leader the Jews hoped and expected, the kingdom would soon have decayed and moved toward collapse because of human disobedience।

<!-- p39 -->
Rather, Jesus established a kingdom that will never be shaken। His mission was rooted not in military might or political dominance, but in divine purpose। As the Anointed One, Jesus explained why he was anointed and how he would fulfill it:

<!-- p54 -->

<!-- p56 -->

<!-- p57 -->
The purpose of Jesus’ ministry and service was both personal and global, both immediate and long-term। His purpose is not single-focused, but is part of God’s divine plan to reconcile humanity to God। After sin entered the world, it brought separation between God and man। God had to drive man out of the garden and from His presence। Throughout the Old Covenant, man could be with God, but only partially। It was exceedingly rare for God to give His Spirit to people, and for the most part people lived in separation from God। He found a people among the Jews, but He desired a permanent, assured, and complete solution to restore His relationship with humanity।

<!-- p58 -->
At the time of Jesus, the presence of God on earth was mostly limited to the innermost part of the temple, the Most Holy Place। Here, behind the thick veil that separated the inner part from the Holy Place, the presence of God dwelt with the Ark of the Covenant। Man was separated from God, and God was separated from His own creation।

<!-- p59 -->
God sent Jesus to repair, rebuild, restore, and reconcile humanity to Himself। (2 Corinthians 5:17-21)

<!-- p60 -->
### The Necessary Work of Jesus

<!-- p61 -->
To reconcile humanity fully to God, Christ’s mission required not only proclamation and healing but also the final act of supreme atonement. Jesus had to become the perfect and complete sacrifice through death on the cross।

<!-- p62 -->
The book of Hebrews explains in great detail throughout its 13 chapters how Jesus became the perfect sacrifice, and that He Himself was both the offering for sin and the High Priest who offered it (Hebrews 3 & 4)। Jesus’ priesthood is not of the Levitical line (after the tribe of Levi), but in the manner of Melchizedek, who is said to have neither beginning nor end, meaning it is not limited by human patterns and customs (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 7:3</span>)। The limitation of the Levitical priesthood is that it relies on the blood of bulls and goats (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 9:12-13</span>) whose sacrifices are not sufficient for a permanent and long-lasting forgiveness of sins। From our study of sin and sacrifice, we know that the consequences of the people’s sins could be postponed for a year on the Day of Atonement (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:3</span>), but that sacrifice had to be continually renewed।

<!-- p63 -->
When Jesus died on the cross (whose details are in Matthew 27, Mark 15, John 19, and Luke 23) He shed His blood and paid for the past, present, and future sins of all humanity। Isaiah 53 reveals Jesus as the Suffering Servant who took upon Himself the full weight of punishment and pain। His once-for-all death became the final, supreme sacrifice। Jesus is the Lamb of God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="29" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:29</span>), without spot or wrinkle (sin); because He was sinless, perfect, and holy, His innocent blood being shed made atonement possible। **No further sacrifice is now necessary or acceptable। The old sacrifices were only temporary-but Jesus’ was complete and final।**

<!-- p64 -->
Through death and resurrection, we have a new and superior covenant (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="8" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 8:6</span>), which is secured by the blood of God’s own Son; Jesus is the Mediator of the covenant, He Himself is the go-between। Through Christ’s sacrifice, we receive salvation, and He is *‘eternal salvation for all who obey Him*’*’s author* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="5" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 5:9</span>)।

<!-- p65 -->
In Greek, the word for salvation is **Sōtēria** (*so-tay-REE-ah - **G4991)**.* Primarily, it means **salvation, deliverance, or preservation**। It encompasses the act of being saved, in a spiritual sense (eternal salvation through Christ) and in physical contexts (deliverance from danger or harm)। The term expresses the idea of safety, wholeness, and restoration।

<!-- p66 -->
**Salvation or Deliverance:**

<!-- p67 -->
- Refers to God’s salvation of humanity from sin and its consequences through Jesus Christ।

<!-- p68 -->
- Used in contexts of eternal salvation (justification, sanctification, and glorification)।

<!-- p69 -->
**Preservation:**

<!-- p70 -->
- Refers to being kept safe, receiving protection, or being spared from danger।

<!-- p71 -->
**Wholeness or Healing:**

<!-- p72 -->
- Encompasses restoration to a state of completeness or health।

<!-- p73 -->
Jesus’ sacrifice paid the price for our forgiveness and opened the way for us to have a relationship with God, preparing the path for us to remain with Him, which depends not on our perfection or our ability to stay away from sin, but only on our obedience।

<!-- p74 -->
When we accept Jesus by faith (to be discussed later), we receive His salvation। This salvation joins us to God, and our name is written in the ***The Lamb’s Book of Life*** (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="REV" data-chapter="20" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Revelation 20:11-15</span>)। The Gospel of John says:

<!-- p75 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p76 -->
> **<u>John 3:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life।
>

<!-- p77 -->
</span>

<!-- p78 -->
To perish means to decay—as with fruit that has spoiled even before being consumed। It is the kind of life that loses the purpose for which it was made। Jesus came to give unceasing, that is, eternal life, and to keep us from perishing। Eternal life is not a life that begins only after death; it begins from the very moment we accept Jesus as our salvation, when we are born again।
