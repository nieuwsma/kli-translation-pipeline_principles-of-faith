# Chapter 12 - Discipleship

<!-- p1 -->
Discipleship means following Jesus, learning from Him, and becoming like Him in the way we live and love. It encompasses a lifelong journey of obedience, transformation, and service, guided by the Holy Spirit and rooted in the Word of God.

<!-- p2 -->
Discipleship is the central command in the Great Commission. Jesus defined this mission as follows:

<!-- p3 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p4 -->
> **<u>Matthew 28:18–20</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And Jesus came to them and said to them, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me.
>
> Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit,
>
> teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you; and behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age." Amen.
>

<!-- p5 -->
</span>

<!-- p6 -->
We, His followers, must "Go and make disciples of all nations." Discipleship is about personal growth and multiplying that growth by teaching others to follow Christ. Discipleship is the means of building the kingdom of God; the teachings of Jesus are the message.

<!-- p7 -->
The call to become a disciple is not merely to attend church or memorize Bible verses—it is an invitation to live like Jesus and to partner with Him to advance the kingdom of God. True discipleship helps believers become spiritually mature and effective for ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:13</span>). It shapes both the heart and the skills needed to serve others and share the gospel. As Paul instructed Timothy in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, discipleship follows a pattern of spiritual multiplication, where trained disciples continually raise up new followers and lead others to Christ.

<!-- p8 -->
Discipleship is both a personal calling and a global mission. On a personal level, it involves growth in faith, cultivating an active spiritual life, and remaining in relationship with others &amp; with God. At the same time, it fuels the church’s mission to reach every nation with the gospel, ensuring that Christ’s message spreads from generation to generation and that lives are continually transformed.

<!-- p9 -->
## Why Is Discipleship Important

<!-- p10 -->
The impact of discipleship goes beyond personal transformation—it has a kingdom impact. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span>, Paul reminded Timothy to entrust what he had learned to faithful people who would be able to teach others also. This intergenerational pattern shows that discipleship is the engine that drives the growth and durability of the church. Discipleship is important because this is the method Jesus chose to spread the gospel and build His church.

<!-- p11 -->
Instead of focusing on large crowds, Jesus deliberately invested in small groups of followers, teaching them, modeling faithfulness, and preparing them to carry forward His mission. This personal, relational approach ensured that His message would multiply as each disciple became capable of making more disciples.

<!-- p12 -->
At its core, discipleship is central to fulfilling the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19–20</span>). Jesus called us not merely to make converts, but to make disciples—followers rooted in the Word of God, empowered by the Holy Spirit, and equipped for ministry. Through discipleship, believers grow in knowledge of Scripture, develop spiritual disciplines, and learn to walk in obedience to Christ. It prepares the church to impact the world, advance the gospel, and reflect the glory of God among all nations.

<!-- p13 -->
### Nature vs. Nurture

<!-- p14 -->
In the study of biology, psychology, and anthropology, we often consider the influence of nature and nurture. A frequently asked question is: Are people more a result of their innate, genetic inheritance? Or are they more a product of their environment and upbringing? The truth is that each person is a blend of nature and nurture. The strong interaction of both elements orchestrates the development of a person’s soul.

<!-- p15 -->
When you received Jesus, the miracle of salvation caused you to be born again from imperishable seed. You received the divine nature! Your spiritual DNA was rewritten! This very divine nature stirs in our hearts the desire to draw near to our Heavenly Father, to know Him, and to experience Him.

<!-- p16 -->
Just as the wonderful, beautiful, innocent nature of a newborn must be shaped by loving parents to develop into a healthy and mature person, so we too must be formed and nurtured.

<!-- p17 -->
Discipleship is the process of spiritual formation that builds on our divine nature and brings us to spiritual maturity. By investing in discipleship, we ensure that the faith is passed down through the generations, leaders are raised up, and the church remains healthy and vibrant both locally and globally.

<!-- p18 -->
### Didactic Model vs. Apprentice Model

<!-- p19 -->
In modern Western education, a student’s primary relationship is with the content. The student’s learning centers on subject matter, materials, books, videos, assignments, and so on. Transformation in education happens through the curriculum. This kind of teaching is called the didactic model.

<!-- p20 -->
The paradigm of the didactic model has not always been the common norm. In Jesus’s era the model and method used was the *apprentice model*. This learning model emphasizes that knowledge is 'absorbed more than it is taught' and is more focused on the relationship between student and teacher than on the student and the curriculum.

<!-- p21 -->
The didactic model gradually became the default method over nearly a millennium—through Europe’s first medieval universities in the 11<sup>th</sup> - 13<sup>th</sup> centuries, the invention of the printing press in the 15<sup>th</sup> century, the Enlightenment of the 17<sup>th</sup> & 18<sup>th</sup> centuries, and the impact of the Industrial Revolution on information and education in the 19<sup>th</sup> & 20<sup>th</sup> centuries.

<!-- p22 -->
The advantage of the didactic model is that it can scale more efficiently than the apprentice model. Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) can have thousands of students at the same time, whereas even a highly skilled master can take at most only a few dozen apprentices concurrently.

<!-- p23 -->
The result of the didactic model is that the student gains proficiency in the material; but the student does not become like the teacher. The teacher is a facilitator for the curriculum. The teacher imparts knowledge.

<!-- p24 -->
In the apprentice model the master imparts not only knowledge but also wisdom. As a result of this model the student becomes so fully like the master that he can reproduce the master’s work and take on his own apprentices.

<!-- p25 -->
The didactic model has use and a place in the kingdom of God; a good example is this book or any other Christian educational material. Jesus, as a rabbi, skilled and trained in the rabbinic method, practiced the apprentice model as the primary model for his disciples.

<!-- p26 -->
Jesus describes the apprentice model this way:

<!-- p27 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="40" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p28 -->
> **<u>Luke 6:40</u>** (NKJV)
>
> A disciple is not above his teacher; but everyone who is perfectly trained will be like his teacher.
>

<!-- p29 -->
</span>

<!-- p30 -->
In the didactic model the student becomes a carrier of knowledge; in the apprentice model, the student becomes like the teacher. Let us study the key words of that passage in the book of Luke.

<!-- p31 -->
[See Table 6](../tables/table6.md)

<!-- p32 -->
In summary, the purpose and goal of the apprentice model used in discipleship are twofold:

<!-- p33 -->
**First, the disciple is fully trained like the teacher.** This does not mean the disciple must imitate everything his teacher does. We have not been commanded to make clones; rather, to form mature men and women who can carry Jesus’s message to the ends of the earth.

<!-- p34 -->
**Second, the disciple now becomes a teacher for others.** The disciple must pass on what he has learned to someone else; otherwise the message of the kingdom stops with them. This does not mean they will come with the same gifts as their teacher, or that they will enter full-time ministry; but it does mean they must reproduce, so that their own training may be considered complete.

<!-- p35 -->
Any living thing that cannot reproduce is barren and its lineage is cut off. An animal that cannot reproduce is not healthy; likewise a congregation that cannot reproduce is also unhealthy.

## Jesus’s model and process of discipleship

<!-- p37 -->
In the Gospels Jesus showed a mature and complete example of discipleship with his chosen disciples. We should broadly use these stages as the basis for our own journey to become and make disciples.

<!-- p38 -->
In the apprentice model there are two roles: master and student. Every master was once a student, and hopefully every student will also become a master. I will use the word *disciple* for the student and the word *disciple-maker* for the master/teacher.

<!-- p39 -->
Every discipleship relationship passes through a progressive process of eight stages. These stages, or steps, are not completely distinct from one another; often the relationship flows between those stages. At each stage there is its own emphasis for the disciple and for the disciple-maker. Later in this chapter we will study the qualities of disciple-makers in depth.

<!-- p40 -->
The eight stages of discipleship are as follows:

<!-- p41 -->
1.  Invitation/Call

<!-- p42 -->
2.  Teaching

<!-- p43 -->
3.  Demonstration

<!-- p44 -->
4.  Participation/Attempt

<!-- p45 -->
5.  Correction

<!-- p46 -->
6.  Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p47 -->
7.  Multiplication

<!-- p48 -->
8.  Remain Connected

<!-- p49 -->
### Invitation/Call

<!-- p50 -->
Discipleship begins with a personal invitation from Jesus to follow Him. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:19</span>, Jesus called His first disciples, saying, *“Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.”* This invitation was both relational and transformational—it required leaving old lifestyles and entering into a new purpose centered on God’s mission. Jesus’ call was not to sit as spectators, but to participate in His ministry and the work of His kingdom.

<!-- p51 -->
Modern disciple-makers should invite others into authentic relationships that inspire spiritual growth and obedience. The invitation to discipleship is deeply personal. Jesus meets people where they are, addressing their specific needs and circumstances, yet He calls them to go deeper—to grow, to serve, and to become like Him. This pattern shows the relational nature of discipleship, where the focus is not merely on knowledge but on a life-transforming relationship with Christ.

<!-- p52 -->
### Teaching

<!-- p53 -->
Teaching is the heart of discipleship. Jesus spent much of His ministry teaching the principles of the kingdom of God, especially in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7). His teachings provided a foundation for living righteously, loving others, and walking in obedience to God. Even today, instruction from God’s Word remains central to discipleship, for Scripture is the primary means for spiritual growth, correction, and training in righteousness (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p54 -->
Disciple-makers must commit to faithfully teaching the Bible and helping others understand it and apply it to their lives. Teaching is more than transmitting knowledge—it requires modeling by living a Christlike life so that disciples can see faith in action. Jesus embodied the truths He taught, showing that effective teaching is both in word and deed. Likewise, disciple-makers should strive to live out what they teach, providing practical examples of obedience and faithfulness.

<!-- p55 -->
### Demonstration

<!-- p56 -->
Jesus did not only teach His disciples—He demonstrated how to live out the truths He proclaimed. He showed, in practical ways, what it means to love, to serve, and to minister. Some examples from the Gospels include Jesus washing the disciples’ feet as an act of humility and service (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="13" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 13:12-15</span>), healing the sick, and feeding the hungry. Through His actions He modeled compassion, faith, and courage, leaving His followers a clear example to imitate.

<!-- p57 -->
In discipleship it is necessary not only to tell, but to show. Modern disciple-makers should be transparent about their faith journeys, including struggles and victories, so that others can see how faith works in real life. Whether praying with those in need, sharing testimonies, or serving the community—discipleship flourishes when it is rooted in practical demonstration. Actions often speak louder than words, making demonstration a powerful tool for training others in the faith.

<!-- p58 -->
### Participation/Attempt

<!-- p59 -->
Jesus did not keep His disciples on the sidelines—He actively involved them in ministry experiences. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-6" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 9:1-6</span> and <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5-15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 10:5-15</span>, Jesus sent the disciples to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons. These practical opportunities allowed the disciples to learn by doing, and to gain confidence and skills under His guidance.

<!-- p60 -->
Modern discipleship should also include practical participation. Disciple-makers should invite disciples to participate in ministry, in ways such as leading prayer groups, sharing testimonies, or serving in outreach programs. When safe spaces for practice are provided, disciples can receive support and encouragement and grow in boldness. Learning is best when put into practice, and participation is the key to turning principles into action.

<!-- p61 -->
### Correction

<!-- p62 -->
Growth often requires correction, and when his disciples misunderstood his teachings or failed in their tasks, Jesus lovingly corrected them. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MRK" data-chapter="9" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-37" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Mark 9:33-37</span> addresses their pride by teaching about servant leadership. In <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="22" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="31-32" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 22:31-32</span>, he prayed for Peter, preparing him to be lifted up from failure and to strengthen others.

<!-- p63 -->
Correction in discipleship should be grace-filled and constructive—rooted in Scripture and focused on restoration—(<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 4:2</span>). It is not about blame, but about helping disciples grow and avoid pitfalls. Disciple-makers should provide accountability, using God’s Word to guide and encourage growth, while also modeling humility by receiving correction themselves.

<!-- p64 -->
### Empowerment/Authorization

<!-- p65 -->
Jesus not only taught and corrected—he empowered his disciples to carry out his own mission. He gave them authority to preach the gospel, heal the sick, and cast out demons (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="18-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:18-20</span>). After the resurrection, he promised the Holy Spirit as the source of power for their ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span>).

<!-- p66 -->
Modern discipleship should also include empowering others to walk in their spiritual gifts and serve effectively. This involves identifying gifts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11-13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Ephesians 4:11-13</span>), encouraging their use, and releasing disciples to serve. Empowerment also emphasizes dependence on the Holy Spirit for strength, courage, and wisdom, ensuring that disciples are spiritually equipped for the work God has called them to do.

<!-- p67 -->
### Multiplication

<!-- p68 -->
The goal of discipleship is not merely personal growth—it is multiplication. Jesus modeled this by training disciples to make more disciples (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). After his resurrection, the apostles continued this multiplying mission, spreading the gospel and planting churches throughout the known world of that time. Paul’s instructions in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 2:2</span> reinforce this principle, urging Timothy to teach others who will in turn teach others.

<!-- p69 -->
Discipleship includes equipping disciples to make other disciples, creating a chain reaction of faith and expanding God’s kingdom. This process requires intentionality—teaching disciples how to mentor, teach, and train, and also sending them with confidence and purpose (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Romans 10:15</span>).

<!-- p70 -->
### Staying Connected

<!-- p71 -->
Discipleship does not end when someone is sent. Jesus modeled ongoing relationship with his disciples, continuing to encourage and guide them even after the resurrection (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="21" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-19" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 21:15-19</span>). The early church also maintained this pattern, gathering regularly for teaching, fellowship, and prayer (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>).

<!-- p72 -->
Modern discipleship should also foster ongoing relationships, providing accountability, encouragement, and support as disciples grow and lead. Maintaining unity in the body of Christ ensures that disciples are never alone, but remain strengthened and encouraged in their faith journey.

<!-- p73 -->
## Who Does Discipleship?

<!-- p74 -->
Everyone in the body of Christ bears the responsibility first to become a disciple, and then to contribute to the process of building discipleship. There is diversity in gifts and functions, so our contributions may look different, but we are all responsible. Ultimately, the people most responsible for the state and health of discipleship in the local church are the leaders (**elders and deacons - <span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 14:23</span>**), but everyone plays their respective roles.

<!-- p75 -->
Discipleship is a living, growing process—like raising a child into maturity. Healthy discipleship gives people strength and life. Just as parents strengthen and nourish a child, making them healthy and mature, so disciple-makers help nourish, strengthen, and mature disciples. The ultimate goal of discipleship is not ‘knowledge transfer,’ but transformation.

<!-- p76 -->
We all must become perfect (mature) and make disciples. Though our gifts are diverse, there are some common traits for all disciple-makers.

<!-- p77 -->
### Characteristics of a Disciple-Maker

<!-- p78 -->
A disciple-maker is one who not only follows Christ but is committed to helping others grow in their faith. This role requires qualities that reflect both spiritual maturity and practical dedication. Disciple-makers must continually rely on God’s power, wisdom, and grace to overcome the challenges that come with leading others in their spiritual journey.

<!-- p79 -->
#### Rooted in Christ

<!-- p80 -->
The foundation of a disciple-maker’s life must be deeply rooted in Christ. Jesus said, *“I am the vine; you are the branches. If you abide in Me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from Me you can do nothing”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="15" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 15:5</span>). Effective disciple-makers know that the ability to grow and to help others comes from staying close to Jesus every day. However, resistance to personal growth and spiritual transformation can hinder this dependence. Disciple-makers must continually cultivate their personal relationship with Jesus through prayer, study of Scripture, and obedience, modeling for those they are discipling what it means to abide in Christ.

<!-- p81 -->
#### Committed to the Mission

<!-- p82 -->
A disciple-maker must remain committed to the mission of making disciples according to the command Jesus gave in the Great Commission (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="28" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="19-20" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 28:19-20</span>). This mission requires intentional focus and priority, acknowledging that making disciples is not an optional task but the central purpose of the church. However, challenges such as time constraints, busyness, and personal distractions can make it difficult to stay focused. Disciple-makers should deliberately schedule time for mentoring, teaching, and relationship-building, viewing disciple-making not as an occasional activity but as a lifestyle (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="19" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 19:10</span>).

<!-- p83 -->
#### Spirit-led

<!-- p84 -->
The work of disciple-making cannot be accomplished by human strength alone; it requires the guidance and power of the Holy Spirit. Jesus promised His disciples that when the Holy Spirit came upon them they would receive power (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 1:8</span}), equipping them to be witnesses and servants of the gospel. Disciple-makers must be sensitive to the Spirit’s leading, relying on His wisdom to navigate complex situations, cultural barriers, and difficult conversations. This dependence on the Holy Spirit enables disciple-makers to act in love, courage, and spiritual discernment, ensuring that their efforts are not self-directed but Spirit-directed.

<!-- p85 -->
#### Humble and Teachable

<!-- p86 -->
Humility is the mark of an effective disciple-maker. As Paul taught, *“Do nothing out of selfish ambition or vain conceit. Rather, in humility value others above yourselves”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 2:3</span>). Disciple-makers acknowledge that they too are lifelong learners who must remain teachable and accountable. They welcome correction and feedback, modeling humility to those they are guiding. Pride can be a major obstacle in the growth process for both disciple-makers and their disciples. Embracing accountability and maintaining a servant’s heart builds trust and creates an environment conducive to growth and transparency.

<!-- p87 -->
#### Patient and Steadfast

<!-- p88 -->
Discipleship is rarely immediate—it is a long-term process that requires patience and perseverance. Paul reminds believers that, *“Let us not grow weary in doing good, for in due season we shall reap if we do not give up”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:9</span>). Growth in faith often includes obstacles, questions, and setbacks, yet disciple-makers are called to walk with others, providing encouragement and support at every step. It is easy to become discouraged when progress seems slow, but disciple-makers are confident that God is at work, even when results are not immediately visible.

<!-- p89 -->
Being a disciple-maker requires a blend of spiritual dependence, commitment, and humility. It is a calling that demands patience and steadfastness while remaining Spirit-led and teachable. Disciple-makers are not expected to be perfect, but they must be faithful-abiding in Christ, investing in others, and trusting that God will bring growth and transformation in His timing. These qualities reflect the love of Christ and help others become mature disciples who can in turn make disciples.

<!-- p90 -->
## Practical Tools for Discipleship

<!-- p91 -->
Effective discipleship requires intentional tools and practices that foster spiritual growth, accountability, and community. These tools help disciples build a strong foundation in faith, grow in intimacy with God, and be equipped to make other disciples. Practical methods such as Bible study, prayer, small groups, and mentoring provide both the structure and support necessary for ongoing growth.

<!-- p92 -->
### Bible Study

<!-- p93 -->
Deep engagement with Scripture is the foundation of discipleship. Jesus emphasized the importance of God’s Word for spiritual growth, declaring: *“Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God”* (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 4:4</span>). Regular Bible study and memorization enable disciples to internalize truth, combat falsehoods, and apply God’s wisdom to daily life.

<!-- p94 -->
Disciples should be encouraged to immerse themselves in Scripture, not merely as an academic exercise, but to know God and transform their hearts (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="119" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="105" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 119:105</span>). Practical approaches such as reading plans, study guides, and group discussions provide opportunities to explore doctrine and discuss its application in community. By developing regular study habits, disciples lay a strong foundation for spiritual maturity and fruitful ministry (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2TI" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16-17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Timothy 3:16-17</span>).

<!-- p95 -->
### Prayer

<!-- p96 -->
Prayer is the lifeline of discipleship, connecting believers to God’s presence, power, and guidance. Jesus taught His disciples how to pray in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="LUK" data-chapter="11" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Luke 11:1-4</span>, emphasizing both dependence on God and spiritual alignment with His will. Through prayer, disciples learn to seek God’s wisdom, intercede for others, and receive strength for service.

<!-- p97 -->
Teaching disciples how to pray—both individually and corporately—equips them to cultivate intimacy with God and discern His leading (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PHP" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Philippians 4:6-7</span>). Incorporating prayer journals, prayer groups, and fasting practices promotes spiritual discipline and empowerment, ensuring that disciples remain Spirit-led and mission-centered.

<!-- p98 -->
### Small Groups and Community

<!-- p99 -->
Discipleship thrives in community. The early church modeled relational discipleship through fellowship, prayer, and breaking bread together (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ACT" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="42-47" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Acts 2:42-47</span>). Small groups and accountability partnerships provide safe spaces for disciples to grow together, share struggles, and celebrate victories.

<!-- p100 -->
The local church serves as the central hub of discipleship relationships, providing opportunities for group Bible studies, prayer meetings, and fellowship events. These gatherings allow disciples to practice love, bear one another’s burdens (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="2" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:2</span>), and encourage one another toward spiritual maturity (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="HEB" data-chapter="10" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="24-25" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Hebrews 10:24–25</span>). Small groups also provide a framework for multiplying leaders, as mature disciples train and send others to lead groups and guide new believers.

<!-- p101 -->
### Mentoring

<!-- p102 -->
Mentoring provides a personal and intentional approach to discipleship. Jesus modeled this by investing deeply in the twelve disciples and walking with them through teaching, correction, and encouragement. Likewise, one-on-one relationships allow disciple-makers to tailor guidance to individual needs, helping disciples grow in faith, character, and leadership.

<!-- p103 -->
Mentors serve as accountability partners, providing support, prayer, and wisdom during times of growth and struggle. Practical tools such as spiritual assessments, goal setting, and regular check-ins help ensure disciples remain focused and motivated. Peer relationships within mentorship structures also foster mutual growth, as both mentor and disciple learn from shared experiences (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PRO" data-chapter="27" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Proverbs 27:17</span>).

<!-- p106 -->

<!-- p107 -->

<!-- p109 -->
