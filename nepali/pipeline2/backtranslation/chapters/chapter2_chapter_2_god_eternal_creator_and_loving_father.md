# Chapter 2 - God-The Eternal Creator and Loving Father

<!-- p1 -->
It is extremely necessary to establish a firm foundation in the knowledge of who God is. By studying this we can understand:

<!-- p2 -->
- His desires, purposes, and works,

<!-- p3 -->
- His nature, character, and attributes,

<!-- p4 -->
- What is said about Him and what He says about Himself.

<!-- p5 -->
Scripture uniquely reveals the attributes of God, with each facet deepening our understanding of the unchanging, eternal God.

<!-- p6 -->
It is challenging, if not impossible, to confine God to a single description, for He is exceedingly vast and multifaceted. We can and will make great efforts to explain, describe, and depict God, but ultimately, as with art and music, He must be experienced to be understood.

<!-- p7 -->
When a person listens to music, they describe it by expressing how it has touched them - how it has resonated within - going beyond mere melody and rhythm. A melody may carry warmth or intensity; the rhythm may throb like a heartbeat, or the crescendos may evoke feelings of excitement or longing. Music can soothe like a gentle wave or electrify like a storm, stirring the listener, steadying their thoughts, or carrying them entirely to another place. This experience is far more than a sequence of sounds; it is the spirit of the music that remains, embodied in how it shapes the listener’s emotions and personal journey.

<!-- p8 -->
Similarly, a viewer of a painting looks beyond colors or brushstrokes, exploring what these elements embody. A landscape may evoke a sense of serene escape, while an abstract work may arouse curiosity or introspection. Certain hues and shades can give warmth, while a composition can create a sense of peace, disorder, or beauty. Painting becomes a living experience, shifting perspective or eliciting such a deep emotional response that words alone cannot contain it. Therefore, both music and art are transformed into more than their physical forms-they become encounters that touch something fundamental, transforming the listener or viewer in deep and personal ways.

## Exploring God through the Old Testament

<!-- p10 -->
### Genesis: God who dwells with His own creation

<!-- p11 -->
A central theme in God’s story is His desire to dwell with His own creation. This is evident throughout the narratives of Genesis. Its fifty chapters and the genealogies from Adam (the first man) to Joseph (the final major figure of the book of Genesis, the last significant leader of God’s people before Moses) cover a period of about ~2,400 years. From these narratives we can see the constancy of God’s goodness despite human wickedness.

<!-- p12 -->
In Genesis, God establishes relationships with certain chosen individuals.

<!-- p13 -->
1.  **Adam and Eve** - The first humans, created by God and placed in the Garden of Eden. God walked and spoke with them, guided them, and established a relationship based on mutual love and obedience. However, this relationship is affected by their disobedience in eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 1-3).

<!-- p14 -->
2.  **Cain** - Although Cain ultimately rebels by killing his brother Abel, God speaks directly to him even before the murder, warning him about the danger of sin and giving guidance. Even after Cain receives punishment, God marks him so that he will not be harmed (Genesis 4).

<!-- p15 -->
3.  **Noah** - God establishes a special relationship with the righteous man Noah during a time of widespread corruption. God commands Noah to build an ark to save his family and the animals from the deluge. Later, God makes a covenant with Noah, promising never again to destroy the earth with a flood and establishing the rainbow as the sign of this covenant (Genesis 6-9).

<!-- p16 -->
4.  **Abraham (Abram)** - Among the most important relationships in Genesis, God calls Abram to leave his homeland and promises to make him the father of a great nation. God makes a covenant with Abram, promising land, offspring, and blessings for all nations through him. Abram’s faith and obedience become central themes, and as part of the covenant his name is changed to Abraham (Genesis 12, 15, 17, 22).

<!-- p17 -->
5.  **Hagar** - Sarah’s Egyptian maidservant, Hagar encounters God in the wilderness after mistreatment. God comforts her, promises a future for her son Ishmael, and instructs her to return to Abraham and Sarah. Hagar addresses God as “El Roi” (“the God who sees me”), acknowledging His care for her (Genesis 16).

<!-- p18 -->
6.  **Isaac** - Isaac, the son of Abraham and Sarah, inherits the covenant promises made to his father. God directly reaffirms these promises to Isaac, including the promise of offspring and blessings. God guides Isaac during a famine, protects him, and establishes the covenantal relationship (Genesis 26).

<!-- p19 -->
7.  **Rebekah** - Although her relationship with God is indirect, God answers Rebekah’s prayer regarding her difficult pregnancy and reveals that her twins, Jacob and Esau, will become two nations with distinct destinies (Genesis 25).

<!-- p20 -->
8.  **Jacob** - God establishes a personal relationship with Jacob, appears to him in dreams, and confirms covenant promises of land, offspring, and blessing. Jacob encounters God many times, including the famous wrestling match where God changes his name to Israel, symbolizing his transformation and the establishment of Israel as a people (Genesis 28, 32, 35).

<!-- p21 -->
9.  **Joseph** - Although Joseph's relationship with God is not based on direct appearances or on covenant as with Abraham or Jacob, God is deeply involved in his life. Through dreams, divine favor, and providence, God guides Joseph's journey from being sold into slavery to becoming a powerful leader in Egypt. Joseph's trust in God's sovereignty enables him to forgive his brothers, because he recognizes that God used his suffering for good (Genesis 37, 39-50).

<!-- p22 -->
Throughout this entire story, God, who desires to dwell with His creation, must be limited to dwelling with a few chosen individuals, and ultimately establishes a people through the covenant with Abraham (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="GEN" data-chapter="17" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Genesis 17:7</span>).

<!-- p23 -->
### Exodus: The God who dwells with His people

<!-- p24 -->
God's desire to dwell with His people is also clearly seen in the second book of the Bible, Exodus. In the book of Exodus, the people of Israel, who for a time lived in mutual peace and harmony in Egypt, are now being oppressed by a Pharaoh who did not know Joseph and feared the Hebrews (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="6-8" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 1:6-8</span>). God's people lived in Egypt for about ~400 years (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="40-41" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 12:40-41</span}), during the latter part of which they lived as slaves (there is no full consensus about how long Israel was in bondage in Egypt).

<!-- p25 -->
In the powerful presence of the bush that burned without being consumed, God reveals Himself to Moses—a murderer who, in exile, was tending his father-in-law's flock in the wilderness, born a Hebrew and raised in Egypt.

<!-- p26 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="14" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p27 -->
> **<u>Exodus 3:14</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM." And He said, "Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, 'I AM' has sent me to you."
>

<!-- p28 -->
</span>

<!-- p29 -->
The phrase **“I AM THAT I AM”** (Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh) is among the deepest declarations of self-revelation in Scripture. When Moses asks for God's name, God answers with this phrase, which can be translated as "I Am Who I Am," "I Will Be Who I Will Be," or simply "I Am." This declaration may seem somewhat puzzling on the surface, but it carries profound meaning. Can you imagine introducing yourself to someone new by saying ‘I am’? When God reveals Himself as ‘I AM THAT I AM’ He is affirming His nature, His eternal presence, and His sovereignty. The meaning and purpose of ‘I am’ are infinite.

<!-- p30 -->
God uses Moses to reveal Himself to Pharaoh:

<!-- p31 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p32 -->
> **<u>Exodus 7:16</u>** (NKJV)
>
> And you shall say to him, 'The Lord God of the Hebrews has sent me to you, saying, "Let My people go, that they may serve Me in the wilderness"; but indeed, until now you have not listened!'
>

<!-- p33 -->
</span>

<!-- p34 -->
Moses tells Pharaoh that God is ‘I AM’ and that God demands the Israelites be freed to worship Him in the wilderness.

<!-- p35 -->
Plagues and calamities occur, but eventually Pharaoh yields and lets the Israelites go.

<!-- p36 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="31" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p37 -->
> **<u>Exodus 12:31</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Then he called for Moses and Aaron by night, and said, "Rise, go out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel. And go, serve the Lord, as you have said."
>

<!-- p38 -->
</span>

<!-- p39 -->
In the wilderness God miraculously delivers His people from the vengeful Egyptian army (Exodus 14), provides a pillar of fire to shield them from the sun and to warm them in the cold desert nights (Exodus 13), gives food from heaven (Exodus 16), and gives water to drink (Exodus 17).

<!-- p40 -->
God brings His people to Mount Sinai, where God commands Moses to consecrate the people and gives them the Law (including the Ten Commandments) and establishes His covenant (the Old Covenant) with them (Exodus 19, 20, 24). This covenant (the Mosaic Covenant) is built upon the Abrahamic Covenant and remains from Moses to Jesus.

<!-- p41 -->
Even though He made a covenant with His people, the relationship appears as that of a loving and jealous God pursuing and dwelling with a very stubborn and ungrateful people (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="32" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="9" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 32:9</span>). Ignoring God’s wondrous interventions, they maintained a rebellious attitude for a long time. God says He will bring them to the Promised Land so they can take possession of it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EXO" data-chapter="33" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Exodus 33:3</span>), but because of fear and disobedience they disregarded the supernatural deliverance God had given, and instead had to wander in the wilderness for 40 years until almost all of that generation died (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="NUM" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="33-34" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Numbers 14:33-34</span>).

<!-- p42 -->
### The Rest of the Old Testament

<!-- p43 -->
Over roughly ~1,400 years from Moses’ death to Christ’s birth, the Israelites experienced a kind of recurring cycle: devotion that brings God’s favor and blessing, then rebellion, God’s judgment, their subsequent repentance, and ultimately God’s deliverance. This pattern is especially evident in the books of **Judges, Kings, and Chronicles**, where the people drift away from God, often worshiping other gods and consequently suffering oppression or exile. In response to their cries, God repeatedly raised up judges, prophets, or leaders to deliver them.

<!-- p44 -->
Throughout these years, though the Israelites repeatedly failed to keep their covenant with Him, God remained faithful, revealing both justice and mercy. He continually preserved and forgave His people, showing His enduring commitment and love.

<!-- p45 -->
As seen in Scripture, God reveals Himself through names that speak of His deeds, relationships, and His nature. Each name represents a particular aspect of His overall character. These names are given as part of the story of how God revealed Himself to His people. For your personal study, a reference table is included in the appendix: *Table 10 - The Names of God in the Old Testament.* Read these names and study the scriptural references. Each reference is a story that vividly portrays how God saved, delivered, and redeemed His people.

<!-- p46 -->
The appendix also includes a set of additional derived attributes that describe His nature, works, and intentions: *Table 11 - The Attributes of God.* These attributes, though somewhat more abstract than the names of God, are no less authentic!

<!-- p47 -->
These names help us understand that God—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (the Trinity)—work together as One. As we continue our study, we will consider the names and attributes of Jesus and the Holy Spirit in greater depth.

<!-- p48 -->
The character and works of God are fully revealed in the unity of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. Scripture teaches that God is one in essence, yet exists as three distinct persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Scripture affirms God’s oneness, saying, “There is no God besides Me” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ISA" data-chapter="45" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Isaiah 45:5</span>). Yet the Godhead is understood as a united unity—three distinct persons who cooperate with one mind and purpose, truly as one. Each person of the Trinity has a unique role: the Father creates, the Son redeems, and the Holy Spirit sanctifies. The word “Trinity” does not appear in the Bible; it is a theological expression used to describe this concept revealed through Scripture.

<!-- p49 -->
The Trinity can also be illustrated by examples from nature that clarify the idea of unity in diversity. Like water, which can be liquid, ice, or vapor yet remains H₂O, each person of the Trinity is fully God but distinct in personhood. Or consider a triangle—a complete figure composed of three sides and three angles; if one side is removed, it is no longer a triangle. Likewise, without all three persons, the nature of God would be incomplete; they all work together in perfect unity.

<!-- p50 -->
- **God the Father**: He is the Creator and Sustainer of all things, often understood as the initiator within the Godhead. His love for humanity is a central theme of the biblical narrative, best exemplified in <span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="3" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="16" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 3:16</span>. In our study we will look in detail at God the Father as the loving Father and righteous Judge who made a way for our salvation.

<!-- p51 -->
- **God the Son**: Jesus, coexistent and coeternal with the Father, was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the virgin Mary. He lived a sinless life, died for the salvation of humanity, and rose again, conquering sin and death. He now sits at the right hand of the Father, interceding on our behalf (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1-3" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 1:1-3</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 1:23</span>; <span class="x-scripture" data-book="1TI" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="5" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">1 Timothy 2:5</span>). In our study we will view Jesus as the perfect atoning sacrifice, the risen Lord, and the head of the Church.

<!-- p52 -->
- **God the Holy Spirit**: The Holy Spirit, coexistent and coequal with the Father and the Son, is sent by the Father through the Son to convict the world of sin and to prepare believers as the Bride of Christ. He empowers, comforts, and sanctifies those who are in Christ (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JHN" data-chapter="14" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="26" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">John 14:26</span>; 15:26; 16:8). In our study we will view the Holy Spirit as the Comforter, given by God to equip us, empower us, sanctify us, and dwell with us.

<!-- p53 -->
Through these distinct persons, the triune nature of God is revealed as perfect unity in purpose, will, and love; in the divine mission of creation, redemption, and sanctification, each person plays a vital role. The Trinity invites believers to experience God fully—as the loving Father, the saving Redeemer, and the transforming Spirit.

<!-- p54 -->
## Conclusion

<!-- p55 -->
We can be known in distinctive ways by our names: husband, father, son, brother, friend. Each of these declares who we are in the context of our relationships with others. We can also be known by our attributes: faithful, patient, compassionate, wise, humorous. Likewise, we can know God by His attributes and names—He is our healer, provider, shepherd, and savior. But tags and words are only small parts of us; for others to truly know us, they must experience us. All the more so with God. To know Him truly and fully, we must experience Him.

<!-- p56 -->
The names of God reveal His character, but personal encounters deepen our understanding of what these names mean. For example, “God my healer” takes on a richer and deeper meaning when we understand how God delivers from the depths of illness, despair, or helplessness. Knowing Him as our provider is transformed when we feel real need and find Him faithful.

<!-- p57 -->
The Scriptures attest to such encounters, showing God’s faithfulness to His people across times and circumstances, confirming that His names and attributes are not merely titles but realities He brings to life—experienced in individual lives. Through this interplay of personal experience and biblical narrative, we come to know God—not as a distant description, but as an active, present relationship in every stage of our lives.
