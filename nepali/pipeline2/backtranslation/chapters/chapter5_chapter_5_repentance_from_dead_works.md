# Chapter 5 - Repentance from Dead Works

<!-- p1 -->
In Christianity we often consider repentance as merely foundational for the beginning of our faith in Jesus. The heart of repentance is much deeper than turning from sin and receiving salvation. Repentance from dead works means to live with our hearts turned away from things that harm us or hinder our growth- and turned toward the things of God.

<!-- p2 -->
The author of the book of Hebrews places ‘repentance from dead works’ first in the list of elementary teachings that come after the foundational principles of Christ (chapter 4).

### Dead Works

<!-- p9 -->
Anyone who has ever been employed knows what work is. Work is the set of actions you do to receive a wage and make a living (employment). Employment is an agreement to exchange your talent and effort for something of value that helps sustain you. You work because you get something from it.

<!-- p10 -->
Our work is important; it can give a sense of purpose, and often a sense of identity. It is very common to be asked, ‘What do you do for a living?’ and the answer often begins with an identity statement, ‘I am ...’. Work can be enjoyable and purposeful, but it is also very likely to be physically or mentally demanding, hard, challenging, and stressful. If your work is none of these, you may find it extremely menial and tedious.

<!-- p11 -->
If you work a 40-hour week, and sleep 8 hours a night, you spend 36% of your waking hours at work, and this does not include the time spent getting ready for work, commuting, managing the household, and the other necessary tasks required to keep life running properly.

<!-- p12 -->
Why do we do all this work? The reasons may vary, but by definition it is because we get something of value from it.

<!-- p13 -->
If the pay stopped, would you continue doing the same job? For almost all of us the easy and painfully clear answer is, emphatically, no!

<!-- p14 -->
If a job were killing you, would you continue to work at it? In my opinion, that would depend on how quickly it was happening.

<!-- p15 -->
The context of the combination of ***nekros*** and ***erga*** indicates labor or work that is dead, that has no vitality, that cannot reproduce! The author of the book of Hebrews is warning us about such dead labor.

<!-- p16 -->
In the study of biology, there are certain criteria used to determine whether a living organism is alive (for our purposes this is only a partial list):

<!-- p17 -->
1.  **Metabolism**: Living things transform energy through metabolic processes to grow, develop, and sustain themselves.

<!-- p18 -->
2.  **Growth and Development**: Life shows increase and progressive change over time, with a specific life cycle and developmental changes.

<!-- p19 -->
3.  **Reproduction**: Living things have the capacity to create offspring or new units, that is, to reproduce.

<!-- p20 -->
4.  **Response to stimuli**: Life responds to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or sound) and can adjust to changes in the environment.

<!-- p21 -->
5.  **Homeostasis**: Living organisms maintain internal stability, such as temperature regulation or pH balance.

<!-- p22 -->
6.  **Adaptation**: Over time, living organisms can adapt to their environment through genetic changes, which helps them survive under different conditions.

<!-- p23 -->
If we apply this criterion of ‘life’ to our deeds with some creative freedom, then ‘dead works’ are those that:

<!-- p24 -->
1.  Do not allow personal growth or development.

<!-- p25 -->
2.  Do not reproduce, cannot produce positive or desirable results.

<!-- p26 -->
3.  Do not respond to external stimuli, to the light of God’s presence, or to the sound of His voice.

<!-- p27 -->
4.  Do not maintain internal stability, become unregulated, are not self-controlled.

<!-- p28 -->
5.  Do not adapt (are excessively rigid) and no longer help us to survive.

<!-- p29 -->
Dead works are not God’s desire for us. God wants to give us life!

<!-- p30 -->

</span>

<!-- p33 -->
The contrast between dead and alive is binary. Dying, however, is gradual. Our dead works may not be completely dead in every category right now, but they become so! Dead works are like poisons; some have a much greater effect.

<!-- p34 -->
Poison harms the body by preventing it from functioning normally. Some poisons have minor effects, like a headache. Others affect the way we think, the way we see, or even whether we can bear children (live fruitfully). Dead works are the same. They may cause only a headache, or they may lead to death.

<!-- p35 -->
Dead works are any actions that, upon review and reflection, do not bring about the abundant life Jesus promised. Maintaining a relationship outside of marriage is an extremely potent poison; it is likely to have a swift, total, and negative impact. Being an unnecessarily stubborn and bitter person gradually poisons an individual, which ultimately pushes everyone away from them.

<!-- p36 -->
To be freed from dead works, we must repent!

<!-- p37 -->
### Repentance

<!-- p38 -->
To ‘repent from dead works’ means to turn to God and to give up anything that is harming our walk with Him - beginning with a change of heart and mind.

<!-- p39 -->
Extending the metaphor of poison for dead works, if you found out that your favorite snack was full of toxic heavy metals that, if eaten, would bring about a fatal condition, what would you do? We would, without hesitation, keep it away from ourselves and never enjoy it again. Because we would understand that consuming it means our untimely, and perhaps painful, death.

<!-- p40 -->
We must repent! We must distance ourselves from those things in this life that are poisoning us and pushing us toward both natural and spiritual death.

<!-- p41 -->
To understand this fully, we need to take a slightly deeper look at the Greek language.

<!-- p42 -->
The term used in Hebrews is ‘repentance.’ It is compared with the term ‘to repent,’ an example of which is found in

<!-- p43 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="4" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p44 -->
> **<u>Matthew 4:17</u>** (NKJV)
>
> From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
>

<!-- p45 -->
</span>

<!-- p46 -->
To repent (***Metanoeō***, pronounced *meh-tah-NO-eh-oh* - **G3340**) is a verb; it is an action or event; it means to change one’s mind or purpose; to turn away from sin and turn toward God.

<!-- p47 -->
Repentance (***Metanoia***, pronounced *meh-TAH-noi-ah* - **G3341**) is a noun; it is the state of having repented; it means the mind has been changed; the state of having turned away from sin and turned toward God.

<!-- p48 -->
When God says in Hebrews ‘repentance from dead works’ He is telling us to remain in the state of having repented! We must live in the continual state of having ‘turned away’ from dead works.

<!-- p49 -->
How many times should we repent? A trick question! We must keep changing our minds until we no longer do dead works. To repent is not to ask forgiveness, or to apologize, although that may be involved. To repent is to turn from the former way of thinking.

### Systems of Change

<!-- p60 -->
There is a cognitive trap in changing our mind. If we don’t see any need to change our mind, why would we ever open our eyes to seek change? Again, returning to the analogy of poison, if you can’t even realize that the poison is killing you, why would you stop consuming it?

<!-- p61 -->
Some people believe that a person must hit ‘rock-bottom’, only then will they look at their life and decide to change. Experience and psychology tell us—people are more complex. In the study of psychology there are many theories related to behavior change; among them, one highly respected theory is the **Transtheoretical Model of Change** developed by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente in the late 1970s **[1].**

<!-- p62 -->
For change, people may pass through a cycle of six stages:

<!-- p63 -->
1.  Precontemplation: No intention to change.

<!-- p64 -->
2.  Contemplation: There is an intention to change in the near future.

<!-- p65 -->
3.  Preparation: Some steps have been taken to change.

<!-- p66 -->
4.  Action: The behavior has been changed for some time.

<!-- p67 -->
5.  Maintenance: The behavior has been changed and is being sustained.

<!-- p68 -->
6.  Termination: There is no desire to return to the previous behavior.

<!-- p69 -->
There is no fixed duration for how long any stage takes. On some issues in life, many people never move through these stages in any meaningful way. In other cases, we can move from stage 1 to 6 quite quickly.

<!-- p70 -->
### Example - Emotional Regulation

<!-- p71 -->
**Scenario:**, a father who experiences a sudden deep realization after yelling at his own child.

<!-- p72 -->
1.  **Precontemplation**: He sees himself as a *firm* yet *loving* parent. He believes raising his voice is necessary. He is not thinking about making a change. In fact, he may even justify it- “My father raised me that way.”

<!-- p73 -->
2.  **Contemplation**: One day, after a long day at work, he suddenly loses control and yells at his little daughter more harshly, louder, and with greater anger than before. She is frightened. Her lip quivers. She walks away silently. In that very moment… something **changes**.

<!-- p74 -->
3.  **Preparation**: He sits alone in the kitchen, stunned. The image of her hurt face loops in his mind again and again. His stomach turns. He tells himself, *“I don’t want to be this kind of person.”* He knows he has to do something different-*now*.

<!-- p75 -->
4.  **Action**: He goes to her room, kneels, apologizes from the heart, and says, *“Daddy was wrong. You shouldn’t have had to endure that.”* She hugs him. Motivated to make the change last, he prays and speaks to a friend for support.

<!-- p76 -->
5.  **Maintenance**: The next day he starts reading parenting books with the aim of working on developing positive emotional self-regulation.

<!-- p77 -->
6.  **Termination**: A few months pass. Yelling is no longer part of the pattern. He isn’t perfect, but something has truly changed. He now has no desire to return to the old behavior.

### Helping Others Change

<!-- p79 -->
Much of our discussion has focused on the act and need of repentance from the perspective of the person who needs to change. But we will encounter people who very much need to change yet are stuck in **Precontemplation**/**Contemplation** - blind to their very need to change. We will also likely meet people who are eager to change but are unsure what to do (**Preparation**).

<!-- p80 -->
When Jesus preached to the crowds, He often encountered people who did not even know that change was necessary. We cannot forcibly change someone’s heart or repent in their place. That is not our place/role, nor is that Jesus’ example.

<!-- p81 -->

Truth is like a light that blinds; when we receive too much of it at once, we are forced to close our eyes—it is very painful. If you are dealing with someone whose heart is closed, I advise you to pray and seek God’s counsel on how to proceed. From experience and from Jesus’ example, sometimes the gradual experience of truth filled with grace begins to open the eyes of the blind. Remember, bringing conviction and light is the work of the Holy Spirit.

<!-- p85 -->
When they begin to experience the transformative process of repentance, it produces good fruit in their lives. If they have been made sick by poison, having their eyes opened, stopping the poison, and taking the antidote—these things begin to heal them.

### Godly sorrow and the goodness of God

<!-- p92 -->
The idea that change happens only after reaching absolute rock bottom is more myth than reality, but God can use sorrow to influence our hearts. God uses sorrow to bring us to repentance. He convicts us about our actions and about the state of our lives and hearts, so that our eyes may be opened and we become aware of our complete and total need for Him.

<!-- p93 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2CO" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p94 -->
> **<u>2 Corinthians 7:10</u>** (NKJV)
>
> For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death.
>

<!-- p95 -->
</span>

<!-- p96 -->
Godly sorrow, or <u>an experience of grief or pain that leads toward a *metanoia*</u>, is not the same as the world’s sorrow. The world’s sorrow is also a similar experience of grief or pain, but the world does not give hope; it offers only relief and the promise of death.

<!-- p97 -->
Sorrow is also different from suffering and bereavement.

<!-- p98 -->
Suffering is enduring physical, emotional, mental, or spiritual pain—especially chronic or acute.

<!-- p99 -->
Bereavement is a multidimensional emotional response to meaningful personal loss, particularly relational or existential.

<!-- p100 -->
Suffering, pain, and sorrow are closely related concepts. In the human condition we will experience all three, we can learn and grow from all three, but God does not bring death and destruction into our lives in order to teach us.

<!-- p101 -->
The Scriptures say that every good and perfect gift is from above, from God (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="JAS" data-chapter="1" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">James 1:17</span>). He is a good Father who knows how to give good gifts to His children (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="MAT" data-chapter="7" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="11" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Matthew 7:11</span>). Godly sorrow brings discomfort, but His discipline is just and loving. Just as a good father would not deliberately break his child’s hand “to teach a lesson,” so likewise God does not impose sickness or disease on His children to teach us.

<!-- p102 -->
### Example of Worldly Sorrow versus Godly Sorrow

<!-- p103 -->
A three-year-old boy was playing in the yard with his two-year-old sister. He was playing with a stick, and he deliberately hit her, causing her to cry and leaving a red mark.

<!-- p104 -->
Hearing the commotion, their father came to investigate, and after finding out what his son had done, he took the stick, broke it in half, and threw it away. He sent his son inside to think about what he had done and comforted his daughter.

<!-- p105 -->
When the father came inside to deal with his son, he found him crying. When asked why he was crying, the boy said, “Because you broke my stick.”

<!-- p106 -->
The father's reply was, “I am saddened that you are more concerned about your stick than about your sister whom you hurt,” and he sent him back to his room for further self-reflection.

<!-- p107 -->
A few minutes later his son came out and apologized. He said that he was still sad because his dad had broken his stick, and because he had hit his sister.

<!-- p108 -->
**Progress!**

<!-- p109 -->
In this example the boy experienced both kinds of sorrow: worldly sorrow for his lost stick, and ultimately godly sorrow for his sister whom he had hurt.

<!-- p110 -->
Godly sorrow and the goodness of God are linked with one another. God is a good Father who lets us experience the consequences of our actions, yet even as we go through those consequences He stands with us.

<!-- p111 -->
God’s steadfast goodness, being ready to deliver and restore us when we come to Him, is a positive factor in our journey of repentance. His goodness does not mean that we should continue in sin, but that even in our sin He makes a way for us to be delivered and to experience His salvation.

<!-- p112 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="ROM" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="4" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">



> **<u>Romans 2:4</u>** (NKJV)
>
> Or do you despise the riches of His goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?
>



</span>

<!-- p113 -->
Even with God’s goodness, mercy, and grace, some people do not repent and do not change; they become stuck in a cycle of despair and pain. In stark contrast, for some people even a simple example of sin and its consequences in someone else’s life is enough to change their thinking.

<!-- p114 -->
Human beings have a great capacity for change; it is rooted in our very nature. Humans are highly adaptable; we can learn and grow, and we can find better ways of doing things. However, having the capacity to change is not the same as having the desire or motivation to change.

<!-- p115 -->
I do not know why some people get stuck while others are so ready to change; but I can say that the entire process of change is truly by the grace of God.

<!-- p116 -->
<span class="x-scripture" data-book="EPH" data-chapter="2" data-lang="en" data-type="quote" data-verses="8-10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">

<!-- p119 -->
The process of repentance begins even before we accept Jesus as our personal Lord and Savior, but a lifestyle of repentance should be maintained throughout our entire Christian journey. We must cast off dead works and, turning away from them, shift our thinking toward God’s truth.

<!-- p120 -->
Repentance can be an entirely natural process, but repentance is also the work of the Spirit, who calls us, opens our eyes to the truth, changes our hearts, and calls us to be transformed. <span class="x-scripture" data-book="GAL" data-chapter="6" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="1" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Galatians 6:1</span> reminds us that some people are ‘stuck’ in transgression (sin) and need help to get out from there! That help comes in the form of mature men and women who help restore us in a spirit of gentleness.

<!-- p121 -->
The Holy Spirit is the greatest factor in which humanity can engage for change, and this is supported by both the Scriptures and research. “Research conducted by Lyons et al. (2010) found that among clients who experienced a spiritual awakening during substance abuse treatment and recovery, 82% were completely abstinent at a 1-year follow-up, which was higher compared to 55% of clients who had not experienced a spiritual awakening.” \[2\] Although people can initiate and facilitate change, we can do much more when we walk with the Spirit of God.

In our upcoming studies on the Holy Spirit and sanctification, we will look more deeply at how the Spirit calls and transforms people’s hearts. Now let’s look at one such example—the example of King David and his repentance.

### Example of David’s repentance

<!-- p124 -->
Perhaps there is no richer example of repentance than the story of King David’s sin found in 2 Samuel 11–12. The story begins when King David stayed in Jerusalem instead of going to war, where he saw Bathsheba bathing. Even though he knew she was the wife of Uriah—who was one of his loyal soldiers—David sent for her and adultery took place between them, as a result of which Bathsheba became pregnant.

<!-- p125 -->
To hide his actions, David summoned Uriah from the battlefield, hoping that he would spend time at home with Bathsheba and it would appear that he was the father of the child. But because of his loyalty to his fellow soldiers, Uriah refused to go home and sleep with Bathsheba. Then David arranged for Uriah to be sent to the front lines, where he was ultimately killed in battle. After Uriah’s death, David quickly married Bathsheba to conceal the pregnancy.

<!-- p126 -->
The Lord was displeased with David’s actions, and He sent the prophet Nathan to confront him. When the prophet confronted him, David immediately admitted, “*I have sinned against the Lord*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="13" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:13</span>). David did not try to justify his actions or make excuses. This confession was the first step of his repentance, where he directly acknowledged his wrongdoing before God.

<!-- p127 -->
In Psalm 51, David’s words, “*For I know my transgressions, and my sin is always before me,*” show his full ownership of his actions. He expresses grief not only for the sin, but also for how it grieved God, saying, “*Against You, You only, have I sinned.*” David displayed a “*broken and contrite heart,*” which is an essential element of true repentance (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="17" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:17</span>). This sorrow was not superficial; he deeply mourned the sins he had committed and the pain they had caused. Such deep repentance signaled that he sought not only forgiveness, but a changed heart.

<!-- p128 -->
David asked God, “*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="10" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:10</span>). He sought inner transformation that would keep him from repeating the same mistakes. Instead of merely asking for forgiveness, David pleaded with God to purify him, saying, “*Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="7" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:7</span>). He understood that only God could purify his heart and restore his joy.

<!-- p129 -->
David’s desire to be made new spiritually reflects his genuine commitment to walk in God’s ways from now on. His plea for a “*willing spirit*” (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="PSA" data-chapter="51" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="12" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">Psalm 51:12</span>) shows his hope to remain aligned with God in every aspect of life.

<!-- p130 -->
Although God forgave David’s sin, the Lord struck the child and he became very ill. David fasted, prayed, and pleaded with God to save the child, but when the child died, David accepted it (<span class="x-scripture" data-book="2SA" data-chapter="12" data-lang="en" data-type="ref" data-verses="15-23" data-version="NKJV" dir="ltr" style="text-align: left;">2 Samuel 12:15-23</span>).

<!-- p131 -->
In Psalm 51 we find his personal prayers of repentance and his public confession of his transgressions. David’s decision to write his plea for mercy—which gave birth to one of the most enduring psalms in the Bible—shows his willingness to be transparent about his sin and God’s forgiveness. This psalm became a testimony of repentance and restoration as a teaching for others, making his journey from guilt to grace memorable.

<!-- p132 -->
David’s journey of repentance was not merely feeling sorry; it was a deep change in his heart and life. Through the confession, acknowledgment, and transformation of heart recorded in Psalm 51, David made his sins a memorable lesson about God’s mercy, repentance, and the possibility of restoration, making his story one of the most important examples of true repentance in the Bible.
